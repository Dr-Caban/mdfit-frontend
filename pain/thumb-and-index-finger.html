Pain at the thumb and index finger: What's Causing It

★★★★★ 5.0 · 89 Google reviews

Pain in the thumb and index finger often feels like an ache or tingling along the thumb side of the hand, in the thumb web, or at the base of the thumb, and it is easily mistaken for carpal tunnel or arthritis. People notice it after hours of texting, gripping a paddle or club, twisting jar lids, or pruning. The scalenes in the neck, which lift the upper ribs and sit over the nerves to the arm, commonly refer pain and tingling to exactly these two fingers. The brachioradialis and supinator near the outer elbow, which turn the palm up and lift the hand, add ache into the thumb web.

Is it this, or something next to it?

A physician should first rule out carpal tunnel syndrome, thumb-base arthritis, De Quervain's tenosynovitis and a neck nerve problem, especially with constant numbness, weakness or wasting of the thumb muscles. Muscle-referred pain here comes and goes with neck or elbow position and is reproduced by pressing the side of the neck or outer forearm. Back-of-wrist pain is central on top, palm-side pain is in the heel of the hand, and ring and little finger pain is on the far edge.

Typical story: a Sarasota resident spends a season of long days on a phone, thumbs working constantly, then takes up pickleball with a tight grip. An ache and occasional tingling settle along the thumb and index finger, worse at the end of long days and when the neck is stiff. Grip strength is normal, and pressing the side of the neck or outer forearm brings on the familiar tingle.

Muscles that commonly refer pain here

Ranked the way the MD Fit pain map ranks them, most common first:

  1. Scalenes. Chest, front and back of the upper arm, inner shoulder blade and down the forearm to the thumb and index finger.
  2. Brachioradialis. Outside of the elbow and the web of the thumb. Worse with: gripping, lifting or typing.
  3. Supinator. Outer elbow and the thumb web; common in tennis elbow that won't settle. Worse with: gripping, lifting or typing.
  4. Subclavius. Under the collarbone and down the front of the arm and forearm into the thumb, index and middle fingers.
  5. Wrist extensors. Outside of the elbow (tennis elbow) and the back of the wrist and hand on the thumb side.

What people with this pain describe

Taken from Dr. Jared's notes on the brachioradialis and the supinator, the two most common sources here:

  • Aching pain on the top and outside of the forearm, near the elbow.
  • Pain that can travel down the forearm to the web of the thumb.
  • A feeling of weakness when gripping or lifting objects.
  • Sharp pain when turning a doorknob, using a screwdriver, or typing.
  • A deep, aching pain at the outer elbow, often mistaken for 'tennis elbow'.
  • Pain that may radiate down the back of the forearm to the web of the thumb.
  • Weakness or sharp pain when trying to turn your palm up against resistance.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the top muscles are involved:

  • Does your pain feel worse when you lift something with your palm facing sideways, like carrying a grocery bag?
  • Do you feel an ache on the outside of your elbow that seems to travel down toward your thumb?
  • Is it painful to shake hands firmly or turn a key in a lock?
  • Do you feel a deep ache on the outside of your elbow, even when you're not gripping anything?
  • Is it painful to turn a doorknob or use a screwdriver, specifically the motion of turning your palm upwards?

How it usually starts

  • Repetitive gripping activities like hammering, using tools, or extensive gardening.
  • Carrying heavy objects with straight arms, such as suitcases or shopping bags.
  • Sports involving forceful wrist and elbow movements like tennis, rock climbing, or rowing.
  • Typing for long hours with poor wrist posture.
  • Repetitive turning motions, such as using tools or playing racquet sports with a lot of spin.
  • Carrying heavy items with bent elbows and palms up, like a waiter's tray.

What it gets mistaken for

Lateral epicondylitis ('Tennis Elbow'); De Quervain's tenosynovitis (thumb tendon pain); Radial nerve entrapment; Radial tunnel syndrome (nerve compression). An evaluation separates muscle pain from these, and anything that needs a physician is referred on.

What makes it worse, and what that tells you

  • Gripping, lifting or typing. Points toward the brachioradialis, supinator, subclavius.

Warning signs: get medical care, not a stretch

  • Numbness or weakness in the hand that is spreading or constant, or dropping things: see a physician for nerve testing.
  • A cold, pale or blue hand, or sudden severe pain: emergency care.
  • Hand pain with chest pressure, shortness of breath or sweating can be a heart attack: call 911.
  • Swelling, redness and fever in a joint: urgent care.

Frequently asked questions

Why does it hurt at the thumb and index finger if nothing is wrong there?

Muscles refer pain: a tight band in one muscle sends a predictable pattern of pain somewhere else. For pain at the thumb and index finger, the usual sources are the scalenes, brachioradialis, supinator.

How long does brachioradialis pain usually take to settle?

With consistent adherence to the protocol, most people feel a significant reduction in pain within 2-4 weeks. Full recovery and strength may take 6-8 weeks.

What makes it more likely to be the brachioradialis?

It is worse with gripping, lifting or typing, and pressing into the muscle reproduces the pain you know. Outside of the elbow and the web of the thumb.

When should I book instead of working on it myself?

Book an evaluation if your pain is severe, doesn't improve after 2 weeks of self-care, or if you experience significant numbness or weakness.
