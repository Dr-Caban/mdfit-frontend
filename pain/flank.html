Pain at the flank or side of the low back: What's Causing It

★★★★★ 5.0 · 89 Google reviews

Pain in the flank or side of the low back often feels like a deep, dull ache between the lowest rib and the top of the pelvis, sharp when rolling over in bed, standing up, coughing or sneezing, and it can bend people slightly to one side. It is noticed after carrying a heavy load on one side, a long day gardening, or a round of golf. The quadratus lumborum, the deep muscle from the lowest rib to the pelvis that side-bends the trunk and hikes the hip, is the main source. The iliocostalis, the outer band of the back extensors, adds a vertical line of ache along the side.

Is it this, or something next to it?

A physician should first rule out kidney problems and other emergencies: flank pain with fever, painful or bloody urination, nausea, a pulsing mass, or pain that comes in intense waves needs urgent care. Muscle flank pain is tender deep between the rib and pelvis and worse side-bending away. Beside-the-spine pain is closer to the midline, SI pain is lower over the back of the pelvis, and tailbone pain is at the base of the spine.

Typical story: a Sarasota golfer plays several rounds in a week and carries a bag on one shoulder, then spends a day hauling mulch bags on the same hip. A deep ache settles into the flank between the ribs and pelvis that stabs when rolling out of bed or coughing. Urination is normal and there is no fever, and pressing deep into the side finds the tender muscle.

Muscles that commonly refer pain here

Ranked the way the MD Fit pain map ranks them, most common first:

  1. Quadratus lumborum. Low back and flank, over the SI joint and buttock, and the point of the hip. Hurts rolling in bed, standing up or coughing. Worse with: standing or walking, turning your head or body, deep breaths or coughing, sitting.
  2. Iliocostalis. Low back and buttock from the lumbar part; the lower shoulder blade and even the belly from the thoracic part. Worse with: standing or walking, sitting, bending or lifting.

What people with this pain describe

Taken from Dr. Jared's notes on the quadratus lumborum and the iliocostalis, the two most common sources here:

  • A deep, nagging ache in the low back, often worse on one side.
  • Sharp, stabbing pain when trying to stand up straight, roll over in bed, or cough.
  • Pain that refers over the top of the buttock (SI joint area) and sometimes to the front of the hip.
  • A feeling of being 'stuck' or unable to stand up straight after sitting for a while.
  • Low-back ache that spreads into the buttock (lumbar part).
  • Ache at the lower shoulder blade, sometimes wrapping toward the belly (thoracic part).
  • Stiffness getting up after sitting.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the top muscles are involved:

  • Do you get a sharp, 'catching' pain in your low back when you try to roll over in bed or get out of a car?
  • Does coughing, sneezing, or taking a deep breath send a sharp pain into your low back or flank?
  • Is it difficult to stand up completely straight after you've been sitting for a while?
  • Is the tender spot a few finger-widths out from the spine, not right beside it?
  • Does the low-back ache spread down into the buttock?

How it usually starts

  • Sitting for long periods, especially with poor posture or in a slumped position.
  • Lifting a heavy object, especially with a twisting motion.
  • A fall or direct blow to the low back, hip, or buttock.
  • Standing or walking on uneven surfaces for extended periods.
  • Long sitting followed by sudden bending or lifting.
  • Lifting and twisting at the same time.

What it gets mistaken for

Lumbar disc herniation; Sacroiliac (SI) joint dysfunction; Kidney pain or kidney stone (check for fever, nausea, or changes in urination); Quadratus lumborum trigger points; Lumbar facet irritation. An evaluation separates muscle pain from these, and anything that needs a physician is referred on.

What makes it worse, and what that tells you

  • Standing or walking. Points toward the quadratus lumborum, iliocostalis.
  • Turning your head or body. Points toward the quadratus lumborum.
  • Deep breaths or coughing. Points toward the quadratus lumborum.
  • Sitting. Points toward the quadratus lumborum, iliocostalis.
  • Bending or lifting. Points toward the iliocostalis.

Warning signs: get medical care, not a stretch

  • Loss of bladder or bowel control, or numbness in the groin or inner thighs: call 911.
  • Leg weakness that is getting worse, or numbness spreading down both legs: emergency care.
  • Back pain with fever, chills or unexplained weight loss, or that is constant and not eased by any position: see a physician.
  • Back pain after a fall or crash, or if you have osteoporosis or take steroids: get checked for a fracture.

Frequently asked questions

Why does it hurt at the flank or side of the low back if nothing is wrong there?

Muscles refer pain: a tight band in one muscle sends a predictable pattern of pain somewhere else. For pain at the flank or side of the low back, the usual sources are the quadratus lumborum, iliocostalis.

How long does quadratus lumborum pain usually take to settle?

Acute QL pain can often be calmed within a few days. Consistent mobility and strengthening can lead to significant improvement in 2-4 weeks, with a focus on preventing recurrence over the next 2-3 months.

What makes it more likely to be the quadratus lumborum?

It is worse with standing or walking, turning your head or body, deep breaths or coughing, sitting, and pressing into the muscle reproduces the pain you know. Low back and flank, over the SI joint and buttock, and the point of the hip. Hurts rolling in bed, standing up or coughing.
