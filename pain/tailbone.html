Pain at the tailbone: What's Causing It

★★★★★ 5.0 · 89 Google reviews

Tailbone pain usually feels like a deep ache or a sharp jab right at the bottom of the spine that is worst when sitting, especially on hard surfaces or when leaning back, and it often eases on standing or lying down. People notice it after long sitting on a boat seat, a bicycle or a bleacher, after a fall onto the buttocks, or after childbirth. The multifidus, the deep stabilizing muscles that run down to the sacrum and tailbone, commonly refer pinpoint pain here. The gluteus maximus, the large buttock muscle that powers hills and stairs, attaches alongside it and adds a broader ache with long sitting.

Is it this, or something next to it?

A physician should first rule out a fracture or dislocation after a fall, infection or abscess, a pilonidal cyst, and rectal or pelvic organ problems, particularly with fever, bleeding or bowel change. Muscle-referred tailbone pain is tender in the buttock and sacrum muscles and eases when weight shifts forward. Beside-the-spine pain is higher in the low back, flank pain is out to the side, and SI pain sits over the dimples a few inches up and to the side.

Typical story: a Sarasota resident spends long weekend days on a hard boat bench and weekdays in a low car seat, slumped back on the tailbone. A deep ache develops at the base of the spine that jabs when sitting down on a hard chair and eases on standing. There has been no fall and no fever, and the muscles beside the sacrum are tender.

Muscles that commonly refer pain here

Ranked the way the MD Fit pain map ranks them, most common first:

  1. Multifidus. Deep, pinpoint pain right next to the spine, the sacrum and sometimes the tailbone.
  2. Gluteus maximus. Buttock and the tailbone; aches with long sitting and walking uphill. Worse with: sitting, stairs, squats or hills.

What people with this pain describe

Taken from Dr. Jared's notes on the gluteus maximus, the most common source here:

  • A deep, nagging ache in the buttock.
  • Pain concentrated around the tailbone (coccyx) and the bony part you sit on (ischial tuberosity).
  • Stiffness and pain after sitting for a long time, especially on hard surfaces.
  • A feeling of weakness when climbing stairs or getting up from a squat.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the top muscles are involved:

  • Is your pain mainly in the fleshy part of your buttock, possibly near your tailbone?
  • Does the ache get worse after sitting for more than 30 minutes, especially on a hard chair?
  • Do you feel pain or weakness when pushing up stairs, walking up a hill, or rising from a deep chair?

How it usually starts

  • Prolonged sitting, which compresses and deactivates the muscle.
  • Sudden increase in activities like running, hiking, or weightlifting (squats, deadlifts).
  • Direct trauma, such as a fall onto the buttocks.
  • Sleeping on a hard mattress or on one side without support.

What it gets mistaken for

Sciatica or lumbar radiculopathy (nerve root irritation).; Sacroiliac (SI) joint dysfunction.; Piriformis syndrome.. An evaluation separates muscle pain from these, and anything that needs a physician is referred on.

What makes it worse, and what that tells you

  • Sitting. Points toward the multifidus, gluteus maximus.
  • Stairs, squats or hills. Points toward the gluteus maximus.

Warning signs: get medical care, not a stretch

  • Loss of bladder or bowel control, or numbness in the groin or inner thighs: call 911.
  • Leg weakness that is getting worse, or numbness spreading down both legs: emergency care.
  • Back pain with fever, chills or unexplained weight loss, or that is constant and not eased by any position: see a physician.
  • Back pain after a fall or crash, or if you have osteoporosis or take steroids: get checked for a fracture.

Frequently asked questions

Why does it hurt at the tailbone if nothing is wrong there?

Muscles refer pain: a tight band in one muscle sends a predictable pattern of pain somewhere else. For pain at the tailbone, the usual sources are the multifidus, gluteus maximus.

How long does gluteus maximus pain usually take to settle?

With consistent application of this protocol, most people feel a significant reduction in pain within 2-3 weeks. Building full strength and resilience can take 6-8 weeks.

What makes it more likely to be the gluteus maximus?

It is worse with sitting, stairs, squats or hills, and pressing into the muscle reproduces the pain you know. Buttock and the tailbone; aches with long sitting and walking uphill.

Book an appointment if your pain is severe, doesn't improve after 2 weeks of self-care, or if you experience any of the 'Red Flags'.
