Pain at the si joint and back of the pelvis: What's Causing It

★★★★★ 5.0 · 89 Google reviews

Pain at the SI joint and the back of the pelvis feels like a deep ache over one of the dimples at the top of the buttock, sharp when standing on one leg, climbing stairs, getting out of a car, or rolling over at night. People notice it during pregnancy, after a stumble, a long walk on soft sand, or weeks of sitting on a wallet. The quadratus lumborum, the deep side muscle that hikes the hip, and the gluteus medius, the side hip muscle that steadies the pelvis on every step, are the usual sources. The longissimus and iliacus also refer pain here.

Is it this, or something next to it?

A physician should first rule out inflammatory arthritis of the SI joint, infection, fracture after a fall, and serious spinal causes with bladder or bowel change or leg weakness. Muscle-referred SI pain is tender in the muscles above and beside the joint and eases when the pelvis is supported. Beside-the-spine pain is higher and central, flank pain is up between the rib and pelvis, and tailbone pain is lower and worse sitting directly on it.

Typical story: a Sarasota resident starts long morning beach walks in soft sand to get back into shape, then sits for hours in a low car seat. A deep ache settles over one dimple at the back of the pelvis that stabs when stepping out of the car or putting on pants standing. There is no fever or leg weakness, and the hip muscles above the joint are tender.

Muscles that commonly refer pain here

Ranked the way the MD Fit pain map ranks them, most common first:

  1. Quadratus lumborum. Low back and flank, over the SI joint and buttock, and the point of the hip.
  2. Longissimus. Low in the buttock and over the SI joint, often well below where the muscle actually is. Worse with: standing or walking, sitting, bending or lifting.
  3. Gluteus medius. Along the belt line, over the SI joint, the buttock and the top of the hip.
  4. Iliacus. Groin, front of the thigh, and the back of the pelvis high in the glute.
  5. Multifidus. Deep, pinpoint pain right next to the spine, the sacrum and sometimes the tailbone.

What people with this pain describe

Taken from Dr. Jared's notes on the longissimus, the most common source here:

  • Pain low in the buttock and over the SI joint, often well below the muscle.
  • Ache beside the spine when standing for long periods.
  • Stiffness in the morning that eases with movement.
  • Pain arching back or straightening up.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the top muscles are involved:

  • Is your pain low in the buttock or over the SI joint, but tender spots are higher up beside the spine?
  • Does long standing or walking upright bring it on?
  • Does arching backward hurt?

How it usually starts

  • Long standing with a sway-back posture.
  • Weak abs and glutes.
  • Heavy lifting with an arched back.
  • Running on hard surfaces.

What it gets mistaken for

SI joint dysfunction; Lumbar facet joint pain; Piriformis trigger points. An evaluation separates muscle pain from these, and anything that needs a physician is referred on.

What makes it worse, and what that tells you

  • Standing or walking. Points toward the quadratus lumborum, longissimus, gluteus medius.
  • Sitting. Points toward the quadratus lumborum, longissimus, iliacus.
  • Bending or lifting. Points toward the longissimus, multifidus.

Warning signs: get medical care, not a stretch

  • Loss of bladder or bowel control, or numbness in the groin or inner thighs: call 911.
  • Leg weakness that is getting worse, or numbness spreading down both legs: emergency care.
  • Back pain with fever, chills or unexplained weight loss, or that is constant and not eased by any position: see a physician.
  • Back pain after a fall or crash, or if you have osteoporosis or take steroids: get checked for a fracture.

Frequently asked questions

Why does it hurt at the si joint and back of the pelvis if nothing is wrong there?

Muscles refer pain: a tight band in one muscle sends a predictable pattern of pain somewhere else. For pain at the si joint and back of the pelvis, the usual sources are the quadratus lumborum, longissimus, gluteus medius.

How long does longissimus pain usually take to settle?

Usually improves within 1 to 3 weeks.

What makes it more likely to be the longissimus?

It is worse with standing or walking, sitting, bending or lifting, and pressing into the muscle reproduces the pain you know. Low in the buttock and over the SI joint, often well below where the muscle actually is.

When should I book instead of working on it myself?

Book an evaluation if pain spreads down the leg, there are nerve symptoms, or no improvement in 2 to 3 weeks.
