Where Does It Hurt?

★★★★★ 5.0 · 89 Google reviews

Pick the spot. Each page lists the muscles that most often send pain there, drawn from Dr. Jared Caban's pain map, plus the free exercise track for that area and the warning signs that mean see a physician instead.

Prefer to start from the muscle? Use the muscle library. For a named problem, see conditions we treat.

Head & face

Head and face pain is one of the most common places for referred muscle pain to be mistaken for something else. The neck muscles, the jaw muscles and the trapezius all send pain into the head, which is why a headache can start in a shoulder that has been hunched over a phone. Posture, clenching and stress are the usual drivers; the pattern (one side, behind the eye, temple, forehead) is the clue to which muscle.

  • ForeheadSternocleidomastoid, Suboccipitals, Occipitalis
  • TempleUpper trapezius, Temporalis, Sternocleidomastoid
  • Behind or around the eyeSuboccipitals, Sternocleidomastoid, Splenius capitis & cervicis
  • Ear areaSternocleidomastoid, Masseter, Upper trapezius

Back of head

The small muscles at the base of the skull and the long neck muscles refer pain up and over the head. Hours of looking down at a screen, sleeping on a stack of pillows, or holding the head still against stress loads them all day. The relief work is gentle: these muscles respond to release and slow, controlled movement, not force.

  • Base of the skullSuboccipitals, Upper trapezius, Splenius capitis & cervicis
  • Back of the headOccipitalis, Suboccipitals, Splenius capitis & cervicis
  • Top of the headSplenius capitis & cervicis, Sternocleidomastoid

Neck

The neck carries the head, roughly the weight of a bowling ball, and every inch it drifts forward adds load to the muscles at the back and sides. Pain here is often referred from the scalenes, levator scapulae and upper trapezius, which also send pain into the shoulder, arm and head. Breathing pattern, desk setup and how you carry bags all matter.

  • Side of the neckScalenes, Levator scapulae, Sternocleidomastoid
  • Front of the neckSternocleidomastoid, Scalenes
  • Where the neck meets the shoulderLevator scapulae, Upper trapezius, Scalenes
  • Back of the neckSuboccipitals, Splenius capitis & cervicis, Levator scapulae

Shoulder

The top of the shoulder is a crossroads: the trapezius, levator scapulae and rotator cuff all meet here, and neck muscles refer into it too. Pain that lives here without a clear injury is usually muscular and improves once the shoulder blade moves well again and the neck muscles stop guarding.

  • Top of the shoulderUpper trapezius, Supraspinatus, Levator scapulae

Chest & ribs

Chest and rib pain has to be cleared by a physician first, every time. Once the heart and lungs are ruled out, the muscles of the chest wall, the scalenes in the neck and the muscles between the ribs are frequent sources, often from breathing patterns, coughing, posture or pressing exercises. They respond well to release and breathing work.

  • Front of the chestPectoralis major lower head, Pectoralis major upper head, Pectoralis minor
  • Side of the ribsSerratus anterior, Intercostals, Latissimus dorsi
  • Along one ribIntercostals, Serratus anterior
  • Belly or side of the abdomenExternal oblique, Intercostals, Psoas

Shoulder (front)

The front of the shoulder is where the biceps, pectorals and the front of the deltoid all converge, and the rotator cuff muscles behind the shoulder refer pain forward into it. That is why so much front-shoulder pain is blamed on the joint when the driver is a tight muscle at the back or in the chest. Overhead work, pressing and sleeping on the arm are the usual aggravators.

  • Under the collarboneSubclavius, Pectoralis major upper head, Scalenes
  • Front of the shoulderInfraspinatus, Anterior deltoid, Biceps long head
  • Outside of the shoulder or upper armLateral deltoid, Supraspinatus, Infraspinatus
  • Deep inside the shoulder jointInfraspinatus, Supraspinatus, Subscapularis

Shoulder (back)

Pain at the back of the shoulder usually comes from the rotator cuff muscles that sit on the shoulder blade, the rear deltoid or the long head of the triceps. These muscles work hardest when you reach behind you, carry at your side or hold the arm up for long periods, and they refer pain down the back of the arm.

  • Back of the upper armTriceps long head, Triceps lateral head, Teres major
  • Back of the shoulderPosterior deltoid, Teres minor, Subscapularis
  • On the shoulder bladeInfraspinatus, Middle trapezius, Supraspinatus

Upper back

The upper back is where tight chest muscles pull the shoulder blades apart and the muscles between the blades strain to hold on. Pain here is often referred from the scalenes in the neck as well as the rhomboids and middle trapezius. Desk work, driving and phone use are the usual loads; a stronger mid-back and a more open chest usually settle it.

  • Between the shoulder bladesScalenes, Rhomboid major, Middle trapezius
  • Inner edge of the shoulder bladeLevator scapulae, Rhomboid minor, Lower trapezius
  • Bottom tip of the shoulder bladeLatissimus dorsi, Serratus anterior, Iliocostalis

Elbow & forearm

Most elbow and forearm pain is tendon and muscle overload from gripping, typing and lifting: the wrist extensors on the outside, the flexors on the inside, the triceps at the point of the elbow and the biceps at the front. Neck and shoulder muscles can refer here too, which is why elbow pain sometimes will not settle until the shoulder is treated.

  • Front of the elbowBiceps long head, Biceps short head, Brachioradialis
  • Point of the elbowTriceps medial head, Triceps long head
  • ForearmFinger extensors, Wrist extensors, Wrist flexors

Wrist & hand

Hand and wrist pain is frequently referred from the forearm muscles and, further up, from the scalenes and pectoralis minor, which can also cause tingling in the fingers. Which fingers are involved is a useful clue. True nerve problems need a physician, so persistent numbness or weakness is always checked.

  • Back of the hand or wristWrist extensors, Finger extensors, Scalenes
  • Palm side of the wrist and handWrist flexors, Finger flexors, Pectoralis minor
  • Thumb and index fingerScalenes, Brachioradialis, Supinator
  • Ring and little fingerPectoralis minor, Pectoralis major lower head, Latissimus dorsi

Low back

Low back pain is the most common reason people walk into MD Fit, and the spine itself is rarely the source. The quadratus lumborum, the deep back extensors, the hip flexors and the glutes all refer pain here, driven by sitting, lifting and how you hinge. Serious red flags are listed below; once they are excluded, movement and strength are the treatment.

  • Right beside the spine (low back)Multifidus, Psoas, Longissimus
  • Flank or side of the low backQuadratus lumborum, Iliocostalis
  • SI joint and back of the pelvisQuadratus lumborum, Longissimus, Gluteus medius
  • TailboneMultifidus, Gluteus maximus

Buttock & hip

The buttock and outer hip are where the gluteal muscles, the piriformis and the quadratus lumborum send pain, and from here pain often runs down the leg in a sciatica-like pattern. Sitting, stairs and side-sleeping are the usual aggravators. Strong, well-timed glutes are the fix for most of it.

  • Middle of the buttockPiriformis, Gluteus minimus, Gluteus medius
  • Upper buttock at the belt lineGluteus medius, Quadratus lumborum, Iliacus
  • Outside of the hipGluteus minimus, Tensor fasciae latae, Gluteus medius

Hip (front)

Front-of-hip and groin pain in adults is usually the hip flexors (psoas and iliacus), the adductors and the front of the quadriceps, shortened by sitting and then asked to sprint, swing or climb. The hip joint itself can be involved, so pain that catches, locks or limits rotation is assessed carefully.

  • GroinIliacus, Adductor longus & brevis, Adductor magnus
  • Front of the hip boneRectus femoris, Iliacus, Tensor fasciae latae

Thigh

Thigh pain is mostly muscular: the quadriceps at the front, the tensor fasciae latae and vastus lateralis on the outside, the adductors on the inside. Hip flexor and gluteal trigger points also refer into the thigh. Sudden pain during sprinting or a kick is a strain and is handled differently from a slow-building ache.

  • Front of the thighRectus femoris, Vastus intermedius, Psoas
  • Outside of the thighVastus lateralis, Tensor fasciae latae, Gluteus minimus
  • Inner thighAdductor longus & brevis, Adductor magnus, Vastus medialis

Back of thigh

Hamstring-area pain comes from the hamstrings themselves, from the piriformis and gluteus minimus referring down the leg, and from the adductor magnus on the inside. Sitting on hard seats, sprinting and bending with straight knees are the common loads. Nerve-related pain down the leg is screened for first.

  • Back of the thighBiceps femoris, Semitendinosus & semimembranosus, Piriformis
  • Buttock creaseSemitendinosus & semimembranosus, Gluteus maximus
  • Back of the kneePopliteus, Gastrocnemius, Biceps femoris

Knee

Most knee pain without a clear injury comes from the quadriceps, the adductors and the hip muscles that control the knee from above, plus the popliteus and calf behind it. Where exactly it hurts, and whether stairs, squatting or sitting provoke it, points to the muscle. Swelling, locking or giving way need a physician's assessment.

  • Kneecap or just below itRectus femoris, Vastus medialis, Vastus lateralis
  • Inside of the kneeVastus medialis, Adductor longus & brevis, Semitendinosus & semimembranosus
  • Outside of the kneeVastus lateralis, Tensor fasciae latae, Biceps femoris

Calf

Calf pain is usually the gastrocnemius or soleus, but the gluteus minimus and peroneals refer here too, so a calf that will not settle may have its driver at the hip. A blood clot can mimic a calf strain and is a medical emergency; the warning signs are listed below.

  • Upper calfGastrocnemius, Gluteus minimus
  • Lower calfSoleus, Gastrocnemius, Tibialis posterior
  • Outer calfGluteus minimus, Peroneals, Piriformis

Ankle & foot

Foot and ankle pain often starts higher up: the tibialis posterior, the calf muscles and the peroneals refer into the arch, heel and sides of the ankle, and the small foot muscles tighten from unsupportive shoes and hard floors. Strength and mobility of the calf and foot, plus footwear, settle most of it.

  • Inner ankle and archTibialis posterior, Foot muscles, Gastrocnemius
  • Outer anklePeroneals, Gluteus minimus
  • Front of the ankle or big toeTibialis anterior
  • Sole of the footFoot muscles, Gastrocnemius, Soleus
