Pain at the groin: What's Causing It

★★★★★ 5.0 · 89 Google reviews

Groin pain often feels like a deep, pulling ache in the crease where the thigh meets the pelvis, sometimes spreading into the inner thigh, sharp when striding out, pivoting on the planted foot, or lifting the knee to get into a car. People notice it after a sudden sidestep in pickleball, long sitting, or a jump in running or cycling. The iliacus, the hip flexor lining the pelvic bowl that lifts the thigh, is an overlooked source. The adductor longus and brevis, the inner thigh muscles that pull the legs together and steady the pelvis, add ache deep in the groin and down the inner thigh.

Is it this, or something next to it?

A physician should first rule out an inguinal hernia, hip joint problems such as a labral tear or arthritis, a stress fracture, and testicular, ovarian or urinary causes, especially with swelling, fever or a bulge on straining. Muscle groin pain is tender to press in the inner thigh or deep in the hip crease and reproduced by squeezing the knees together. Front-of-hip-bone pain sits higher and more lateral on the bony point, while joint pain pinches deep with hip rotation.

Typical story: a Sarasota pickleball player lunges hard for a wide shot during a tournament weekend, then sits for a long drive home. A deep pulling ache sets into the groin crease that catches when getting out of the car or crossing the leg to tie a shoe. There is no bulge or swelling, and squeezing a pillow between the knees reproduces the ache.

Muscles that commonly refer pain here

Ranked the way the MD Fit pain map ranks them, most common first:

  1. Iliacus. Groin, front of the thigh, and the back of the pelvis high in the glute. Often overlooked. Worse with: sitting, running or sports.
  2. Adductor longus & brevis. Deep groin, the inner thigh and down to the inner knee and top of the shin.
  3. Adductor magnus. Deep inside the pelvis and groin, and the inner thigh from the groin toward the knee.
  4. Psoas. Vertical band along the low back, plus the front of the thigh and groin.
  5. Pectineus. Deep in the groin just below the crease, and into the upper inner thigh. A common missed cause of a groin that will not settle. Worse with: running or sports, sitting, stairs, squats or hills.
  6. Gracilis. A hot, superficial, stinging line down the inner thigh, sometimes continuing to the inner knee. Worse with: running or sports, stairs, squats or hills, sitting.

What people with this pain describe

Taken from Dr. Jared's notes on the iliacus and the pectineus, the two most common sources here:

  • A persistent ache deep in the groin or front pocket area.
  • Pain that refers to the front of the thigh.
  • A tender spot on the front of the hip, just inside the bony point (ASIS).
  • Pain that can also refer to the low back or upper buttock area.
  • Deep groin pain just below the crease, hard to pinpoint.
  • Pain in the upper inner thigh.
  • A groin strain that has not settled with usual adductor rehabilitation.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the top muscles are involved:

  • Is your primary pain located deep in your groin, like in your front pocket?
  • Do you feel a very tender spot if you press your fingers just inside the bony point at the front of your hip?
  • Does the pain get worse when you are sitting for a long time, especially while driving?
  • Is your groin pain deep and just below the crease rather than further down the inner thigh?
  • Has a groin strain failed to settle with standard adductor exercises?

How it usually starts

  • Prolonged sitting, which keeps the muscle in a shortened position.
  • Activities involving repetitive hip flexion, like running, cycling, or kicking.
  • Driving for long periods, especially with the gas pedal foot.
  • Wearing a heavy tool belt that presses on the front of the hips.
  • Sports with cutting, kicking and rapid direction change.
  • A sudden increase in training load.

What it gets mistaken for

Hip joint impingement (FAI) or labral tear; Inguinal hernia; Sports hernia (athletic pubalgia); Inguinal hernia, which must be excluded.; Hip joint problems including labral tear and osteoarthritis.. An evaluation separates muscle pain from these, and anything that needs a physician is referred on.

What makes it worse, and what that tells you

  • Sitting. Points toward the iliacus, adductor magnus, psoas.
  • Running or sports. Points toward the iliacus, adductor longus & brevis, adductor magnus.
  • Stairs, squats or hills. Points toward the adductor longus & brevis, adductor magnus, pectineus.

Warning signs: get medical care, not a stretch

  • Groin pain with a lump that will not push back in, vomiting or fever can be a hernia emergency: go to the ER.
  • Groin or lower belly pain with fever, burning urination or blood in the urine: see a physician.
  • In pregnancy, new pelvic or groin pain should be checked by your OB.
  • A fall followed by inability to bear weight: get an X-ray.

Frequently asked questions

Why does it hurt at the groin if nothing is wrong there?

Muscles refer pain: a tight band in one muscle sends a predictable pattern of pain somewhere else. For pain at the groin, the usual sources are the iliacus, adductor longus & brevis, adductor magnus.

How long does iliacus pain usually take to settle?

Most people experience noticeable relief within 1-2 weeks of consistent release work and stretching. Building strength to prevent recurrence typically takes 4-6 weeks.

What makes it more likely to be the iliacus?

It is worse with sitting, running or sports, and pressing into the muscle reproduces the pain you know. Groin, front of the thigh, and the back of the pelvis high in the glute. Often overlooked.

When should I book instead of working on it myself?

Schedule a consultation if the pain is sharp, doesn't improve with 2 weeks of this protocol, or if you suspect a hernia.
