Pain at the back of the knee: What's Causing It

★★★★★ 5.0 · 89 Google reviews

Pain behind the knee usually feels like a deep ache or a tight fullness in the hollow at the back of the joint, sharp when squatting, kneeling, walking downhill, or fully straightening the leg. People notice it after a hilly hike, a downhill run, or long standing with the knees locked. The popliteus, the small muscle that unlocks the knee at the start of every step and steadies it on slopes, is a frequent and overlooked source. The gastrocnemius, the upper calf muscle that crosses the back of the knee, and the hamstrings that attach on either side of the hollow add aching and tightness.

Is it this, or something next to it?

A physician should first rule out a deep vein clot with calf swelling, warmth and tenderness, a Baker's cyst with a visible lump, a meniscus or ligament injury with locking, giving way or swelling. Muscle pain here is tender in the soft hollow or upper calf and reproduced by a deep squat or resisted knee bend. Back-of-thigh pain is higher in the hamstring belly, and buttock-crease pain is at the sit bone far above the knee.

Typical story: a Sarasota resident visits the mountains for a week of hiking with long downhill stretches, then returns to flat beach walks with the knees locked straight. A deep ache settles into the hollow behind one knee that catches when squatting to the bottom drawer or stepping down a curb. There is no swelling, lump or locking, and pressing the soft hollow reproduces the ache.

Muscles that commonly refer pain here

Ranked the way the MD Fit pain map ranks them, most common first:

  1. Popliteus. Back of the knee; worse crouching, walking downhill or running. Worse with: stairs, squats or hills, running or sports.
  2. Gastrocnemius. Upper calf, back of the knee and the arch of the foot; deep cramp-like tightness. Worse with: running or sports, stairs, squats or hills, standing or walking.
  3. Biceps femoris. Back of the knee on the outside and up the back of the thigh.
  4. Semitendinosus & semimembranosus. The buttock crease, back of the thigh and the inner back of the knee, sometimes the inner calf.
  5. Semimembranosus. The buttock crease and the back of the thigh, concentrating deep in the inner back of the knee.
  6. Plantaris. The back of the knee and the upper inner calf.

What people with this pain describe

Taken from Dr. Jared's notes on the popliteus and the gastrocnemius, the two most common sources here:

  • Sharp pain located directly in the back of the knee.
  • Pain that is worse when walking or running, especially downhill.
  • A feeling of instability or that the knee might 'give way' when twisting.
  • Difficulty fully straightening or 'locking' the knee.
  • A deep, cramp-like ache in the upper or middle part of the calf.
  • Pain that can refer up into the back of the knee.
  • Aching pain in the arch of the foot, especially in the morning or after rest.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the top muscles are involved:

  • Is your pain located precisely in the hollow at the back of your knee?
  • Is the pain significantly worse when you walk or run downhill?
  • Do you feel a sharp pain when you try to squat down low?
  • Do you get intense, cramp-like pain in your upper calf, sometimes even at night?
  • Does your pain also seem to travel into the arch of your foot?

How it usually starts

  • Overuse in sports that involve a lot of downhill running (e.g., trail running).
  • A sudden twist of the knee while the foot is planted.
  • Chronic over-pronation of the foot (foot rolling inward).
  • Muscle imbalances, particularly weak hamstrings or glutes.
  • Sudden increase in running mileage or intensity.
  • Activities with a lot of jumping or hill climbing.

What it gets mistaken for

Posterior horn of the meniscus tear; Baker's cyst (a fluid-filled sac behind the knee); Hamstring tendinopathy; Soleus muscle strain (pain is typically lower in the calf); Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT) - a blood clot (this is a medical emergency). An evaluation separates muscle pain from these, and anything that needs a physician is referred on.

What makes it worse, and what that tells you

  • Stairs, squats or hills. Points toward the popliteus, gastrocnemius, semimembranosus.
  • Running or sports. Points toward the popliteus, gastrocnemius, biceps femoris.
  • Standing or walking. Points toward the gastrocnemius.

Warning signs: get medical care, not a stretch

  • A swollen, warm, red or tender calf, especially after travel, surgery or bed rest, can be a blood clot: call 911 or go to the ER.
  • Sudden severe calf pain with a pop, and you cannot push off the foot: urgent care.
  • Leg pain with numbness in the groin or loss of bladder or bowel control: call 911.
  • Coldness, paleness or no pulse in the foot: emergency care.

Frequently asked questions

Why does it hurt at the back of the knee if nothing is wrong there?

Muscles refer pain: a tight band in one muscle sends a predictable pattern of pain somewhere else. For pain at the back of the knee, the usual sources are the popliteus, gastrocnemius, biceps femoris.

How long does popliteus pain usually take to settle?

Because it's a small stabilizer, popliteus pain can be stubborn. Consistent, gentle exercises should bring noticeable relief in 2-3 weeks, with a return to more demanding activities in 4-6 weeks.

What makes it more likely to be the popliteus?

It is worse with stairs, squats or hills, running or sports, and pressing into the muscle reproduces the pain you know. Back of the knee; worse crouching, walking downhill or running.

When should I book instead of working on it myself?

Book an appointment if you suspect a meniscus or ligament injury, if the knee is locking or giving way, or if pain does not improve with 2-3 weeks of conservative care.
