Pain at the temple: What's Causing It

★★★★★ 5.0 · 89 Google reviews

Temple pain usually feels like a one-sided throb or a pressing ache just in front of the ear and above the cheekbone, sometimes reaching the eyebrow or the upper teeth. People often notice it after a stressful morning, on waking after a night of clenching, or late in the day when the shoulders have crept up toward the ears. The upper trapezius, the big shoulder-shrugging muscle, commonly refers a question-mark shaped ache up the side of the neck into the temple. The temporalis, the fan-shaped chewing muscle under the temple itself, adds a dull toothache-like quality when the jaw is tight.

Is it this, or something next to it?

A physician should first rule out head emergencies, including a sudden severe headache, a new headache with jaw pain and vision change in an older adult, fever, or weakness, which need urgent care. Muscle-related temple pain is usually tender to press directly over the temple or the top of the shoulder and shifts with jaw clenching. Forehead pain spreads across both sides; behind-the-eye pain feels deeper and worsens with eye movement; ear-area pain is lower and rarely throbs.

Typical story: a Sarasota professional drives an hour to and from the office, sits through long meetings with shoulders hunched, and wakes most mornings with a sore jaw. Mid-afternoon a steady one-sided ache sets in over the temple, with the same side of the upper shoulder feeling like a rock. It is better on vacation and worse during busy weeks.

Muscles that commonly refer pain here

Ranked the way the MD Fit pain map ranks them, most common first:

  1. Upper trapezius. Up the side of the neck, behind the ear and into the temple (a question-mark shape).
  2. Temporalis. Temple headache, above the eyebrow and into the upper teeth. Worse with: clenching or chewing, stress.
  3. Sternocleidomastoid. Forehead, around and behind the eye, cheek, ear and top of the head.

What people with this pain describe

Taken from Dr. Jared's notes on the temporalis, the most common source here:

  • A headache felt in the temple and side of the head.
  • Pain that feels like it's above your eye or behind your eyebrow.
  • Sensitivity or aching in your upper teeth, which can be mistaken for a dental problem.
  • Pain when chewing hard or chewy foods.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the top muscles are involved:

  • Do you get headaches primarily in your temples?
  • Do your upper teeth ache, even though your dentist says they are healthy?
  • Is your headache worse after chewing gum, eating a tough steak, or clenching your jaw?

How it usually starts

  • Bruxism (clenching or grinding teeth), especially during sleep or concentration.
  • Excessive gum chewing or eating very hard/chewy foods.
  • Dental issues, such as a misaligned bite or recent dental work.
  • Emotional stress and anxiety.

What it gets mistaken for

Tension-type headache or migraine.; Dental problems (cavities, abscesses).; Trigeminal neuralgia.. An evaluation separates muscle pain from these, and anything that needs a physician is referred on.

What makes it worse, and what that tells you

  • Clenching or chewing. Points toward the temporalis.
  • Stress. Points toward the upper trapezius, temporalis.

Warning signs: get medical care, not a stretch

  • A sudden, severe headache unlike any you have had, or the worst headache of your life: call 911.
  • Headache with fever and a stiff neck, confusion, slurred speech, weakness or numbness on one side, or loss of vision: call 911.
  • Head pain after a fall or blow to the head, especially with vomiting or drowsiness: emergency care.
  • Jaw or face pain with chest pressure, shortness of breath or sweating can be a heart attack: call 911.

Frequently asked questions

Why does it hurt at the temple if nothing is wrong there?

Muscles refer pain: a tight band in one muscle sends a predictable pattern of pain somewhere else. For pain at the temple, the usual sources are the upper trapezius, temporalis, sternocleidomastoid.

How long does temporalis pain usually take to settle?

You can often feel relief from acute pain within a few days of starting massage and relaxation. Lasting improvement, especially if related to clenching, may take 4-6 weeks of consistent habit changes and exercises.

What makes it more likely to be the temporalis?

It is worse with clenching or chewing, stress, and pressing into the muscle reproduces the pain you know. Temple headache, above the eyebrow and into the upper teeth.

When should I book instead of working on it myself?

Book an appointment if your pain is severe, you have clicking or locking in your jaw, or if self-care doesn't provide relief after 2-3 weeks. A dentist or TMJ specialist may be needed.
