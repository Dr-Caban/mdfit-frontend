Pain at the kneecap or just below it: What's Causing It

★★★★★ 5.0 · 89 Google reviews

Pain at the kneecap or just below it feels like a deep ache around or behind the kneecap, grinding on stairs, or a sharp point at the top of the shin, and it flares after sitting with the knee bent through a movie or flight. People notice it on stairs, squatting, kneeling, or standing up from a low chair. The quadriceps, the four thigh muscles that straighten the knee and control how the kneecap tracks in its groove, are the most common source, particularly the rectus femoris and vastus medialis. Tight bands in the vastus lateralis and intermedius add pressure on the kneecap.

Is it this, or something next to it?

A physician should first rule out a fracture or patellar tendon injury after a fall or sudden pop, arthritis with swelling and warmth, a meniscus tear with locking, and infection with a hot red joint. Muscle-referred kneecap pain comes with tender quads and eases when the thigh is relaxed, without swelling. Inside-of-knee pain sits along the inner joint line and tracks the adductors and vastus medialis, while outside-of-knee pain follows the outer band from the hip.

Typical story: a Sarasota resident flies in for the season, sits for hours with the knees bent, then starts climbing condo stairs daily and kneeling to plant a garden. A deep ache sets in around and just below one kneecap that grinds coming down stairs and stiffens after sitting through dinner. There is no swelling or locking, and pressing the thigh muscles finds tender bands.

Muscles that commonly refer pain here

Ranked the way the MD Fit pain map ranks them, most common first:

  1. Rectus femoris. A deep ache around and behind the kneecap, and the front of the hip bone.
  2. Vastus medialis. Inner knee and inside of the kneecap.
  3. Vastus lateralis. Down the outer thigh to the outside of the knee.
  4. Vastus intermedius. Front of the mid-thigh, worse climbing stairs or standing up from a chair.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the top muscles are involved:

  • Is your main pain a deep ache around or under your kneecap?
  • Does the pain get worse after you've been sitting for a while with your knees bent?

How it usually starts

  • Prolonged sitting, which keeps the muscle in a shortened state at the hip and lengthened at the knee.
  • Overuse from activities like kicking, sprinting, or cycling.

What it gets mistaken for

Patellofemoral pain syndrome (runner's knee).; Patellar tendonitis (jumper's knee).. An evaluation separates muscle pain from these, and anything that needs a physician is referred on.

What makes it worse, and what that tells you

  • Stairs, squats or hills. Points toward the rectus femoris, vastus medialis, vastus lateralis.
  • Sitting. Points toward the rectus femoris, vastus intermedius.
  • Running or sports. Points toward the rectus femoris, vastus medialis, vastus lateralis.

Warning signs: get medical care, not a stretch

  • Pain after a fall or impact with deformity, or you cannot bear weight or use the limb: get an X-ray.
  • A joint that is hot, red, swollen and painful, especially with fever: urgent care.
  • Numbness, pins and needles or weakness that is spreading or constant: see a physician.
  • Pain that is constant, worse at night and not changed by movement or position: see a physician.

Frequently asked questions

Why does it hurt at the kneecap or just below it if nothing is wrong there?

Muscles refer pain: a tight band in one muscle sends a predictable pattern of pain somewhere else. For pain at the kneecap or just below it, the usual sources are the rectus femoris, vastus medialis, vastus lateralis.

How long does rectus femoris pain usually take to settle?

Pain from sitting should decrease within a week or two. Building the strength to handle activities like running and stairs without pain typically takes 4-8 weeks of consistent effort.

What makes it more likely to be the rectus femoris?

It is worse with stairs, squats or hills, sitting, running or sports, and pressing into the muscle reproduces the pain you know. A deep ache around and behind the kneecap, and the front of the hip bone. Worse on stairs or after sitting.
