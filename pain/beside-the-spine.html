Pain right beside the spine (low back): What's Causing It

★★★★★ 5.0 · 89 Google reviews

Pain right beside the spine in the low back usually feels like a deep, pinpoint ache or a sharp catch in a spot the size of a coin just to one side of the vertebrae, often worst when bending forward, standing up from a chair, or rolling over in bed. People notice it after a long drive, a weekend of lifting, or weeks of sitting slumped. The multifidus, the small deep muscles that stitch each vertebra to the next and steady the spine during every movement, are the most common source, and they weaken quickly when the back is sore. The psoas, the deep hip flexor that runs along the front of the lumbar spine, adds a vertical band of ache that makes standing tall hard after sitting.

Is it this, or something next to it?

A physician should first rule out serious causes: low back pain with fever, unexplained weight loss, loss of bladder or bowel control, numbness in the saddle area, progressive leg weakness, or pain after a significant fall needs urgent care, and kidney problems when pain comes with urinary changes. Muscle pain here is tender to press right beside the spine and is not usually worse with a cough or sneeze. Flank pain sits further out to the side, SI pain is lower over the dimples of the pelvis, and tailbone pain is at the very bottom.

Typical story: a Sarasota resident returns for the season after a long drive down, then spends the first weekend unpacking boxes and bending over garden beds. A deep pinpoint ache develops just to one side of the low spine that catches when straightening up from a chair. Legs are strong and sensation is normal, and pressing the spot beside the spine finds the exact tender point.

Muscles that commonly refer pain here

Ranked the way the MD Fit pain map ranks them, most common first:

  1. Multifidus. Deep, pinpoint pain right next to the spine, the sacrum and sometimes the tailbone. Worse with: sitting, standing or walking, bending or lifting.
  2. Psoas. Vertical band along the low back, plus the front of the thigh and groin. Hard to stand up straight after sitting. Worse with: sitting, standing or walking.
  3. Longissimus. Low in the buttock and over the SI joint, often well below where the muscle actually is.

What people with this pain describe

Taken from Dr. Jared's notes on the multifidus and the psoas, the two most common sources here:

  • A deep, sharp, or burning pain felt right next to the spine.
  • Pain is often pinpoint and you can put a finger directly on it.
  • Can refer pain down to the sacrum, tailbone, or occasionally into the abdomen.
  • A feeling of instability or 'giving way' in the low back.
  • A deep, vertical band of aching pain along one or both sides of your lower back.
  • Pain that refers to the front of your thigh and groin area.
  • Difficulty and pain when trying to stand up straight after sitting for a while.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the top muscles are involved:

  • Can you point with one finger to a specific, deep spot of pain right next to your spine?
  • Do you feel a deep ache near your tailbone or sacrum that doesn't seem to be in the buttock muscles?
  • Does your back feel weak or like it might 'give out' with certain movements?
  • Do you feel a deep ache running vertically up your low back, right next to the spine?
  • Is it difficult or painful to stand up fully straight after sitting for more than 20-30 minutes?

How it usually starts

  • A sudden twist or awkward lift that injures the deep spinal structures.
  • Prolonged sitting, which allows the multifidus muscles to weaken and atrophy.
  • Chronic poor posture, especially slouching.
  • Following a lumbar disc injury, as pain can inhibit these muscles from firing correctly.
  • Prolonged sitting with hips flexed (e.g., desk work, driving).
  • Sleeping in a fetal position.

What it gets mistaken for

Lumbar facet joint syndrome (very closely related and often co-existing); Sacroiliac (SI) joint dysfunction; Coccydynia (tailbone pain); Lumbar disc herniation; Hip joint arthritis or labral tear. An evaluation separates muscle pain from these, and anything that needs a physician is referred on.

What makes it worse, and what that tells you

  • Sitting. Points toward the multifidus, psoas, longissimus.
  • Standing or walking. Points toward the multifidus, psoas, longissimus.
  • Bending or lifting. Points toward the multifidus, longissimus.

Warning signs: get medical care, not a stretch

  • Loss of bladder or bowel control, or numbness in the groin or inner thighs: call 911.
  • Leg weakness that is getting worse, or numbness spreading down both legs: emergency care.
  • Back pain with fever, chills or unexplained weight loss, or that is constant and not eased by any position: see a physician.
  • Back pain after a fall or crash, or if you have osteoporosis or take steroids: get checked for a fracture.

Frequently asked questions

Why does it hurt right beside the spine (low back) if nothing is wrong there?

Muscles refer pain: a tight band in one muscle sends a predictable pattern of pain somewhere else. For pain right beside the spine (low back), the usual sources are the multifidus, psoas, longissimus.

How long does multifidus pain usually take to settle?

Because these are deep stabilizing muscles, retraining them takes time and focus. You may feel less 'instability' in 2-4 weeks, but building true endurance and automatic activation can take 3-6 months of consistent practice.

What makes it more likely to be the multifidus?

It is worse with sitting, standing or walking, bending or lifting, and pressing into the muscle reproduces the pain you know. Deep, pinpoint pain right next to the spine, the sacrum and sometimes the tailbone.

When should I book instead of working on it myself?

Book an evaluation if you have any red flag symptoms, if the pain is the result of a specific injury, or if you are not making steady progress after 4 weeks. This type of pain often benefits from hands-on assessment.
