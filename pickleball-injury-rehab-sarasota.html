Why pickleball injures people who were fine at other sports

Pickleball looks gentle. It is not. The court is small, so every point is a series of hard stops, lateral shuffles and quick reaches, and the kitchen line rewards lunging forward and then backpedaling. Many Sarasota players pick it up in their sixties after years away from cutting sports, and the calves, Achilles tendons, rotator cuffs and knees have not tolerated that load in a long time. Add long open-play sessions, concrete courts and Florida heat, and the first season goes fine, then something gives.

The injuries we see most

  • Rotator cuff irritation, especially the supraspinatus and infraspinatus, from overheads and high backhands. A deep ache at the top or front of the shoulder, worse reaching overhead and lying on that side. See shoulder impingement and rotator cuff tears.
  • Tennis elbow on the backhand side from a tight grip and wristy dinks; the wrist extensors refer pain to the outside of the elbow and the back of the hand.
  • Calf strains in the gastrocnemius from a sudden push-off, often felt as a pop in the upper calf.
  • Achilles tendon pain and plantar fasciitis from repeated split steps on hard courts.
  • Patellar tendon pain below the kneecap from lunging to the kitchen line.
  • Ankle sprains from rolling over the outside of the foot on a lateral reach; the peroneals stay guarded long after the ligament heals.

How we treat it

The evaluation finds which muscles reproduce your pain and which movement loaded them. A shoulder that hurts on overheads often traces to a stiff mid-back and a shoulder blade that does not rotate up. Tennis elbow usually improves faster when the forearm is released and the grip is retrained than when the elbow is treated in isolation. Dr. Jared does the soft-tissue work in the clinic and assigns the corrective exercises as videos in the app. Persistent tendon pain is managed with progressive loading rather than rest alone.

The Pickleball Beginner Protocol: six weeks to court readiness

The protocol runs from Movement Quality, Stability and Control in week one to Full Integration, Court Readiness and Consistency in week six. Each workout opens with a 5 to 8 minute warm-up of ankle, knee and hip circles, arm swings and torso rotations, and closes with 5 to 10 minutes of light walking, stretching and breathing.

The strength work builds in a straight line: Glute Bridge, Split Squat, Lateral Step Down and Farmer Carry for the lower body; Shoulder Set, Banded Row, Incline Push-Up and Banded External Rotation for the shoulder; Dead Bug, Side Plank and Pallof Press for the trunk. Court-specific movement starts with the Lateral Shuffle and Split Step plus Shadow Volley, then adds the Crossover Step to a cone, Forward and Backward Run with a plant, and in weeks five and six a Rotational Medicine Ball Throw and Step-Up with Knee Drive.

Intermediate and advanced protocols

The Pickleball Intermediate Protocol (30 to 45 minute workouts) adds load and reaction: Lateral Lunge to Stick, weighted Single-Leg RDL, Half-Kneeling Cable Press, Forward and Back Pedal with Turn and Sprint, and the Lateral Bound to Stick. The Pickleball Advanced Protocol (45 to 60 minutes) is for tournament players: Trap Bar Deadlift, Lateral Skater Bound, Box Jump to Stick, Face Pull to External Rotation for shoulder health, Med Ball Rotational Slam and Split Step to Sprint and Backpedal with reactive cone taps. Both keep the same key cue: land soft, stay low, shoulders down.

Playing while you rehab

Often you can. Typical modifications are dinking-only sessions while a shoulder calms down, a looser grip and a lighter paddle for elbow pain, a calf and Achilles warm-up before the first game, playing doubles rather than singles, and capping open play at 90 minutes for the first few weeks. Your plan tells you exactly what to keep, modify or pause. There is no off-season here, so we build one into the week.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Supraspinatus: Deep ache at the top and side of the shoulder that can run down the outside of the arm to the elbow.
  • Infraspinatus: Deep in the front of the shoulder joint, down the front and outside of the arm.
  • Wrist extensors: Outside of the elbow (tennis elbow) and the back of the wrist and hand on the thumb side.
  • Gastrocnemius: Upper calf, back of the knee and the arch of the foot; deep cramp-like tightness.
  • Peroneals: Outer ankle and outside of the foot; common after ankle sprains.
  • Rectus femoris: A deep ache around and behind the kneecap, and the front of the hip bone.

What to expect

Muscle-driven shoulder and elbow pain often eases within two to four weeks of release and corrective work. Tendon problems such as Achilles or patellar pain usually take longer because tendon adapts slowly, often six to twelve weeks of progressive loading. A calf strain depends on the grade. The beginner protocol is six weeks, and most players are back to league play before it ends, with restrictions lifted as the movement improves.

When to get medical care first

  • A pop in the calf or back of the ankle with inability to rise onto your toes may be an Achilles rupture and needs urgent medical care.
  • A knee that locks, gives way or swells rapidly within hours of an injury should be assessed by a physician before you play again.
  • An ankle you cannot bear weight on after a sprain, or with pain over the bone itself, needs an X-ray.
  • Chest pain, shortness of breath or dizziness during play: stop and call 911.
  • Shoulder weakness that appears suddenly after a fall may mean a tear that needs imaging.

Frequently asked questions

Can I keep playing while I rehab?

Often yes, with modifications such as dinking-only sessions, a lighter paddle or shorter play. Your plan tells you what to keep, modify or pause, and the restrictions come off as the movement improves.

Is the beginner protocol only for new players?

No. It is for anyone returning from injury or who has never trained for the court, including strong players. Dr. Jared moves you to the intermediate or advanced protocol when the movement is ready.

What should I bring to the first visit?

Your paddle and the shoes you play in; watching your ready position and backhand shows where the load is going.

Why does my shoulder hurt if the MRI only shows age-related changes?

Most people over fifty show cuff changes on imaging without pain. Pain usually tracks with how the shoulder blade and mid-back move and how guarded the cuff muscles are, which is what the evaluation tests.
