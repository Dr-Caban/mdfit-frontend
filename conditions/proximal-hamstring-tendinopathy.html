What high hamstring tendon pain feels like

The pain sits deep in the buttock crease, right over the sitting bone, and people usually point to it with one finger rather than a whole hand. The signature is that it hurts to sit. A hard chair, a long drive down I-75, a bleacher seat or a boat bench will bring it on within minutes, and standing up gives relief.

  • An ache that builds the longer you sit, and eases after you get up and walk.
  • Pain with uphill running, lunging, deadlifting or any deep bend at the hip.
  • Stiffness and soreness in the first steps of a run that may warm up, then return worse afterwards.
  • Tenderness when you press the sitting bone itself.
  • Stretching the hamstring feels like it should help, and makes it worse.

It builds slowly in most people. Runners, rowers, cyclists on a high saddle and people who have recently added hill work or hip hinging are the common stories. It also turns up in people who simply sit a great deal on a hard surface.

Why it is not sciatica and not piriformis syndrome

All three produce buttock pain, which is why this gets mislabelled so often. The distinctions are usually clear once you know what to ask.

  • Tendon pain is local and mechanical. It stays at the sitting bone, hurts to press, and is driven by load. Sciatica travels. It runs down the back of the leg, often past the knee, and brings burning, numbness or pins and needles with it.
  • Sitting makes both worse, but differently. Tendon pain is worst on a hard edge that compresses the tendon against the bone. Nerve pain is worse with slumped sitting and often improves if you sit upright.
  • The stretch test splits them. Pulling the straight leg up toward you compresses the hamstring tendon against the bone and reproduces tendon pain. The same movement can also tension the sciatic nerve, so Dr. Jared adds neck flexion or ankle movement, which changes nerve tension but not tendon compression.
  • Piriformis syndrome sits higher and more to the middle of the buttock, and is usually sore with sitting cross-legged and with resisted hip rotation.

The tendon and the nerve also sit within a few centimetres of each other, so the two problems can coexist, and an irritated tendon can irritate the nerve beside it.

The muscles involved

Three hamstrings share that attachment on the sitting bone, and the surrounding muscles decide how much load lands there.

  • Biceps femoris is the outer hamstring and the most commonly involved. It refers pain into the back of the thigh and behind the knee.
  • Semitendinosus and semimembranosus, the inner hamstrings, share the same bony origin and add a deeper, more medial ache.
  • Gluteus maximus is the key player, because when it is not doing its share of hip extension, the hamstrings take the load. A weak glute is the most common reason this tendon keeps getting overloaded.
  • Adductor magnus attaches close by and extends the hip too; it often tightens alongside.
  • Piriformis sits just above and frequently guards, which is part of why the two diagnoses get muddled.

How MD Fit treats it

Tendons do not respond to rest and stretching the way strained muscles do. They respond to graded load, and they get worse with compression. That shapes everything.

Assess. Dr. Jared confirms the tendon is the source, screens the nerve so sciatica is not missed, and tests hip extension strength, glute function and how you hinge and run. Release. Hands-on work goes to the hamstring belly, glute max and adductor magnus, deliberately avoiding heavy pressure directly on the irritated tendon attachment. Correct. You learn to unload the tendon immediately: how to sit, how to hinge from the hips without dropping into end-range flexion, and which stretches to stop. Homework. Load builds steadily through bridges and hip extension work with the hip in a neutral position first, then into longer ranges and finally into the speed and hill work you want back.

What you can do this week

  1. Stop stretching it. Pulling the leg toward your chest compresses the tendon against the bone and is the single most common thing that keeps this going.
  2. Pad your seat and sit on a cushion, or shift forward so the sitting bone is not pressed on a hard edge. Stand up every twenty or thirty minutes.
  3. Pause hill running, deep lunges and heavy deadlifts for now. Flat walking is usually fine.
  4. Try a gentle isometric: lie on your back, heel on the floor with the knee slightly bent, and press the heel down into the floor at a moderate effort for thirty seconds. If that eases the pain, it is a good sign the tendon is the source.

Other causes of sitting-bone pain

A sudden tearing pain during a sprint or a slip, with bruising down the thigh, points to a hamstring tear rather than a gradual tendinopathy, and a complete avulsion off the bone needs a surgeon's opinion promptly. Ischial bursitis produces similar sitting pain and often travels with the tendon problem. Pain that is more central and worse with standing on one leg may be the sacroiliac joint. In adolescents, pain at this attachment can be a growth plate injury and needs imaging. Deep buttock pain with bowel or bladder changes is a medical emergency.

Sarasota notes

This one shows up in our runners who have discovered the Legacy Trail bridges, in cyclists who sit a long time on a narrow saddle, and in rowers. It is also common in people who are not athletic at all and simply sit for long stretches. The treatment is the same either way: take the compression off, then build the tendon back up so it tolerates the loads you actually want.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Biceps femoris: Back of the knee on the outside and up the back of the thigh.
  • Semitendinosus & semimembranosus: The buttock crease, back of the thigh and the inner back of the knee, sometimes the inner calf.
  • Gluteus maximus: Buttock and the tailbone; aches with long sitting and walking uphill.
  • Adductor magnus: Deep inside the pelvis and groin, and the inner thigh from the groin toward the knee.
  • Piriformis: Mid-buttock and back of the hip, down the back-outside of the thigh toward the calf.

What to expect

Tendons respond slowly compared with muscles. Many people notice pain settling within the first few weeks once compression is removed and isometric loading starts, while rebuilding full tolerance for running, hills and heavy hinging is usually a matter of months rather than weeks. Dr. Jared sets the progression on your response, not a calendar.

When to get medical care first

  • Sudden tearing pain at the sitting bone during a sprint or slip, with bruising down the thigh and weakness: possible tendon avulsion, see a physician promptly.
  • Numbness in the groin or inner thighs, or new loss of bladder or bowel control: go to the emergency room now.
  • Progressive weakness in the leg or a foot that drags or slaps when you walk.
  • Sitting-bone pain in a child or adolescent, which may involve a growth plate and needs imaging.
  • Buttock pain with fever, unexplained weight loss, night pain that does not change with position, or a history of cancer.

Frequently asked questions

Why does stretching my hamstring make it worse?

Pulling the leg toward your chest presses the tendon against the sitting bone. Tendons that are already irritated respond badly to that compression, which is why this condition often worsens with the exact stretch people assume will help.

Is this the same as a pulled hamstring?

No. A strain is a sudden tear in the muscle, usually with a clear moment and bruising. Tendinopathy builds gradually at the attachment on the bone and is driven by repeated load rather than one event.

Can I keep running?

Often yes, on flat ground and at a volume that does not leave the tendon sore the next morning. Hills and speed work are usually the first things to pause and the last things to return.

How do I know it is not sciatica?

Tendon pain stays at the sitting bone and hurts to press. Sciatica travels down the leg with burning, numbness or pins and needles. Dr. Jared tests both, because they can occur together.
