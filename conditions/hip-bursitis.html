Pain on the outside of the hip

The sorest spot is the bony bump on the side of the hip (the greater trochanter), which is tender to press. Lying on that side in bed is miserable, and many people switch to the other side only to find the top hip aches as it hangs forward. Getting up from a low chair, climbing stairs, standing on one leg to put on socks, and walking any real distance all bring it on. The pain may spread a hand's width down the outside of the thigh, but it rarely goes below the knee.

It tends to come on gradually in people over 40, more often women, after a change in activity: a new walking routine, a lot of stairs on vacation, taking up pickleball, or a stretch of standing with the weight parked on one hip. The modern term is greater trochanteric pain syndrome, which reflects that the gluteal tendons, not the bursa, are usually the irritated tissue.

The muscles that create outer hip pain

Several muscles attach at or pass over the trochanter, and each has a signature:

  • Gluteus medius holds the pelvis level when you stand on one leg. When it is weak and its tendon is overloaded, it hurts right at the bone and refers along the belt line and the top of the hip.
  • Gluteus minimus sits underneath it and refers pain down the outside of the thigh and leg, sometimes to the ankle, which is why outer hip pain is so often mistaken for sciatica.
  • Tensor fasciae latae (TFL) tightens up to compensate for weak glutes and pulls the IT band taut over the trochanter, compressing the tendons beneath it.
  • Vastus lateralis, the outer quad, refers down the outer thigh and makes side-lying painful.
  • Quadratus lumborum hikes the hip and refers to the point of the hip; when it is tight, the pelvis tilts and the gluteals on the other side work overtime.

Compression is the key idea. Crossed legs, standing on one hip, side-sleeping and aggressive outer-hip stretching all squeeze the tendons against the bone, and much of the early relief comes from reducing that squeeze.

What you can do today

  1. Sleep with a pillow between your knees if you lie on your side, and a pillow behind you so the top leg does not drop forward. If the sore side is down, a folded towel under the waist takes pressure off the hip.
  2. Uncross your legs and stand evenly. Sitting cross-legged and standing with the weight parked on one hip are the two postures that most compress the tendon.
  3. Stop stretching the outer hip for now. Pigeon pose and the cross-body IT band stretch push the tendon into the bone. Save them for later.
  4. Keep walking, shorter and flatter. Several short walks on level ground beat one long one with hills and stairs while the tendon is irritable.

When outer hip pain is something else

  • Hip joint arthritis usually hurts in the groin and front of the thigh, stiffens in the morning and limits turning the hip inward. See hip osteoarthritis.
  • Low back referral and sciatica send pain below the knee, often with tingling, and change with bending and sitting rather than with pressing the hip. See sciatica.
  • SI joint pain sits further back, near the dimples above the buttock. See SI joint pain.
  • A gluteal tendon tear, usually in older adults, causes marked weakness and a limp where the pelvis drops on every step.
  • Stress fracture of the femoral neck in runners or people with low bone density, which causes deep groin pain with walking and is urgent.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Gluteus medius: Along the belt line, over the SI joint, the buttock and the top of the hip.
  • Gluteus minimus: Outer hip down the side of the thigh and leg to the ankle, or down the back of the thigh and calf.
  • Tensor fasciae latae: Front-outer hip down the outside of the thigh, usually stopping at the knee.
  • Vastus lateralis: Down the outer thigh to the outside of the knee; can make lying on that side painful.
  • Quadratus lumborum: Low back and flank, over the SI joint and buttock, and the point of the hip.

When to get medical care first

  • Hip pain after a fall with inability to bear weight, a leg that looks shortened or turned out, which can mean a hip fracture and needs emergency care.
  • A hot, red, swollen hip with fever or chills, which can mean an infected bursa or joint.
  • Pain that is constant, severe at rest and worsening at night regardless of position, especially with a history of cancer or unexplained weight loss.
  • Numbness, tingling or true weakness traveling down the leg, or a new foot drop.
  • Loss of bowel or bladder control alongside hip or back pain, which is an emergency.

Frequently asked questions

Is hip bursitis the same as greater trochanteric pain syndrome?

They describe the same outer hip pain. The newer name reflects that the gluteal tendons and muscles are usually the irritated tissue, while the bursa is often a bystander.

Should I stretch my IT band for hip bursitis?

Usually not early on. Cross-body IT band and outer hip stretches compress the tendon against the bone. Strengthening the gluteus medius and releasing the TFL and outer thigh work better.

Will a cortisone shot fix it?

An injection may ease the pain for a while, which is a decision for you and your physician. It does not strengthen the tendon or change the habits that overloaded it, so we pair any injection with rehab.
