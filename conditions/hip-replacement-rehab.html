Your surgeon's precautions come first

Every total hip comes with a set of instructions that depend on the surgical approach. A posterior approach usually means avoiding deep hip bending past 90 degrees, crossing the legs and turning the knee inward for a set period. An anterior approach often has fewer restrictions but may limit extension and outward rotation early on. There are also rules about weight-bearing, driving and how long to use a walker or cane. Dr. Jared reviews these with you before any hands-on work or exercise and does not bend them. When this page and your surgeon disagree, follow the surgeon.

What the recovery usually feels like

The deep joint pain that made you decide on surgery is typically gone quickly. What lingers is different: a tired ache on the outside of the hip, a pull in the front of the thigh or groin, stiffness getting out of a chair or car, and a leg that feels longer or shorter than the other for a while. Many people notice the low back aching more than the hip in the first weeks, because the pelvis is finally moving differently.

Night discomfort lying on the operated side is common early. So is a lurching walk, the Trendelenburg pattern, where the trunk sways over the new hip because the side-hip muscles are weak.

Muscles that need attention after a hip replacement

In Dr. Jared's experience these are the usual suspects:

  • Gluteus medius and gluteus minimus hold the pelvis level on one leg. They were weak before surgery and are often cut or retracted during it. They refer pain along the belt line, over the outer hip and down the side of the leg, and their weakness is the limp.
  • Tensor fasciae latae takes over when the glutes are weak and produces a front-outer hip ache down the outside of the thigh.
  • Psoas and iliacus shortened from years of sitting and limping. They make it hard to stand up straight and cause groin and front-of-thigh pain, sometimes a snapping sensation over the front of the new joint.
  • Quadratus lumborum hikes the hip on the arthritic side for years; after surgery it stays short and drives flank and low-back pain.

The MD Fit approach to hip replacement rehab

Dr. Jared starts with a careful assessment: walking pattern, leg-length feel, pelvic control on one leg within your precautions, hip flexor length and where pressing on muscle reproduces your pain. Then release: hands-on soft-tissue work to the TFL, glutes, hip flexors and low back, staying well clear of the incision until it is healed and cleared. Correction focuses on standing tall and walking evenly, since a limp that becomes a habit is harder to undo later. Homework is assigned from the free MD Fit app, with short videos such as Standing Clamshell, Glute Walk and March Balance that fit the phase you are in. Finally, strengthening of the side-hip and gluteal muscles, calf and trunk so the new joint has the support it was missing for years.

Because Dr. Jared is a physician and clinical exercise physiologist, the loading is prescribed, tracked and adjusted rather than left to guesswork.

Simple things you can start today

  • Walk short distances often, with the walker or cane your surgeon prescribed, concentrating on equal step length and a tall trunk.
  • Lie flat on your back for a few minutes once or twice a day if your surgeon allows, to let the front of the hip lengthen.
  • Pump the ankles regularly and follow any blood-thinner or compression instructions exactly.
  • Use a pillow between the knees in bed if your precautions call for it.

When the pain is something else

Groin pain that persists well after surgery can involve the iliopsoas tendon rubbing on the implant, and a surgeon should evaluate it. Outer hip pain is more often trochanteric bursitis or gluteal tendon pain than the implant. Buttock pain running down the leg may be piriformis or sciatica, and low back and SI joint problems are frequent companions of a long-arthritic hip. Any sudden severe pain, a clunk, or the leg appearing suddenly shorter or rotated needs the surgeon urgently.

Life in Sarasota after a new hip

Getting back to golf, boating and long walks on the beach is realistic for most people, but stepping onto a boat, bending to tee up and sitting in a low golf cart are exactly the motions early precautions limit. Dr. Jared sequences these back in once your surgeon clears them, and many snowbirds schedule a block of visits around their season here.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Gluteus medius: Along the belt line, over the SI joint, the buttock and the top of the hip.
  • Gluteus minimus: Outer hip down the side of the thigh and leg to the ankle, or down the back of the thigh and calf.
  • Tensor fasciae latae: Front-outer hip down the outside of the thigh, usually stopping at the knee.
  • Psoas: Vertical band along the low back, plus the front of the thigh and groin.
  • Iliacus: Groin, front of the thigh, and the back of the pelvis high in the glute.
  • Quadratus lumborum: Low back and flank, over the SI joint and buttock, and the point of the hip.

How MD Fit treats hip replacement

  1. Assess. A hands-on evaluation of how you move and which muscles reproduce your pain.
  2. Release. Targeted soft-tissue work on the muscle at the source.

When to get medical care first

  • Calf pain, swelling, warmth or redness, or sudden chest pain or shortness of breath: a possible blood clot or pulmonary embolism, call 911 or go to the ER.
  • Sudden severe hip pain, a clunk or pop, the leg appearing shorter or turned, or inability to bear weight: possible dislocation, seek emergency care.
  • Fever, chills, or redness, warmth or drainage at the incision.
  • New numbness, tingling or weakness in the foot or lower leg.
  • A fall onto the operated hip, even without obvious injury.

Frequently asked questions

When should I start rehab after a hip replacement?

Your surgical team begins basic mobility in the hospital. Outpatient work with MD Fit starts when your surgeon clears it, and early visits stay strictly within your precautions.

Why does my low back hurt more than my hip now?

The spine and pelvis spent years compensating for a stiff hip. Once the joint moves freely, the quadratus lumborum and hip flexors that adapted to the old pattern often complain until they are released and retrained.

Will I always limp?

Most people walk evenly once the gluteus medius and minimus regain strength and the limp habit is retrained. That is a major focus of the strengthening phase.

Can I get soft-tissue work near the incision?

Only after it is fully healed and your surgeon has cleared it. Early on, hands-on work is directed at muscles away from the surgical site.
