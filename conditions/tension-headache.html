First, the headache that is never a muscle problem

Before anything else: the worst headache of your life, a headache that arrives suddenly like a clap of thunder, or a headache with fever, stiff neck, confusion, weakness, trouble speaking or vision loss is an emergency. Call 911. A new headache after a head injury, or a changed headache pattern over age 50, needs a physician promptly. MD Fit works with headaches that a physician has already recognized as muscular or tension-type.

The pattern of a tension headache

Most people describe pressure rather than throbbing. It sits on both sides, often like a band around the head or a cap pressing down. It can start at the base of the skull, settle over the temples, or gather behind the eyes. The neck and shoulders usually feel tight at the same time, and the pain tends to grow in the afternoon after hours at a screen or a stressful stretch. Light and sound are not usually a problem the way they are with migraine, and the pain rarely stops you from functioning, which is partly why people live with it for years.

Where the pressure actually comes from

A muscle under constant low-level load develops tender knots that refer pain somewhere else. In tension headache the usual sources are:

  • Upper trapezius: refers up the side of the neck, behind the ear and into the temple in a question-mark shape. The single most common headache muscle I treat.
  • Suboccipitals: a band from the base of the skull to behind the eye and forehead.
  • Sternocleidomastoid: forehead and eye pain, sometimes with dizziness or ear fullness.
  • Temporalis: temple pain and upper tooth ache from clenching.
  • Splenius and occipitalis: top-of-head and back-of-head ache with scalp tenderness.

Pressing on the right spot in the neck or shoulder and reproducing your exact headache is the clearest sign that the muscle is involved.

Treating the source instead of the symptom

At MD Fit I start by mapping your headache: where it starts, where it spreads, what time of day it arrives, and what your shoulders and jaw are doing when it does. Then I palpate the candidate muscles and see which one reproduces your pain. Hands-on release of that muscle and its neighbors is usually the first treatment, and many people feel the headache soften on the table.

Relief that lasts comes from the next steps. We correct the postures and habits that keep the trapezius and suboccipitals loaded, I give you a few minutes of daily homework in the free MD Fit app, and once the neck is calmer we strengthen the deep neck flexors and the lower trapezius so the upper traps are no longer carrying the head alone.

What helps between visits

  • Set a reminder every half hour to drop your shoulders away from your ears and take three slow belly breaths.
  • Raise your monitor so the top third of the screen is at eye level, and bring the phone up to your face instead of dropping your head to the phone.
  • Check your jaw during the day. Teeth apart, tongue resting on the roof of the mouth.
  • Warm shower or heat pack on the upper shoulders in the evening, followed by gentle neck stretches.

These are general suggestions. If anything increases your pain or brings on new symptoms, stop and speak with a physician.

Look-alikes worth knowing about

Migraine usually throbs, favors one side, and comes with nausea or sensitivity to light. A cervicogenic headache starts from the upper neck joints and is triggered by neck position. Jaw clenching produces a temple and jaw pattern. Sinus infection, eye strain, dehydration, medication overuse and high blood pressure can all cause head pain too. Many people have more than one type. Your physician sorts out the categories; I address the muscular contribution.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Upper trapezius: Up the side of the neck, behind the ear and into the temple (a question-mark shape).
  • Suboccipitals: A band of headache from the base of the skull wrapping to behind the eye and the forehead.
  • Sternocleidomastoid: Forehead, around and behind the eye, cheek, ear and top of the head.
  • Temporalis: Temple headache, above the eyebrow and into the upper teeth.
  • Splenius capitis & cervicis: Pain on the top of the head, and deep ache inside the head behind the eye.
  • Occipitalis: Ache at the back of the head that can shoot through the skull to behind the eye, with scalp tenderness.

When to get medical care first

  • The worst headache of your life or a headache that reaches full force in seconds: call 911.
  • Headache with fever, a stiff neck you cannot flex, confusion, weakness, trouble speaking or vision loss: call 911.
  • Headache that follows a head injury, especially with vomiting or drowsiness.
  • A new headache after age 50, or a clear change in a headache pattern you have had for years.
  • Headache that wakes you from sleep or worsens steadily over days or weeks.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if my headache is from muscles?

A strong clue is that pressing on a tender spot in the neck, shoulder or jaw reproduces the exact headache. Headaches that build with desk time or stress and ease with heat and movement also point to muscle.

Can massage help a tension headache?

Hands-on release of the upper trapezius, suboccipitals and SCM often eases the headache quickly. Lasting change comes from correcting posture, breathing and strength so the muscles stop re-tightening.

Do I need to stop taking my headache medication?

No. Keep following your physician's plan. Our work addresses the muscular drivers alongside whatever medical care you already have.

Is the free app enough on its own?

The app gives you the daily stretches and posture resets Dr. Jared prescribes. Many people do well with it alone; others need a few hands-on sessions to release muscles that will not let go with stretching.
