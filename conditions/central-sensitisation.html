The alarm, not the fire

Pain is not a reading of tissue damage. It is the output of a system that weighs incoming signals and decides how much danger to report. Most of the time that system is well calibrated and the two track each other closely.

When pain persists, the system can become more efficient at producing it. The nerves carrying the signal become easier to trigger, the threshold for reporting danger drops, and the processing further up amplifies what arrives. The practical result is more pain from less input.

A smoke alarm comparison gets most of the way there. A sensitive alarm going off at toast is not imagining the smoke and is not broken in a mysterious way. It is doing its job at a setting that no longer suits the kitchen. Nobody would call that smoke imaginary, and nobody should call this pain imaginary either.

What it looks like from the inside

Several experiences that feel strange or embarrassing to describe are standard features of a sensitised system, and recognising them usually comes as a relief.

  • Spread. The painful area is larger than the original problem, or has moved to the other side.
  • Things that should not hurt, hurting. A waistband, a bedsheet, a light touch, cold air.
  • Volume. A knock that would once have been nothing is now genuinely severe.
  • Delay. The cost of activity arrives a day or two later, which makes it very hard to link cause to effect.
  • Unpredictability. The same walk is fine on Tuesday and impossible on Friday, which other people read as inconsistency.
  • Travelling companions. Poor sleep, fatigue, fog and low mood, all of which feed back into sensitivity.

Why a clear scan does not mean nothing is wrong

Imaging shows structure. It does not show how sensitive the system reporting on that structure has become. A normal scan tells you there is no fracture, tumour or large disc problem, which is worth knowing. It tells you nothing about pain processing.

This is the point at which many people are told there is nothing wrong, hear nothing can be done, and stop looking. Both conclusions are wrong. Something is wrong, it is measurable in the sense that the behaviour is consistent and recognised, and sensitised systems do settle.

It also does not mean there is nothing mechanical left to find. Guarding, deconditioning and ordinary trigger points commonly sit on top of sensitisation and are treatable in their own right.

What turns the volume back down

Nothing turns a sensitised system down quickly, and anybody offering that should be treated with suspicion. What does help is unglamorous and reasonably well agreed on.

  1. Movement at a dose that does not trigger the alarm. This is the central one and the one most often got wrong. Done at the right size, regular movement gradually widens what the system accepts. Done too big, it confirms the threat and the threshold drops further. See pacing and flares.
  2. Understanding the mechanism. Knowing why pain is behaving this way genuinely reduces it, because threat is one of the inputs the system weighs. This is not positive thinking; it is removing a real amplifier.
  3. Sleep. Poor sleep lowers the pain threshold directly, which is why sensitisation and broken sleep reinforce each other.
  4. Treating what is still local. A muscle that reproduces your exact pain when pressed is a live input worth switching off.
  5. Time, without stopping. The common failure is a good run, overdoing it, a flare, then weeks of nothing.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Upper trapezius: Up the side of the neck, behind the ear and into the temple (a question-mark shape).
  • Quadratus lumborum: Low back and flank, over the SI joint and buttock, and the point of the hip.
  • Multifidus: Deep, pinpoint pain right next to the spine, the sacrum and sometimes the tailbone.
  • Gluteus medius: Along the belt line, over the SI joint, the buttock and the top of the hip.
  • Psoas: Vertical band along the low back, plus the front of the thigh and groin.
  • Scalenes: Chest, front and back of the upper arm, inner shoulder blade and down the forearm to the thumb and index finger.

When to get medical care first

  • New pain that is different in character from your usual pattern, which deserves assessment rather than being attributed to sensitisation.
  • Unexplained weight loss, fever, or night sweats alongside pain.
  • Progressive weakness, numbness, or loss of bladder or bowel control, which needs urgent medical assessment.
  • Pain that is constant, unrelated to position or activity, and wakes you every night.
  • A history of cancer with new persistent pain anywhere.
  • Thoughts of self-harm. Persistent pain and low mood travel together often, and that needs support rather than endurance.

Frequently asked questions

Are you saying the pain is in my head?

No. The pain is produced by a nervous system that has become more sensitive, which is a physical change in how signals are processed. It hurts exactly as much as it feels like it hurts.

Can a sensitised system go back to normal?

It can settle, often substantially, and usually slowly and unevenly rather than in a straight line. Nobody honest can promise a particular endpoint, which is different from saying nothing changes.

If movement helps, why does exercise keep making me worse?

Almost always because the dose was set for an ordinary body. The same exercise at a smaller starting size, held until it is genuinely easy before increasing, behaves completely differently.

Does this mean my original injury was never real?

Not at all. Sensitisation usually starts with a genuine injury or condition. What happens is that the alarm stays turned up after the original problem has settled, so the pain outlasts its cause.
