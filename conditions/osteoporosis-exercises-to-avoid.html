The short list: positions usually worth avoiding

These are the ones that come up consistently in bone care. They share a theme: they load the front of the vertebral bodies, which is where compression fractures happen.

  • Loaded end-range spinal flexion. Rounding the back hard while holding weight, or against resistance. Sit-ups, crunches, loaded toe-touches and machine abdominal crunches are the usual examples.
  • Forceful twisting under load. Rotating the trunk while holding something heavy, and particularly the combination of bending and twisting at once, as in swinging a bag out of a car boot across the body. Seated rotation machines fall into this.
  • Deep forward-bend stretching held at end range, including some familiar yoga and Pilates shapes. Both disciplines have a great deal to offer here; several specific positions are worth modifying.
  • High-impact work where loss is significant or a fracture has already happened. This one is a judgement call rather than a rule, and it depends entirely on your own report and history.

That is close to the whole list. Notice what is not on it.

The longer list: what people give up and should not

Fear does more damage here than most people realise, because the body interprets doing less as a reason to keep less bone and less muscle. These are frequently abandoned after a diagnosis and usually do not need to be:

  • Resistance training. Often the first thing stopped, and often the most important thing to keep. Bone needs meaningful load to adapt, and lifting is how that load is delivered safely and progressively.
  • Carrying things. Groceries, watering cans, grandchildren within reason. Loaded carries are an excellent and very ordinary form of bone loading.
  • Walking, hiking and stairs. Weight-bearing and worth doing, even though walking alone is usually not enough stimulus on its own.
  • Golf, tennis, pickleball and dancing. Frequently continued, sometimes with adjustments to how much end-range trunk rotation the swing demands.
  • Gardening. Usually a matter of changing how you get down to the ground, hinging at the hips rather than rounding the spine, rather than stopping.
  • Travel, grandchildren, ordinary life. The thing that most often follows a diagnosis is a shrinking world, and that has its own costs.

Why under-loading is a risk of its own

Bone responds to the forces placed on it. Reduce those forces and the signal to maintain bone reduces with them, which is why extended bed rest is hard on the skeleton and why an inactive year is not a neutral year.

There is a second, more immediate reason. Most fractures in this population follow a fall. Strength, balance and reaction time are what keep you upright, and all three decline with inactivity faster than bone density changes in either direction. Someone who stops moving to protect their bones frequently ends up at higher risk of the event that breaks one.

The aim, then, is not avoidance. It is substitution: replacing the few genuinely risky positions with loaded alternatives that achieve the same thing from a safer shape.

The substitutions that usually work

Nearly every cautioned movement has a version that keeps the benefit:

  • Instead of crunches, train the trunk in neutral: planks, dead bugs, carries, anti-rotation work.
  • Instead of a loaded forward bend, hinge at the hips with a neutral spine. This is a skill worth drilling, because you use it dozens of times a day.
  • Instead of a seated twist machine, train rotation from the hips and mid-back with the lumbar spine held steady.
  • Instead of touching the toes with straight legs, stretch the hamstrings lying down with the spine supported.

The muscles that make these substitutions possible are the back extensors, iliocostalis and longissimus, which hold the chest up; multifidus, which controls each segment; gluteus maximus, which supplies hip extension so the back does not; and the external oblique and abdominal wall, which brace the trunk in neutral rather than curling it.

Why this page cannot be specific to you

What is appropriate depends on things this page does not know: your T-scores at the hip and spine, whether you have already had a fracture, what medication you are on, and what your physician has advised. Someone with mild loss and no fracture history has far more latitude than someone with a previous vertebral fracture, and the plan should reflect that difference honestly rather than applying the most cautious version to everyone.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Iliocostalis: Low back and buttock from the lumbar part; the lower shoulder blade and even the belly from the thoracic part.
  • Longissimus: Low in the buttock and over the SI joint, often well below where the muscle actually is.
  • Multifidus: Deep, pinpoint pain right next to the spine, the sacrum and sometimes the tailbone.
  • Gluteus maximus: Buttock and the tailbone; aches with long sitting and walking uphill.
  • External oblique: Abdominal wall, the side of the belly and down into the groin; can feel like heartburn or a side stitch.
  • Quadratus lumborum: Low back and flank, over the SI joint and buttock, and the point of the hip.

What to expect

Confidence usually returns faster than anything measurable, often within the first few sessions once it is clear what is safe. Strength and balance improve over weeks. Bone itself adapts slowly, and that is tracked by your physician rather than felt.

When to get medical care first

  • Sudden, severe back pain after a minor strain, a cough or lifting something light, which can indicate a vertebral fracture and needs a physician promptly.
  • Any new pain after a fall, however minor the fall seemed at the time.
  • Losing height, or a forward curve in the upper back that is visibly increasing.
  • Back pain that is worse lying flat at night, or comes with fever or unexplained weight loss.
  • New numbness, leg weakness, or any change in bladder or bowel control: go to the emergency room.

Frequently asked questions

Can I still lift weights with osteoporosis?

For most people, appropriately dosed resistance training is encouraged rather than avoided, because bone needs meaningful load to adapt. What needs care is position and progression, which is what an assessment is for.

Is yoga safe?

Much of it usually is. The positions worth modifying are the ones that push into deep end-range spinal flexion or loaded twisting. A good instructor will adapt these if you tell them about your diagnosis.

Should I stop playing golf or tennis?

Usually not. Many people continue, sometimes with adjustments that reduce how much end-range trunk rotation the swing demands. Giving up activity entirely carries its own cost to bone, strength and balance.

Why does advice vary so much between sources?

Because appropriate caution depends on how much density has been lost and whether a fracture has already happened. General advice has to cover the most fragile case, which is why personalised guidance from your physician and a qualified exercise professional is more useful.
