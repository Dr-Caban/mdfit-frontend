The relationship runs both ways

Regular physical activity is commonly associated with better sleep for many people, and poor sleep makes training feel considerably harder — effort feels higher, coordination is worse, motivation drops and soreness tends to linger.

That two-way street matters practically. If you are sleeping badly, the answer is rarely to add more training volume on top; it is usually to stabilise the sleep and keep training consistent but moderate until it settles. Pushing harder through a period of poor sleep is how people end up injured or quitting.

I want to be careful here rather than overstate: exercise is not a treatment for insomnia or for a diagnosed sleep disorder, and I do not treat either. What I can do is build a plan that supports sleep rather than fighting it, and notice when something looks like it needs a physician.

Timing, and the thing people get wrong

The common belief is that evening exercise ruins sleep. For most people this is overstated, and finishing moderately intense work an hour or two before bed is usually fine. The bigger problems in practice are:

  • Hard, high-intensity sessions very late, which leave some people wired.
  • Caffeine taken before an evening session, which is far more likely to be the culprit than the exercise.
  • Phones and screens in the hour afterwards while winding down.
  • Training at a time you cannot sustain — the five a.m. plan that costs an hour of sleep most nights is usually a net loss.

If you only have evenings, train in the evening. Consistency beats theoretical optimisation almost every time.

Stress, recovery and reading your own week

Your body does not separate work stress, family stress and training stress into different accounts. They draw on the same recovery capacity. A fortnight that is brutal at work is a fortnight to hold training steady rather than add to it — not because training is bad for stress, but because the total load has a ceiling.

Useful signals that the total is too high:

  • Resting heart rate noticeably up for several mornings.
  • Sessions that normally feel fine feeling disproportionately hard.
  • Soreness taking longer to clear than usual.
  • Sleep worsening rather than improving with training.
  • Losing interest in sessions you normally look forward to.

When those appear, the correct move is usually to reduce intensity and keep frequency — stay in the habit, lower the demand. That is one of the main things a weekly follow-up is for: having someone look at your week and say “this is a week to hold, not push.”

Where the muscles come into it

Sleep and stress show up physically, and people often arrive describing the symptom rather than the cause. Jaw clenching through the night loads the masseter and temporalis, and is a common contributor to temple and jaw pain. Stress-driven shoulder posture keeps the upper trapezius and levator scapulae working all day, which is why the neck and shoulder are so often where it lands.

Hands-on work genuinely helps those muscles, which is part of what medical massage is for here. It addresses the symptom well. It does not address the sleep or the stress driving it, and I would rather say that plainly than sell you a course of massage as a solution to a problem it does not touch.

When it is not a training problem

Some things are not for an exercise physiologist to manage, and it matters to be clear about which.

Loud snoring with pauses in breathing, waking unrefreshed however long you sleep, or falling asleep during the day are reasons to speak to your physician about assessment for a sleep disorder. Persistent low mood, anxiety that is interfering with your life, or thoughts of harming yourself belong with your physician or a mental health professional, promptly — and exercise, whatever its general benefits, is not a substitute for that care.

What belongs here is the part where your training plan is built to fit the life you actually have, adjusted when that life gets harder, and designed so that the weeks you are stressed and sleeping badly are the weeks you keep the habit instead of losing it.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Masseter: Jaw, cheek, in front of the ear and the back teeth.
  • Temporalis: Temple headache, above the eyebrow and into the upper teeth.
  • Upper trapezius: Up the side of the neck, behind the ear and into the temple (a question-mark shape).
  • Levator scapulae: The angle where the neck meets the shoulder, down the inner edge of the shoulder blade.
  • Sternocleidomastoid: Forehead, around and behind the eye, cheek, ear and top of the head.

When to get medical care first

  • Loud snoring with pauses in breathing, or waking unrefreshed no matter how long you sleep: ask your physician about assessment for a sleep disorder.
  • Falling asleep during the day, particularly while driving: this needs medical attention rather than a training adjustment.
  • Persistent low mood, or anxiety that is interfering with daily life: please speak to your physician or a mental health professional.
  • Thoughts of harming yourself: contact your physician, a crisis line, or emergency services now. Exercise is not the answer to this and you should not wait.
  • New chest pain, palpitations or breathlessness waking you at night: seek medical care rather than assuming it is stress.

Frequently asked questions

Will exercising in the evening keep me awake?

For most people, moderately intense work finishing an hour or two before bed is fine. Very hard late sessions and pre-workout caffeine are far more likely culprits than the exercise itself.

Should I skip training when I've slept badly?

Usually not skip, but reduce. Keeping the habit at lower intensity tends to work better than either pushing through at full effort or abandoning the week.

Is exercise a treatment for insomnia?

No, and it is not presented as one here. Regular activity is commonly associated with better sleep for many people, but a persistent sleep problem should be assessed by your physician.

My jaw and shoulders are always tight. Is that stress?

Night-time jaw clenching and held shoulder posture are common physical expressions of it. Hands-on work helps those muscles considerably, though it does not address whatever is driving the clenching.
