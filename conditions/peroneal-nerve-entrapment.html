Read this before anything else

A foot that will not lift is a neurological sign, not a muscle strain. New or worsening weakness lifting the foot, a foot that slaps the ground, or tripping on the toe should be assessed by a physician without delay. Sudden weakness accompanied by severe back pain, numbness in the saddle area, or loss of bladder or bowel control is an emergency: seek emergency care immediately.

The reason for urgency is simple. The same symptom can come from the nerve at the knee, from a nerve root in the back, or from something affecting the nervous system more broadly, and the treatment for each is entirely different.

What people notice

  • The toe catching on carpet, kerbs or stairs, often described as tripping over nothing.
  • A slapping sound as the foot lands, because it cannot lower under control.
  • Numbness or tingling on the top of the foot and the front of the ankle, or along the outer calf.
  • Difficulty turning the foot outward, or an ankle that feels unreliable on uneven ground.
  • Tenderness at the outside of the knee just below the joint, where the nerve crosses the bone.

Sensation and strength often change together, but not always. Numbness on its own is less alarming than weakness, and weakness always takes priority.

Why the nerve gets compressed at the knee

The nerve is unusually exposed where it curves around the outside of the lower leg bone. Common contributors:

  • Habitual leg crossing, which presses the nerve against bone for long periods. This is one of the most frequent and most easily fixed causes.
  • Prolonged squatting, kneeling or sitting on the heels, including gardening and tiling work.
  • A tight plaster cast, brace, boot or compression stocking pressing at that exact spot.
  • Lying still for a long period, including after surgery or an illness, with the leg rolled outward.
  • Significant weight loss, which removes the small amount of padding over the nerve.
  • A knee injury, dislocation or surgery in that area, or a ganglion near the joint.

Tightness in the peroneals, biceps femoris and the outer calf can add tension along the nerve's path, and a weak tibialis anterior is usually the muscle whose failure people actually feel.

The back is the other possibility

A compressed L5 nerve root in the low back produces very similar weakness. Telling them apart matters:

  • At the knee, symptoms usually follow a position or pressure, there is often tenderness and a tingle when the spot is tapped, and back pain is typically absent.
  • From the back, there is often low back or buttock pain, symptoms may travel the length of the leg, and weakness can include turning the foot inward, which the peroneal nerve does not control.

Nerve conduction studies and imaging settle this. Sciatica-type pain and disc-related symptoms are covered separately.

Once a physician has established what is compressing the nerve and whether it needs intervention, there is real work to do. Nerve recovery is slow and I will not promise a timeline. What I can say is that protecting the nerve from further pressure is the part that matters most, and it is usually within your control.

Changes that protect the nerve now

  1. Stop crossing your legs. This alone resolves some cases and costs nothing to try.
  2. Check anything that presses just below the outside of the knee: boots, braces, compression garments, the edge of a chair.
  3. Avoid long periods squatting or kneeling until symptoms settle.
  4. If the foot is catching, use a brace rather than risking a fall. A trip on a kerb causes more damage than the nerve problem itself.

Sarasota note

Falls are the real risk here, and uneven pavers, kerbs and pool surrounds are everywhere. If the toe is catching, deal with the trip hazard first, before any rehabilitation plan. A fall in an older adult changes everything, and a temporary brace is a small price.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Peroneals: Outer ankle and outside of the foot; common after ankle sprains.
  • Tibialis anterior: Front of the shin, front of the ankle and the big toe.
  • Biceps femoris: Back of the knee on the outside and up the back of the thigh.
  • Gastrocnemius: Upper calf, back of the knee and the arch of the foot; deep cramp-like tightness.
  • Soleus: Heel and Achilles tendon, lower calf; can contribute to plantar fasciitis.

When to get medical care first

  • New or worsening weakness lifting the foot, a foot that slaps the ground, or tripping on the toe: see a physician without delay.
  • Sudden leg weakness with severe back pain, numbness in the saddle area, or loss of bladder or bowel control: seek emergency care immediately.
  • Weakness in both legs, or weakness that is spreading upward.
  • Numbness or weakness that began after a knee injury, dislocation or surgery that has not been assessed.
  • Calf pain with swelling, warmth or breathlessness: seek urgent care, as a blood clot must be excluded.

Frequently asked questions

Can crossing my legs really cause foot drop?

Sustained pressure on the nerve at the outside of the knee is a recognised cause, and leg crossing is one of the common ways it happens. It is also one of the easiest contributors to remove.

Is this the same as sciatica?

Not usually. Sciatica-type symptoms come from a nerve root in the low back and often include back or buttock pain travelling down the leg. Compression at the knee is usually local, positional, and without back pain.

Should I wear a brace?

If your foot is catching, a brace reduces the risk of a fall while the nerve recovers. That is a safety decision worth making early rather than late, and your physician can advise on the right type.

How long does the nerve take to recover?

Nerve recovery varies widely depending on the cause and how long the pressure was present. Dr. Jared will not give you an invented timeline; the honest answer comes from your physician and from how you respond over the first weeks.
