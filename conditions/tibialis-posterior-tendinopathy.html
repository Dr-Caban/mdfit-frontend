How it starts and what you notice

The first complaint is usually an ache along the inside of the ankle and arch, just behind and below the bony knob on the inner ankle. It is sore after walking, worse on uneven ground, and often mistaken for a simple strain that will pass.

  • Pain and sometimes swelling tracking behind the inner ankle bone and forward into the arch.
  • Tiredness or aching in the arch after standing or walking, particularly later in the day.
  • Difficulty rising onto the toes on that one leg, or a heel that does not turn inward as you rise.
  • Shoes wearing down unevenly on the inner edge, or an arch that looks lower than the other side.
  • Pain that moves to the outside of the ankle later on, as the heel drifts outward and bones begin to pinch.

A useful self-check is the single heel raise. Stand near a wall for balance and rise onto the toes of the painful foot alone. If you cannot lift the heel, or it lifts without the heel rolling inward, that tendon is not doing its job.

Why this one is different from plantar fasciitis

Both produce arch pain and both hurt more with standing, so they are regularly confused. The locations and the pattern differ.

  • Where it hurts. Plantar fasciitis is centred under the heel on the sole, right where the fascia attaches. Tibialis posterior pain tracks behind the inner ankle bone and along the inner arch, above the sole rather than under it.
  • First steps in the morning. Plantar fascia pain is classically sharpest on the first few steps out of bed and eases as you move. Tendon pain here tends to build through the day with use.
  • Shape change. Plantar fasciitis does not flatten the arch. A failing posterior tibial tendon does, progressively, and that is the reason it deserves early attention.
  • The heel raise. Most people with plantar fasciitis can rise onto one toe, uncomfortably. Many with a significant tendon problem cannot.

They can coexist, particularly in a foot that has been flattening for a while, because the fascia is then taking strain the tendon used to handle.

The muscles that share the load

  • Tibialis posterior itself is the arch's main active support. Trigger points in it refer pain along the Achilles and into the sole.
  • Soleus and gastrocnemius matter enormously. A tight calf stops the ankle bending forward, so the foot compensates by collapsing through the arch instead, loading the tendon on every step.
  • Peroneals on the outside of the leg are the opposing group; they often become overactive as the foot drifts outward.
  • The small muscles of the foot are the arch's second line of support and are usually weak in a foot that has been flattening.

Calf tightness is the finding Dr. Jared sees most consistently, and addressing it takes a surprising amount of strain off the tendon.

What you can do today

  1. Change your shoes. A supportive shoe with a firm heel counter, worn indoors as well as out, takes immediate strain off the tendon. Avoid flat, unsupportive sandals and going barefoot on hard floors.
  2. Shorten your walks for now and break them into several short ones rather than one long one.
  3. Work on calf length twice a day with the knee straight and again with the knee bent, so both calf muscles are addressed.
  4. Avoid the sand. Walking on a soft, sloping beach is one of the harder surfaces for this tendon.

Other causes of inner ankle pain

Tarsal tunnel compression produces burning or tingling into the sole rather than a mechanical ache, often worse at night. An ankle sprain on the inner side involves the deltoid ligament and follows an injury. A stress fracture of the inner shin or the navicular bone gives pinpoint bone tenderness and needs imaging, particularly in runners. Shin splints run higher up the inner shin. Inflammatory arthritis can affect this tendon and belongs with a physician.

Why this matters in Sarasota

Flat, sandy beaches, flip-flops for much of the year and a large population of walkers over fifty make this common here. The combination of an unsupportive shoe and soft, uneven sand asks a great deal of a tendon that is already struggling. Catching it while you can still do a single heel raise is the difference between a straightforward rehabilitation and a longer road.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Tibialis posterior: Inner ankle and arch, along the inner shin; common with flat feet.
  • Soleus: Heel and Achilles tendon, lower calf; can contribute to plantar fasciitis.
  • Gastrocnemius: Upper calf, back of the knee and the arch of the foot; deep cramp-like tightness.
  • Peroneals: Outer ankle and outside of the foot; common after ankle sprains.
  • Foot muscles: Heel and sole of the foot; first-step-in-the-morning pain like plantar fasciitis.

What to expect

Early-stage tendon irritation often settles over a number of weeks once footwear changes and calf length improve. A tendon that has already allowed the arch to drop takes considerably longer and may need bracing or a specialist's input. Dr. Jared reassesses the single heel raise as the practical marker of progress.

When to get medical care first

  • You cannot rise onto the toes of that foot at all, or the arch has visibly collapsed: see a physician or foot specialist promptly.
  • Pinpoint tenderness over bone with pain that continues at rest, which may indicate a stress fracture.
  • Burning, numbness or tingling spreading into the sole of the foot, particularly at night.
  • A hot, red, swollen ankle with fever or feeling unwell.
  • Sudden inability to walk after a snap or pop at the inner ankle.

Frequently asked questions

Will arch supports fix this?

Support takes strain off the tendon and is often part of early care, but it does not strengthen anything. Support plus progressive loading of the tendon and the calf is what changes the outcome over time.

Is a flat foot always a problem?

No. Many people have low arches their whole lives with no pain at all. What matters is a foot that is changing shape, becoming painful, or losing the ability to do a single heel raise.

Can I keep walking for exercise?

Usually yes, in supportive shoes, on flat even ground, in shorter sessions. Soft sand, hills and long distances are the ones to pause while the tendon settles.

Why does my calf matter for a foot tendon?

A tight calf limits how far the ankle can bend forward during a step, so the foot rolls inward through the arch to compensate. That compensation lands directly on this tendon, thousands of times a day.
