The problem with the last session

Formal cardiac rehab is a good programme. It is also a finite one. When it ends, people usually land in one of three places:

  • They join a regular gym, find nobody there understands their history, and quietly stop going.
  • They keep doing exactly what they did in rehab, at the same intensity, for years — safe, but no longer progressing.
  • They do nothing, lose what they gained over the following months, and feel it.

None of those is the intended outcome. The intent is that you keep going, with the dose continuing to move. That requires someone who can read your history and adjust it, which is what a Clinical Exercise Physiologist is trained for.

There is also a group who were never offered formal rehab at all — because of where they were treated, insurance, timing, or because their event was managed in a way that did not trigger a referral. The same applies to them.

This starts with your cardiologist, not with me

Being explicit, because this is the part that matters most: this service is for people who are medically stable and have been cleared by their cardiologist to exercise. It is not a substitute for cardiac rehab, not a substitute for cardiology care, and not somewhere to come instead of being properly assessed after a cardiac event.

What I need from you at the evaluation is your diagnosis, your medication list, your discharge summary or rehab summary if you have one, and any limits your cardiologist has set — a heart rate ceiling, a weight restriction, sternal precautions after surgery, anything they told you to avoid. Those limits are the frame the plan is built inside, not suggestions to work around.

With your permission I write to your cardiologist after the evaluation and whenever the plan changes meaningfully. I do not manage your medication, your devices or your cardiac care. I manage the exercise.

What continuing looks like in practice

Rehab usually leaves people with a walking or cycling habit and modest strength work. The next phase is about carefully extending both, in ways your history allows.

  • Aerobic progression that moves beyond the fixed rehab dose — longer, or slightly harder, but changing one variable at a time so a reaction is traceable.
  • Resistance training built around controlled breathing. The caution after a cardiac event is straining and breath-holding, not weights as such, and getting the breathing automatic is most of the work early on.
  • Sternal precautions respected in full if you had open surgery, with the upper body progressed only on the timeline your surgeon set.
  • Everyday capacity — stairs, carrying shopping, getting off the floor, walking the course. Most people care far more about these than about any number.

The large muscle groups do the useful work here: gluteus maximus, the quadriceps, latissimus dorsi and calves.

Intensity, symptoms and knowing when to stop

Much of the confidence people lose after a cardiac event is about not knowing which sensations are normal. Part of the job is handing that judgement back to you.

We go through how to set intensity in a way that works for your medication — several cardiac drugs blunt the heart rate response, so a heart rate target alone can be misleading. We go through what effort is meant to feel like, what breathlessness is expected versus concerning, and the specific symptoms that mean stop today and call someone.

You also get a written plan that your cardiologist can read, so everyone is working from the same document rather than from your recollection of what I said.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Gluteus maximus: Buttock and the tailbone; aches with long sitting and walking uphill.
  • Vastus lateralis: Down the outer thigh to the outside of the knee; can make lying on that side painful.
  • Latissimus dorsi: Bottom tip of the shoulder blade and down the back of the arm to the ring and little fingers.
  • Gastrocnemius: Upper calf, back of the knee and the arch of the foot; deep cramp-like tightness.
  • Pectoralis major upper head: Front of the shoulder over the front delt, often mistaken for a front-shoulder joint problem.

When to get medical care first

  • Chest pain, pressure, tightness or discomfort spreading to the jaw, neck, back or arm: stop and call 911.
  • Severe or unusual shortness of breath, fainting, or a feeling that you are about to pass out: stop and seek emergency care.
  • A racing, pounding or irregular heartbeat that is new for you, or that does not settle with rest.
  • New or worsening swelling in the ankles or legs, or breathlessness when lying flat.
  • Any symptom your cardiologist specifically told you to watch for: their instruction takes priority over anything in your exercise plan.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need my cardiologist's clearance first?

Yes. This service is for people who are medically stable and cleared to exercise. Bring any limits they have set and they will be built into the plan.

Is this the same as cardiac rehab?

No. Formal cardiac rehab is a monitored medical programme. This is exercise care for afterwards, for people who are stable and cleared, and it is not a replacement for rehab if you have been offered it.

I had bypass surgery. When can I start upper body work?

On the timeline your surgeon set. Sternal precautions are respected in full, and the upper body is progressed only once they have lifted them.

My heart rate does not go up much on my medication. How do we set intensity?

Several cardiac medications blunt the heart rate response, so a heart rate target can be misleading. We use methods that still work for you, and we go through this at the evaluation.
