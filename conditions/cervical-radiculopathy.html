Cervical Radiculopathy, a Pinched Nerve in the Neck, Sarasota

Cervical radiculopathy is irritation of a nerve root where it leaves the spine in the neck. People usually call it a pinched nerve. The pain and tingling travel into the shoulder, arm or hand along a path that follows that particular nerve. It is a physician's diagnosis; this page is about the pattern, what conservative care involves, and when it needs more than that.

What the pattern looks like

  • Pain that travels from the neck into the shoulder blade, down the arm, and often into specific fingers.
  • Tingling or numbness in a band rather than the whole arm, following one nerve's territory.
  • Weakness in a specific movement, such as lifting the arm, straightening the elbow or gripping.
  • Pain often eased by putting the hand on top of the head, which takes tension off the nerve root.
  • Worse with extending the neck, turning toward the painful side, or coughing and sneezing.
  • Deep ache around the inner edge of the shoulder blade, which is a classic referral and often the first symptom.

Arm symptoms that follow a line and come with a specific weakness are different from the diffuse ache of a muscular problem, and that distinction drives everything that follows.

How this relates to a herniated disc

These two terms describe different parts of the same story, and the site covers both.

  • Herniated disc describes the structure: disc material that has moved out of place. A disc can bulge without causing any symptoms at all, which is common on imaging.
  • Cervical radiculopathy describes the symptom: a nerve root being irritated, and the arm pain, tingling or weakness that results.

A disc is one way to irritate a nerve root. Arthritic narrowing of the opening the nerve passes through is another, and it becomes more common with age. The same arm symptoms can arise from either, which is why imaging alone does not decide treatment.

What else can mimic it

Arm symptoms have several sources, and more than one can be present.

  • Thoracic outlet symptoms involve the whole arm, feel heavy, and change with arm position rather than neck position.
  • Cubital tunnel affects only the ring and little fingers and is provoked by a bent elbow.
  • Carpal tunnel affects the thumb, index and middle fingers, is worst at night, and is relieved by shaking the hand.
  • Shoulder problems such as a cuff tear refer down the arm but rarely past the elbow and do not numb the fingers.
  • Trigger points in the scalenes, levator scapulae and rhomboids produce convincing arm and shoulder blade pain without any nerve involvement at all.

Aggressive stretching and strong traction on an irritable nerve root tends to make things worse, so early work is gentle and guided by what happens to the arm symptoms, not by what happens to the neck.

Early measures that usually help

  1. Find the position that reduces the arm symptoms and use it often, including resting the hand on your head if that helps.
  2. Raise the screen to eye level and stop looking down at a phone for long stretches.
  3. Support the arm on a pillow when sitting, which takes load off the neck.
  4. Judge progress by whether symptoms are retreating toward the neck rather than by whether the neck hurts less. Symptoms moving out of the hand and back up the arm is the direction you want.

Sarasota note

Long drives, laptop work and a few hours of pickleball with the head extended all show up in the history. So does sleeping propped up on several pillows. If the arm wakes you at night, mention it, because night symptoms often respond to a change in sleeping position faster than anything else.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Scalenes: Chest, front and back of the upper arm, inner shoulder blade and down the forearm to the thumb and index finger.
  • Levator scapulae: The angle where the neck meets the shoulder, down the inner edge of the shoulder blade.
  • Upper trapezius: Up the side of the neck, behind the ear and into the temple (a question-mark shape).
  • Splenius capitis & cervicis: Pain on the top of the head, and deep ache inside the head behind the eye.
  • Rhomboid major: Between the shoulder blade and the spine.
  • Sternocleidomastoid: Forehead, around and behind the eye, cheek, ear and top of the head.

When to get medical care first

  • Weakness that is getting worse, muscle wasting in the arm or hand, or dropping objects: see a physician promptly.
  • Clumsy hands, difficulty with buttons or handwriting, or unsteadiness walking, which can indicate pressure on the spinal cord itself: seek urgent assessment.
  • Loss of bladder or bowel control alongside neck or arm symptoms: seek emergency care immediately.
  • Neck pain with fever, unexplained weight loss, or a history of cancer.
  • Arm symptoms that began after a fall or car accident that has not been assessed.

Frequently asked questions

Is this the same as a herniated disc?

They are related but not identical. A herniated disc describes the structure; cervical radiculopathy describes the nerve root irritation and the arm symptoms. Arthritic narrowing can cause the same symptoms without a disc herniation.

Why does putting my hand on my head help?

Raising the arm overhead reduces tension on the nerve root, so symptoms often ease. It is a recognised feature and a useful clue, though a physician confirms the cause.

Should I be stretching my neck hard?

No. An irritable nerve root usually reacts badly to aggressive stretching or strong traction. Gentle movement guided by what happens to the arm symptoms is the safer approach.

Will I need surgery?

Many people improve without it. Surgery is usually considered when weakness is progressive, when there are signs of pressure on the spinal cord, or when symptoms persist despite a genuine course of conservative care.
