Where it hurts narrows it down

  • Front, around or under the kneecap. Worse on stairs, hills and after long sitting. Usually how the kneecap tracks, driven by the vastus medialis, vastus lateralis and hip control. See kneecap pain.
  • Inside of the knee. Medial meniscus, the inner ligament, or referral from VMO and the adductors.
  • Outside of the knee. Often IT band, and the cause is usually hip, not knee.
  • Behind the knee. Popliteus, the hamstring attachments, calf origins, or a Baker's cyst.
  • Below the kneecap, on the tendon. Tendon loading. See patellar tendinopathy.

Why the hip usually matters more than the knee

Watch someone with kneecap pain do a single-leg squat and the knee typically drifts inward. That is not a knee fault. It is the gluteus medius failing to hold the thigh bone steady, so the femur rotates in under a kneecap that then tracks badly.

Below, a stiff ankle or tight calf stops the shin travelling forward in a squat or on stairs, and the knee takes the compensation. This is why a programme built only of quad exercises often stalls.

What the evaluation looks at

Dr. Jared tests knee range and ligament stability, then looks above and below: hip strength and rotation, ankle dorsiflexion, how you load the leg on a step and a squat. He palpates the quadriceps, adductors, calf and hamstring attachments to see which reproduce your pain.

Imaging reports are read in context. Meniscal and arthritic changes appear on scans of plenty of comfortable knees, so the exam matters more than the report for deciding what to treat.

How MD Fit treats knee pain

Soft-tissue work on the quadriceps, IT band attachments, adductors and calf usually changes how the knee feels in the first session. That window is used for correction: restoring ankle dorsiflexion, teaching the knee to track over the foot, and loading the hip so the thigh bone stays where it should.

Strength work then rebuilds the quadriceps, glutes and calf through the ranges you actually use, which for most people means stairs, getting out of a chair, walking distance and sport.

Post-surgical knees follow your surgeon's protocol and precautions. See knee replacement rehab and ACL reconstruction recovery.

Arthritic knees still respond to strength

A report saying arthritis is not a verdict on what you can do. Cartilage change is extremely common with age and correlates poorly with how much a knee hurts. Plenty of people have significant changes on imaging and walk comfortably, and plenty have mild changes and hurt a great deal.

What reliably helps is strength around the joint and keeping the knee moving through its range. Quadriceps strength in particular tracks closely with function in arthritic knees. Loading is generally good for joint tissue, provided it is built up sensibly rather than jumped into.

If a knee eventually needs replacing, going into surgery with a stronger leg makes the recovery afterward shorter and more predictable. See knee osteoarthritis.

What you can do this week

  1. Test your ankle: kneel with one foot forward and push the knee over the toes without the heel lifting. If one side is clearly stiffer, that side is loading the knee differently.
  2. On stairs, lead down with the painful leg under control rather than dropping onto it.
  3. Keep the quadriceps working daily, even isometrically, because a knee that hurts loses quad strength quickly.
  4. Walk on flat ground rather than hills while it settles.

Knees in an active Sarasota population

Pickleball asks for sudden lateral movement and stopping, golf asks for rotation under load, and plenty of people here do both several times a week. The knees take it well when the hips and ankles share the work, and badly when they do not. Rehab here is built around returning to the sport, not around avoiding it.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Vastus medialis: Inner knee and inside of the kneecap; can make the knee feel like it buckles.
  • Vastus lateralis: Down the outer thigh to the outside of the knee; can make lying on that side painful.
  • Rectus femoris: A deep ache around and behind the kneecap, and the front of the hip bone.
  • Gluteus medius: Along the belt line, over the SI joint, the buttock and the top of the hip.
  • Gastrocnemius: Upper calf, back of the knee and the arch of the foot; deep cramp-like tightness.
  • Popliteus: Back of the knee; worse crouching, walking downhill or running.

When to get medical care first

  • A knee that locks and will not straighten, which may be a displaced meniscal fragment and belongs with a surgeon promptly.
  • Sudden swelling within an hour of an injury, which suggests bleeding in the joint.
  • A knee that gives way repeatedly or feels unstable under normal walking.
  • A hot, red, swollen knee with fever: this needs urgent medical assessment.
  • Inability to bear weight after an injury, or clear deformity.
  • Calf swelling, redness or tenderness with new pain behind the knee, which needs same-day medical assessment.

Frequently asked questions

Do you help after knee replacement?

Yes. Rehab rebuilds range of motion, strength and confidence on stairs, walking and daily activities, working within your surgeon's protocol.

Can I avoid surgery?

Some knee pain improves fully with the right strengthening. Your evaluation tells you what to expect, and surgical questions stay with your surgeon.

What should I bring?

Comfortable clothes you can move in, and any surgical notes or imaging reports you have.

My scan showed arthritis. Is exercise still worth it?

Usually yes. Strength around an arthritic knee often improves pain and function, and loading is generally good for joint tissue rather than harmful.
