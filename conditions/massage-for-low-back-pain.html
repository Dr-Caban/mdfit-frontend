The usual muscular suspects

When testing reproduces your familiar back pain, it is most often from one of these:

  • The quadratus lumborum, between the last rib and the pelvis, which refers along the belt line and into the upper buttock and is a frequent source of the "stuck" feeling on standing up.
  • The multifidus, deep beside the spine, producing pinpoint pain right next to the vertebrae.
  • The gluteus medius and gluteus minimus, which refer into the buttock and down the leg convincingly enough to be mistaken for nerve pain.
  • The psoas and iliacus, hip flexors that tilt the pelvis and are often implicated in backs that hurt after long sitting.
  • The iliocostalis and longissimus of the spinal group, which refer up and down the back and into the flank.

What a session realistically changes

Honest expectations make this work better. What soft-tissue work can reasonably do is reduce the guarding that makes movement feel unsafe, restore some range so bending and turning are less restricted, and give short-term relief that creates a window for movement.

What it does not do is realign anything, lengthen a muscle permanently, or resolve a disc problem. If your back pain is being driven by hours of sitting, a job that involves repeated lifting, or hips that do not share the load, the muscles will tighten again because they are responding to a demand that has not changed.

That is why the most useful part of a back session is often the last ten minutes: the movements and homework that get the area loaded again. Rest beyond a day or two tends to make back pain worse, not better.

Pain down the leg

Leg pain changes the picture and deserves care. Muscles can refer down the leg: the gluteus minimus in particular produces a convincing band of pain down the outside of the thigh and calf. So can nerve irritation, which is a different problem with a different plan, covered on the sciatica page.

The practical distinction used during testing is whether pressing a muscle reproduces the leg symptom, and whether there is numbness or weakness rather than pain alone. Numbness, weakness, or a foot that drags should be evaluated by a physician rather than massaged.

Making it last

Backs that stay better tend to belong to people who did three things: kept moving in the first days, built hip and trunk capacity over the following weeks, and changed something about the daily load that was producing the problem.

Hands-on work fits at the start of that, when guarding and pain make movement hard to begin. The low back pain page covers the wider plan, the SI joint page covers pain that sits lower and to one side, and strength work is what usually decides whether you are back here next season.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Quadratus lumborum: Low back and flank, over the SI joint and buttock, and the point of the hip.
  • Multifidus: Deep, pinpoint pain right next to the spine, the sacrum and sometimes the tailbone.
  • Gluteus medius: Along the belt line, over the SI joint, the buttock and the top of the hip.
  • Psoas: Vertical band along the low back, plus the front of the thigh and groin.
  • Iliocostalis: Low back and buttock from the lumbar part; the lower shoulder blade and even the belly from the thoracic part.
  • Gluteus minimus: Outer hip down the side of the thigh and leg to the ankle, or down the back of the thigh and calf.

  2. Release. Targeted soft-tissue work on the muscle at the source.

What to expect

Many people feel freer at the end of a session and for a day or two after. Lasting change usually tracks with the strength and daily-load work over several weeks rather than with how many massages were booked.

When to get medical care first

  • New loss of bladder or bowel control, or numbness around the groin, inner thighs or genitals: go to the emergency department now.
  • Progressive leg weakness, a foot that drags, or increasing falls.
  • Back pain with fever, chills or unexplained weight loss.
  • Back pain after a significant fall or accident, before a fracture has been ruled out.
  • Night pain that consistently wakes you and is not eased by changing position.
  • A history of cancer or osteoporosis with new back pain, which should be reviewed by a physician first.

Frequently asked questions

Will massage fix a disc problem?

No. Soft-tissue work can reduce the muscle guarding that accompanies a disc issue and make movement more tolerable, but it does not change the disc itself. Numbness or weakness in the leg needs medical assessment.

Should I rest my back instead?

Beyond a day or two, prolonged rest tends to make back pain worse. Gentle movement is usually part of the plan, and reducing guarding is one reason hands-on work is useful early.

My back pain goes into my leg. Is massage still appropriate?

Sometimes, because several hip muscles refer down the leg. If there is numbness, weakness or a dragging foot, an evaluation comes first.

How many sessions will I need?

That depends on how long it has been going on and what is driving it. If nothing has changed after a fair trial, the answer is to reassess rather than keep booking.
