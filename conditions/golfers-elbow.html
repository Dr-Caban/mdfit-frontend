Recognising it

The pain sits on the bony bump on the inner side of the elbow, and is reproduced by gripping, by bending the wrist against resistance, and often by a firm handshake. It can run down the inside of the forearm. Mornings are frequently the worst part of the day.

The muscles involved are the wrist flexors and finger flexors, plus the pronators that turn the palm down. In golf it is typically the trailing arm; in the gym it is usually heavy pulling or deadlifting; in daily life it is tools, gardening and carrying.

What else can produce the same pain

Two things sit close by and matter:

  • The ulnar nerve runs behind the inner elbow in a shallow groove. If your symptoms include numbness or tingling in the ring and little fingers, that is nerve rather than tendon and the plan changes.
  • The inner elbow ligament can be strained by throwing or heavy valgus load. Pain with throwing and a feeling of instability points there, and needs assessment before loading.

Referral from the neck and from pectoralis minor can also reach the inner arm, which is why the shoulder and neck get screened even when the elbow is what hurts.

Why the shoulder keeps turning up

Grip is stabilised from the shoulder blade. When the scapular muscles are weak, the forearm compensates on every grip, and a tendon that was coping becomes a tendon that is not. This is consistently why isolated forearm treatment helps for a few weeks and then stops helping.

In golf specifically, a swing that relies on the arms rather than rotation through the hips and trunk loads the forearms heavily. Addressing that is often what stops the recurrence after the tendon settles.

How MD Fit treats it

Hands-on release of the flexor mass and pronators usually eases the grip pain in the first session, and the ulnar nerve is screened at the same time. Correction looks at grip size, how you carry and lift, and shoulder blade strength.

The tendon work itself is graded loading: isometric holds first when gripping is painful, then slow controlled work through range with the lowering phase emphasised, building load over weeks. Some discomfort during the work is fine; pain that is worse the next morning means the dose was too high. Daily routines come through the free MD Fit app.

What you can do this week

  1. Carry with the palm up rather than down, and use both hands where you can.
  2. Check grip sizes on clubs and tools. Too thin a grip makes you squeeze harder for the same control.
  3. Keep using the arm within comfort. Complete rest lowers tendon capacity.
  4. Lower pulling volume in the gym temporarily rather than stopping training.

Playing through it

Most people keep playing with adjustments. That usually means fewer range sessions, avoiding mats and hard ground, taking less turf, and giving the tendon a day between rounds while the loading programme runs. Dr. Jared sets weekly limits so you can see what is allowed rather than guessing.

For the swing mechanics behind it, see golf. The relationship between the hips, trunk and arms in a swing is usually where the lasting answer sits.

Why tendons take the time they take

Tendon is slow tissue with limited blood supply, and the changes that make it painful develop over months. Rebuilding capacity happens on a similar timescale. The first couple of weeks of a loading programme rarely feel like much is happening, which is exactly when people abandon it.

The practical measure of progress is not whether it hurts today but whether your tolerance is climbing: more grip, more holes, more load, before symptoms appear. That trend is what your weekly check-ins track.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Wrist flexors: Inside of the elbow (golfer's elbow) and the palm side of the wrist.
  • Finger flexors: Palm and the front of the fingers, sometimes a 'trigger finger' catching feeling.
  • Pectoralis minor: Front of the shoulder and chest, down the inner arm to the last three fingers.
  • Triceps medial head: The point of the elbow and the inner elbow, a golfer's-elbow look-alike, sometimes the ring and little fingers.
  • Brachioradialis: Outside of the elbow and the web of the thumb.
  • Scalenes: Chest, front and back of the upper arm, inner shoulder blade and down the forearm to the thumb and index finger.

When to get medical care first

  • Numbness or tingling in the ring and little fingers, or weakness in the hand, which suggests nerve involvement rather than tendon.
  • Elbow pain after a fall, with swelling or inability to straighten the arm, which may be a fracture.
  • A hot, red, swollen elbow with fever, which needs urgent medical assessment.
  • A sudden pop with immediate loss of strength, which may be a tendon rupture.
  • A feeling that the elbow is unstable or gives way with throwing or lifting.

Frequently asked questions

Does it affect my golf swing?

It can, but most people keep playing while the tendon rebuilds. Dr. Jared gives you clear limits each week so you know what is reasonable.

Is this different from tennis elbow?

Same idea, different side. Tennis elbow is outside the elbow; golfer's elbow is inside. The treatment principles are similar.

Do I need imaging?

Rarely at the start. If the pain is not improving, or if there are red flags, imaging is arranged through your doctor.

My little finger tingles. Is that part of it?

That points to the ulnar nerve rather than the tendon, and it changes the plan. Mention it at your evaluation so it is tested properly.
