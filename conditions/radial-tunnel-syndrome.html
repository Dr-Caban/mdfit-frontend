How it differs from tennis elbow

This is the distinction that matters, and it comes down to where the tenderness sits and what the pain feels like.

  • Tennis elbow is tender right on the bony bump at the outside of the elbow. The pain is sharp with gripping, lifting a kettle or a backhand, and it is usually worst during activity. Pressing that bony point reproduces it precisely.
  • Radial tunnel is tender three or four finger-widths further down the forearm, in the muscle belly below the elbow rather than on the bone. The pain is a deep, dull ache rather than a sharp catch, it often troubles people at night and at rest, and it tends to follow sustained forearm rotation, such as using a screwdriver or wringing out a cloth.

A useful marker: resisting the middle finger straightening, with the elbow out straight, often provokes radial tunnel pain while leaving true tennis elbow unchanged. Resisted turning of the palm upward against resistance does the same. These are assessment findings, not self-tests to push hard at home.

The practical consequence is that treatment aimed at the tendon on the bone does nothing for a nerve being squeezed an inch further down, which is why the elbow that will not settle is the classic presentation.

What people describe

  • A deep ache in the upper forearm, below the outside of the elbow, that is hard to point to precisely.
  • Night ache that disturbs sleep, which is unusual for a straightforward tendon problem.
  • Pain with twisting actions rather than with pure gripping: jar lids, door handles, screwdrivers, a tennis serve.
  • A sense of weakness or fatigue in the forearm, usually from pain rather than true nerve weakness.
  • Months of treatment aimed at the elbow with little change.

True weakness lifting the wrist or straightening the fingers is a different and more serious finding, and it belongs with a physician rather than on a treatment table.

Where the nerve gets caught

The radial nerve passes between and through muscles of the upper forearm, including a fibrous arch at the top of the supinator, the muscle that turns your palm upward. Several things narrow that path:

  • Repetitive forearm rotation at work or in sport, which thickens and shortens the supinator.
  • Heavy or sustained gripping, which loads the wrist extensors sitting over the nerve.
  • Tightness in the brachioradialis and finger extensors on the same side.
  • A lateral triceps that is tight, which can tether the nerve higher in the arm.
  • Restriction further up at the shoulder or neck, which reduces how much the nerve can glide along its whole length.

Treatment releases the supinator and the extensor group, restores forearm rotation, and uses gentle nerve gliding rather than aggressive stretching, which irritates nerve tissue. If there is genuine weakness straightening the fingers or lifting the wrist, that suggests the nerve is being compressed enough to affect the muscles it supplies, and that needs medical assessment and probably nerve testing first.

What usually helps early on

  1. Change the grip before changing the exercise. A thicker handle, a lighter tool and less sustained squeezing often reduce symptoms quickly.
  2. Reduce repeated forearm twisting for a few weeks, including screwdrivers, wringing and heavy jar opening.
  3. Avoid elbow straps placed over the sore area. A strap designed for tennis elbow can press directly on the irritated nerve.
  4. Do not stretch into tingling or increased ache. Nerve tissue does not respond to force the way muscle does.

Sarasota note

Tennis, pickleball and golf all involve repeated forearm rotation, and so does boat work and gardening. If an elbow has been treated as tennis elbow for months without change, it is worth having the sore point re-mapped, because an inch makes the difference between two completely different problems.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Supinator: Outer elbow and the thumb web; common in tennis elbow that won't settle.
  • Wrist extensors: Outside of the elbow (tennis elbow) and the back of the wrist and hand on the thumb side.
  • Finger extensors: Back of the forearm and back of the hand into the middle or ring finger; a 'stiff fingers' feeling.
  • Brachioradialis: Outside of the elbow and the web of the thumb.
  • Triceps lateral head: Back of the upper arm and the outside of the elbow, a common tennis-elbow look-alike, and the back of the forearm.

When to get medical care first

  • Weakness straightening the fingers or lifting the wrist, or a wrist that drops: see a physician promptly.
  • Numbness on the back of the hand that is constant rather than intermittent.
  • Visible wasting of the forearm muscles compared with the other side.
  • Elbow pain after a fall or direct blow that has not been assessed, particularly with swelling or an inability to straighten the arm.
  • Night pain that is severe and worsening, with fever, unexplained weight loss or a history of cancer: see a physician.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if it is this or tennis elbow?

The simplest marker is where it is tender. Tennis elbow is sore right on the bony bump at the outside of the elbow. Radial tunnel is sore three or four finger-widths further down the forearm, and often aches at night. A physician confirms the distinction.

Can I have both at once?

Yes, and that combination is part of why some elbows do not respond to treatment aimed at only one of them. Mapping the tender point carefully is what separates them.

Why did my elbow strap make it worse?

Straps are designed to offload the tendon near the bone. Positioned over the radial tunnel, the pressure sits directly on the irritated nerve, which commonly increases symptoms.

Should I rest completely?

Usually not. Reducing the specific provoking loads, particularly repeated forearm twisting and hard gripping, tends to work better than stopping all activity.
