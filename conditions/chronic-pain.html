Why long-standing pain is not just old acute pain

When pain persists, the nervous system becomes more efficient at producing it. The pathways carrying the signal become more sensitive, the threshold drops, and the area that hurts often spreads beyond where the original problem was. This is a real, physical change, not an imagined one.

The practical consequence is that the amount of pain stops being a reliable measure of how much tissue damage there is. Something that would have been a minor ache becomes significant, and the pain can continue after the original injury has long healed.

This explains the experience people describe as being told their scan is normal while they are clearly in pain. The scan looks at structure; the problem has moved into sensitivity.

What is usually still driving it

Sensitisation does not mean there is nothing physical left to find. In practice there is almost always a mechanical contribution that has been overlooked:

  • Guarding patterns. Years of protecting an area leaves muscles holding permanently, and holding muscles hurt in their own right.
  • Referred pain from muscles that was never identified, which is common because it does not appear on imaging and is easy to miss on a short exam.
  • Deconditioning. Activity dropped, strength followed, and now less provokes more.
  • Compensation elsewhere. The original problem changed how you move, and the new pattern created its own sources of pain.
  • Sleep, stress and inflammation, all of which raise sensitivity measurably.

What the evaluation does differently

The first visit is long, which matters, because long-standing pain has layers and a fifteen-minute appointment cannot find them. Dr. Jared takes the full history, including what has been tried and what happened each time, then palpates systematically to find which muscles reproduce your actual pain.

That reproduction is important. When pressing a specific muscle recreates the exact pain you have lived with for years, it tells you something is still mechanically contributing, and that is treatable. Many people have never had anyone do this.

His medical training is part of why this works as triage too: conditions that should have been investigated get flagged and referred rather than treated around.

How treatment is built

Hands-on release addresses the muscles found at the evaluation, which often produces change in the first session and, as much as anything, demonstrates that the system can still respond. That matters for someone who has concluded it cannot.

Then movement is rebuilt gradually. Graded exposure is the principle: start comfortably below what provokes you and build, so the nervous system relearns that the movement is safe. Doing too much too soon produces a flare and confirms the fear; doing nothing confirms it differently.

Strength work follows, because capacity is protective. Sleep, stress and daily habits get discussed honestly, since they change pain sensitivity directly. Daily routines come through the free MD Fit app.

For the structure of a longer programme and how sessions are paced, see the chronic pain programme.

What progress looks like

Rarely a straight line, and rarely pain disappearing first. Usually what improves first is function: walking further, sleeping better, lasting longer before symptoms arrive. Pain intensity tends to follow that, not lead it.

Flares happen and are not evidence of failure or of damage. The measure that matters is the trend over a month: tolerance rising, flares shorter, recovery faster.

On being told it is in your head

A great many people arrive having heard some version of this, and it is both unhelpful and inaccurate. Changes in how the nervous system processes pain are physical changes. The pain is real, it is generated by a real mechanism, and that mechanism responds to the right input.

What is also true is that sleep, stress and mood influence pain sensitivity measurably, which is a reason to address them as part of the plan rather than a reason to dismiss the pain.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Upper trapezius: Up the side of the neck, behind the ear and into the temple (a question-mark shape).
  • Quadratus lumborum: Low back and flank, over the SI joint and buttock, and the point of the hip.
  • Psoas: Vertical band along the low back, plus the front of the thigh and groin.
  • Gluteus medius: Along the belt line, over the SI joint, the buttock and the top of the hip.
  • Scalenes: Chest, front and back of the upper arm, inner shoulder blade and down the forearm to the thumb and index finger.
  • Multifidus: Deep, pinpoint pain right next to the spine, the sacrum and sometimes the tailbone.

How MD Fit treats chronic pain

  1. Assess. A hands-on evaluation of how you move and which muscles reproduce your pain.
  2. Release. Targeted soft-tissue work on the muscle at the source.
  3. Correct. Mobility and movement fixes so the problem stops coming back.
  4. Assign homework. Short daily video routines in the free MD Fit app.
  5. Strengthen. Progressive strength work so you stay pain-free and keep doing what you love.

When to get medical care first

  • New or changing pain that is different from your usual pattern, which deserves assessment rather than being folded into the existing diagnosis.
  • Unexplained weight loss, night sweats, or fever alongside pain.
  • Progressive weakness, numbness, or loss of bladder or bowel control: this needs urgent medical assessment.
  • Pain that is constant, unrelated to position or activity, and wakes you every night.
  • A history of cancer with new persistent pain anywhere.
  • Thoughts of self-harm. Long-standing pain and low mood often travel together, and this needs support rather than endurance.

I have tried everything. Why would this be different?

Treatment starts by finding which muscles reproduce your exact pain, which is a step many people have never had. That then guides hands-on work, graded movement and strengthening.

Is this a replacement for my doctor?

No. MD Fit works alongside your medical care and refers out when something needs a specialist.

What about stress and sleep?

Your plan can include breathing, sleep and recovery habits, which influence pain sensitivity directly and play a substantial role.

Will I have to push through pain?

No. The approach is graded exposure, which means starting below what provokes you and building. Pushing into pain tends to produce a flare and set things back.
