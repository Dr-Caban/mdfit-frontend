What shoulder bursitis feels like

Bursitis tends to be a hotter, more constant ache than plain impingement. The outside and top of the shoulder throb at rest, especially at night, and the pain can wake you whichever way you lie. Lifting the arm out to the side or forward hurts across a broad range, not just a narrow arc, and even small movements like reaching for a cup can sting. The upper arm often aches down toward the deltoid insertion. Occasionally the shoulder looks slightly puffy at the front or side.

It can flare after a single overload (a weekend of painting a ceiling, a fall onto the shoulder, moving boxes) or build gradually on top of long-standing rotator cuff irritation. Older adults and people with a forward, rounded posture are more prone to it.

The muscles behind the squeeze

A bursa does not inflame itself. Something is compressing it repeatedly, and in the shoulder that is a set of muscular habits:

  • The supraspinatus tendon lies directly under the bursa. When it is swollen, weak or scarred, the bursa gets caught between it and the bony roof on every lift. The two conditions nearly always travel together.
  • The lateral deltoid lifts the arm, and when the cuff is not holding the ball down it drives the ball upward into the roof, compressing the bursa.
  • The upper trapezius shrugs the shoulder instead of letting the blade rotate; the lower trapezius should be doing that job and is usually weak.
  • The pectoralis minor tips the shoulder blade forward and down, lowering the roof onto the bursa. This is the posture of long hours at a screen.
  • The subscapularis, the cuff muscle on the front of the shoulder blade, often becomes tight and shortens the capsule, so the shoulder loses rotation and the bursa gets pinched sooner in the arc.

What you can do today

  1. Protect it from repeated small insults. For a week or two, keep the elbow below shoulder height and close to the body when you reach, and avoid leaning on that elbow.
  2. Use ice or a cool pack on the outside of the shoulder for short periods if it is hot and throbbing, especially before bed.
  3. Change the sleep setup. Lie on the other side with a pillow hugged in front to support the sore arm, or on your back with a pillow under the elbow so the arm does not hang.
  4. Keep the rest of the arm moving. Gentle pendulum swings and elbow, wrist and hand movement keep the shoulder from stiffening while the bursa calms.

What else looks like bursitis

  • Rotator cuff tendinopathy or tear: the usual companion. Weakness lifting or rotating the arm points toward a tear. See rotator cuff tear.
  • Calcific tendinitis: a calcium deposit in the supraspinatus tendon that can cause sudden, severe shoulder pain; an X-ray shows it.
  • Frozen shoulder: progressive stiffness in all directions, worst for reaching behind the back and rotating outward. See frozen shoulder.
  • Septic (infected) bursitis: a hot, red, swollen shoulder, often with fever, needing urgent medical care.
  • Inflammatory arthritis or polymyalgia: both shoulders aching and stiff in the morning, often with the hips, in older adults; this needs a physician and blood work.
  • Neck referral: pain that changes with head position or comes with tingling in the hand.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Supraspinatus: Deep ache at the top and side of the shoulder that can run down the outside of the arm to the elbow.
  • Lateral deltoid: Outside of the shoulder cap and the side of the upper arm toward the deltoid's insertion.
  • Upper trapezius: Up the side of the neck, behind the ear and into the temple (a question-mark shape).
  • Lower trapezius: Up the back of the neck to the base of the skull, the top of the shoulder, and along the inner shoulder blade.
  • Pectoralis minor: Front of the shoulder and chest, down the inner arm to the last three fingers.
  • Subscapularis: Back of the shoulder, down the back of the arm, sometimes a band around the wrist.

When to get medical care first

  • A hot, red, visibly swollen shoulder, especially with fever or chills, which can mean an infected bursa or joint and needs same-day medical care.
  • Inability to lift the arm at all after a fall, or a visible deformity of the shoulder or collarbone.
  • Severe, constant pain that is not changed by any position and is worsening, particularly with unexplained weight loss or a history of cancer.
  • Numbness, tingling or weakness in the arm or hand.
  • Shoulder pain with chest pressure, shortness of breath, sweating or nausea: call 911.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between bursitis and impingement?

Impingement is the pinching mechanism; bursitis is the inflamed cushion that results when the pinching goes on long enough or a single overload sets it off. Most bursitis is treated by fixing the impingement mechanics once the inflammation calms.

Do I need a cortisone injection?

Some people get useful relief from one when the bursa is very irritable and blocking rehab; that is a decision with your physician. It treats the inflammation, not the mechanics, so we pair it with the corrective work.

Should I move the shoulder or rest it?

Both, in balance. Avoid the overhead and reaching tasks that provoke it for a short time, but keep the shoulder gently moving every day so it does not stiffen into a frozen shoulder.
