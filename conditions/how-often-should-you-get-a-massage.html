How Often Should You Get a Massage?

The four phases, and what each needs

In a flare. Something is actively painful and limiting what you do. This is the one phase where closer spacing genuinely helps, because each session buys a window of reduced guarding that lets you move, and movement is what settles a flare. Weekly or fortnightly for the first few weeks is a common pattern here.

Rehabbing. The sharp phase has passed and you are rebuilding. Hands-on work now supports the loading programme rather than leading it, so sessions typically space out while the exercise work ramps up.

Maintaining. Nothing is wrong, you have a known area that grumbles, and you want to keep it quiet. Monthly suits most people in this position, and some do well with less.

Training hard. Frequency follows the training block rather than the calendar. Heavier weeks and peak mileage justify more; off-season or a deload justifies less. The sports massage page covers how to time it around competition.

The question that matters more than frequency

Is the interval getting longer over time?

That single trend tells you more than any schedule. If you started weekly, moved to fortnightly, and are now comfortable at six weeks, the plan is working and the underlying problem is genuinely improving. If you have been at weekly for eight months and the same muscle is tight every time, massage has become a way of managing a problem rather than resolving it.

Neither of those is a reason to feel bad. The second one is simply a signal to change the conversation: what is reloading that tissue every week, and what would need to be stronger, or set up differently, for it to stop? That is usually a question about the desk, the training week, or a capacity gap somewhere in the chain, and it is answered with strength work and homework rather than another hour on the table.

Signs to come in sooner

  • A familiar problem is starting to return, and you recognise the early version of it. Catching it here is cheaper and quicker than waiting until it is a full flare.
  • Training volume has stepped up, or a competition block is starting.
  • Sleep is being disturbed by a known tight area.
  • You have just come through a period of unusual load: a move, a long flight, a stretch of overtime, a bout of illness that kept you still.

Signs to space things out: the area stays quiet between visits, your range is holding, and the homework is going in without the tightness returning. That is the outcome to aim for.

Making each session go further

Three things reliably extend the interval, and all three are free:

  • Do the homework. The videos added to your free MD Fit app after a session exist because an hour of hands-on work cannot outvote the rest of the week. The people who space out fastest are the ones doing two or three short sessions between visits.
  • Use the range the same day. New range that is not used tends to be given back. A walk, the mobility work, or simply moving through the positions that were restricted helps the change hold.
  • Change one thing about the load. The chair height, the pillow, the warm-up, the weekly mileage jump. One structural change beats several extra sessions.

If cost is a factor, and for a self-pay practice it often is, this is where the leverage is. Session options are on the medical massage page, but the honest advice is that money spent on fewer sessions plus consistent homework usually beats money spent on more sessions without it.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Upper trapezius: Up the side of the neck, behind the ear and into the temple (a question-mark shape).
  • Quadratus lumborum: Low back and flank, over the SI joint and buttock, and the point of the hip.
  • Gluteus medius: Along the belt line, over the SI joint, the buttock and the top of the hip.
  • Soleus: Heel and Achilles tendon, lower calf; can contribute to plantar fasciitis.
  • Pectoralis minor: Front of the shoulder and chest, down the inner arm to the last three fingers.
  • Multifidus: Deep, pinpoint pain right next to the spine, the sacrum and sometimes the tailbone.

What to expect

A reasonable expectation is that the useful interval lengthens over the first couple of months as the corrective work takes hold. If it is not lengthening at all, that is worth raising rather than simply booking the next one.

When to get medical care first

  • Needing a massage just to get through the week, with no improvement in the interval over months.
  • Pain that is spreading, or that now includes numbness or weakness, which needs an evaluation rather than another session.
  • New night pain that consistently wakes you, or pain with fever or unexplained weight loss.
  • A new area of swelling, redness or warmth in a limb, which should be assessed before any massage.
  • Pain following a fall or accident that has not been medically assessed.

Frequently asked questions

Is weekly massage too much?

Not during an active flare or a heavy training block, where it has a clear job. Weekly indefinitely, with no change in the interval over months, usually means something else needs attention.

Can I have too much massage?

The practical limits are soreness and cost rather than danger. Sessions stacked so closely that you are always mildly sore are not helping, and that is a sign to reduce either frequency or pressure.

How do I know it is working?

The interval should get longer and the baseline should get quieter. Relief that lasts three days after every session for a year is managing the problem, not resolving it.

Is a monthly massage worth it if nothing hurts?

For plenty of people yes, as maintenance on a known grumbly area and as a regular check on how things are moving. It is a reasonable choice, just not a medical necessity.
