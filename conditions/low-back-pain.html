Why the back ends up doing the hips' work

Sitting shortens the hip flexors and switches off the glutes. Over months the pelvis tips forward, the lumbar arch deepens, and the muscles beside the spine hold a contraction they were never meant to hold all day. By the time it hurts, the back is not the cause. It is the part that gave out first.

This is why so many people get a few good days from a massage and then return to the same ache. The tissue was released; the loading pattern that produced it was not changed.

  • Psoas and iliacus stay short from hours at a desk or in a car, tipping the pelvis and deepening the arch.
  • Gluteus maximus stops firing for hip extension, so the back extensors supply it on every step.
  • Quadratus lumborum takes up the slack and refers a deep ache into the flank and upper buttock.
  • Multifidus, the deep segmental stabiliser, often switches off after an episode and does not come back on its own.

What your pain pattern is telling you

Where and when it hurts narrows the field considerably:

  • Worse sitting, better standing and walking. Often disc-related loading. Sitting raises pressure through the front of the disc.
  • Worse standing and walking, better sitting or leaning forward. Points toward the joints at the back of the spine or toward narrowing in the canal.
  • Worse first thing, loosens within an hour. Usually muscular and stiffness-driven.
  • One-sided, right beside the spine, reproduced by pressing it. Typically muscular and the most responsive to hands-on work.
  • Travels below the knee with numbness or tingling. Now a nerve question, and it changes the plan. See sciatica and disc-related pain.

What happens at your evaluation

Dr. Jared watches you move before he touches anything: how you bend, how you get out of a chair, whether your hips or your back do the hinging. Then he palpates to find which muscles reproduce your exact pain, and tests hip extension and rotation, because a hip that cannot extend forces the back to extend instead.

Imaging is usually not needed first. Disc bulges and arthritic changes show up on scans of people with no pain at all, which is why a report rarely explains why today hurts. If something in the history or exam suggests imaging or a specialist, he says so and refers.

What you can do this week

  1. Break up sitting every 30 to 45 minutes, even briefly. Position changes matter more than any one stretch.
  2. Hinge at the hips when you pick something up. Push your hips back, keep your shins close to vertical, let the back stay long.
  3. Lie on your back with knees bent and gently press your low back into the floor for a few breaths, several times a day.
  4. Keep walking. Rest beyond a day or two usually makes back pain worse, not better.

If one position reliably eases it, use it often. That information is useful at your evaluation.

Conditions that need ruling out first

Most low back pain is mechanical, but not all of it. Pain that does not change with position, that wakes you at night, or that comes with fever, unexplained weight loss or a history of cancer belongs with a physician first. Pain with abdominal or flank symptoms can be kidney-related. Dr. Jared's medical training is part of why this triage happens at the first visit rather than after six weeks of treatment.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Psoas: Vertical band along the low back, plus the front of the thigh and groin.
  • Iliacus: Groin, front of the thigh, and the back of the pelvis high in the glute.
  • Quadratus lumborum: Low back and flank, over the SI joint and buttock, and the point of the hip.
  • Gluteus maximus: Buttock and the tailbone; aches with long sitting and walking uphill.
  • Multifidus: Deep, pinpoint pain right next to the spine, the sacrum and sometimes the tailbone.
  • Longissimus: Low in the buttock and over the SI joint, often well below where the muscle actually is.

When to get medical care first

  • New loss of bladder or bowel control, or numbness in the groin, inner thighs or saddle area: go to an emergency room now.
  • Leg weakness that is getting worse, a foot that drops, or new trouble walking.
  • Back pain with fever, chills or unexplained weight loss.
  • Back pain after a significant fall or accident, especially with osteoporosis.
  • Pain that does not change at all with position and wakes you from sleep every night.
  • A history of cancer with new, persistent back pain.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need imaging before my first visit?

Usually not. Dr. Jared starts with a hands-on evaluation of how you move and which muscles reproduce your pain. If anything in the exam suggests imaging or a specialist, he tells you and refers.

How many visits does low back pain take?

It depends on how long it has been going on and what is driving it. Many people feel a difference in the first few sessions, then build strength so it stays gone. You leave every visit with homework videos in the MD Fit app.

Can I keep working out?

In most cases yes, with changes. Your plan tells you what to keep, what to modify and what to pause while the source muscle recovers.

Is my disc bulge the reason my back hurts?

Not necessarily. Disc bulges are common on scans of people with no pain at all. What matters is whether your symptoms match what the exam finds, which is what the evaluation sorts out.
