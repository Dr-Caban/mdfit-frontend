Where it hurts and when

Biceps tendon pain lives at the front of the shoulder, about a thumb's width out from the armpit crease, and can be pinpointed with one finger. Pressing there reproduces it. It travels down the front of the upper arm, sometimes to the elbow crease. Carrying grocery bags, curls, lifting a suitcase into an overhead bin, reaching behind you, opening a heavy door and throwing all irritate it. Sleeping on the shoulder is uncomfortable. Some people feel or hear a snap at the front of the shoulder as the tendon moves in its groove.

Typical triggers are a jump in pulling or curling volume at the gym, a season of racquet sport or golf, a lot of overhead work, or a shoulder that has had rotator cuff problems for a while, because when the cuff is weak the biceps gets recruited as a stabilizer and wears out.

The muscles that load the tendon

  • Biceps, long head: the sore tendon itself. Its trigger points refer pain over the groove and down the front of the arm toward the elbow crease. Dr. Jared's notes list repeated overhead work and throwing, heavy curls and palm-up carrying, cuff weakness and rounded shoulders that pinch the tendon as the usual causes.
  • Biceps, short head and coracobrachialis: they attach beside the long head and refer to the front of the shoulder near the collarbone tip and the inner arm; when tight they pull the shoulder forward and add tension through the whole front of the joint.
  • Anterior deltoid and the upper pectoralis major: heavy pressing without enough pulling leaves these short and overworked, and both refer pain over the front of the shoulder, often blamed on the joint.
  • Infraspinatus: a rotator cuff muscle on the back of the shoulder blade whose trigger points refer pain deep into the front of the shoulder. Treating the back of the shoulder to fix the front surprises people, but it is a classic pattern.

How MD Fit treats biceps tendon pain

Dr. Jared Caban first makes sure it is the tendon: he palpates the groove, tests the biceps against resistance, checks the rotator cuff for weakness and screens for a labral tear or instability, which change the plan. He also looks at the posture of the shoulder blade, because a forward, rounded shoulder narrows the groove and angles the tendon so it rubs with every lift.

The hands-on work releases the chest, front deltoid, coracobrachialis and infraspinatus so the ball of the shoulder sits back where the groove is straight. The tendon itself is treated gently; aggressive work on an irritated tendon makes it worse. Correction then focuses on the shoulder blade and the rotator cuff so the biceps stops being the stabilizer of last resort. Your homework in the free MD Fit app begins with isometric holds that calm the tendon, and progresses to slow, controlled loading so the tendon rebuilds its capacity to carry, curl and reach.

What you can do today

  1. Carry with the palm facing in and the elbow slightly bent rather than palm-up with the arm straight; the tendon is far more tolerant of that position.
  2. Pause curls, pull-ups and heavy pressing for a short period, and keep rows and lower-body training going so you stay active.
  3. Try a gentle isometric. Press your fist upward into the underside of a table with the elbow bent at a right angle, at a comfortable effort, and hold for a slow breath or two. Pain should not climb while you hold.
  4. Sleep off the shoulder with a pillow hugged in front to support the arm.

When front-of-shoulder pain is not the biceps

  • Shoulder impingement and bursitis: a painful arc raising the arm out to the side rather than a point-tender groove; they often travel together with biceps tendon irritation. See impingement.
  • SLAP or labral tear: deep pain with clicking or catching, often after a fall or in throwers, with a feeling the joint is not quite stable.
  • Subscapularis tendon problems, which sit just inside the groove and refer to the back of the shoulder and wrist.
  • AC joint pain at the end of the collarbone, worst reaching across the chest.
  • Pectoralis major tear: a pop and bruising on the chest during a heavy press.
  • Biceps rupture: a pop followed by a bulge in the upper arm and bruising; this needs prompt evaluation, although many people function well afterwards.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Biceps long head: Front of the shoulder over the biceps groove and down the front of the arm toward the elbow crease.
  • Biceps short head: Front of the shoulder near the collarbone tip, the inner front of the arm and the elbow crease.
  • Coracobrachialis: Front of the shoulder, the back of the upper arm and forearm, and the back of the hand toward the middle finger.
  • Anterior deltoid: Front of the shoulder and down the front of the upper arm, close to the muscle itself.
  • Pectoralis major upper head: Front of the shoulder over the front delt, often mistaken for a front-shoulder joint problem.
  • Infraspinatus: Deep in the front of the shoulder joint, down the front and outside of the arm.

When to get medical care first

  • A pop at the front of the shoulder or arm followed by a new bulge in the upper arm and bruising, which can be a tendon rupture and should be evaluated promptly.
  • The shoulder catches, locks or feels like it slips out of place.
  • Inability to lift the arm at all after a fall or injury.
  • A hot, red, swollen shoulder or fever.
  • Shoulder or arm pain with chest pressure, shortness of breath, sweating or nausea: call 911.

Frequently asked questions

Should I rest the arm completely?

No. Tendons recover by being loaded carefully, not by being ignored. We reduce the provoking tasks for a while and replace them with isometrics and slow loading the tendon can tolerate.

Why are you working on the back of my shoulder for pain in the front?

The infraspinatus, on the back of the shoulder blade, refers pain deep into the front of the shoulder, and tight chest and front-deltoid muscles pull the ball forward so the biceps tendon rubs in its groove. Both are usually part of the picture.

What if the tendon ruptures?

A long-head biceps rupture produces a pop and a bulge. It should be evaluated, but many people, especially past middle age, recover good function without surgery. Your physician will advise based on your activity and goals.
