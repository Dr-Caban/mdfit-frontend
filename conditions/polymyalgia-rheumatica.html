Read this first

PMR is closely linked to another condition, giant cell arteritis, which can threaten sight permanently and does so quickly. The warning signs below are an emergency and must not wait for an appointment:

  • A new, severe headache, particularly around the temple.
  • Tenderness of the scalp, noticed when brushing hair or resting on a pillow.
  • Any change in vision: blurring, double vision, or loss of sight in part or all of one eye, even briefly.
  • Jaw ache or fatigue that comes on while chewing and eases on stopping.

If any of these appear, seek emergency medical care immediately. Call 911 for sudden vision loss. Sight lost to giant cell arteritis is generally not recovered, and prompt treatment is what prevents it.

What the picture usually looks like

The presentation is distinctive once you know it, and it is quite unlike the gradual wear people expect from ageing.

  • It arrives quickly. Many people can name the week. Over days to a few weeks, not years.
  • Both sides at once. Shoulders, upper arms, neck, hips and thighs, symmetrically.
  • Mornings are the worst by a distance. Stiffness lasting well over an hour, often much of the morning.
  • Simple movements become difficult. Getting out of bed, rising from a chair, lifting arms to wash hair or fasten a bra.
  • It is stiffness and ache rather than sharp pain, and strength is usually preserved once you are moving, even though everything feels weak.
  • General unwellness is common: fatigue, low appetite, low mood, sometimes a mild fever or weight loss.

Almost always over fifty, and more common with age.

What it is not, and why that matters

PMR gets mistaken for several things this practice does treat, which is exactly why it is on this site.

Unlike frozen shoulder, it affects both shoulders together and the hips as well, and the restriction is stiffness rather than a hard block to passive movement. Unlike ordinary shoulder pain or rotator cuff problems, there is no injury, both sides start together, and mornings dominate. Unlike fibromyalgia, it comes on over weeks rather than persisting for years, it is concentrated in the shoulder and hip girdles rather than being widespread, and the morning stiffness is far more pronounced.

The reason the distinction matters: PMR responds to medical treatment, typically well and sometimes dramatically. Months of massage and exercise for a condition that needs a prescription is time and money spent on the wrong thing, and it delays a treatment that works.

Why a physician comes first

Diagnosis rests on the clinical picture together with blood tests for inflammation, and on excluding the conditions that look similar. That is a physician's work, and there is no soft-tissue equivalent.

Dr. Jared Caban holds a medical degree alongside his clinical exercise physiology and massage credentials, and on this particular condition the most useful thing that training does is recognise the pattern and say so. If what you describe fits PMR, you will be told to see a physician promptly, and that advice is the service.

Treatment is medical and is managed by your physician, including any decisions about medication and about reducing it over time. None of that is adjusted here.

Where movement work does fit

Once PMR is confirmed and medical treatment has begun, there is a genuine role for the kind of work done here, alongside that care rather than in place of it.

  • Keeping range. Weeks of stiffness and reduced use leave shoulders and hips tight in their own right, and that tightness persists after the inflammation settles.
  • Rebuilding strength. Prolonged inactivity costs muscle, particularly over sixty. Progressive resistance work is a clinical exercise physiologist's core competence.
  • Bone and medication. Longer-term steroid treatment has implications for bone density that your physician will discuss. Appropriate loading matters, which is covered on exercise and bone density.
  • Balance and falls. Weakness around the hips affects stability, which is the subject of balance and fall prevention.

Hands-on work can ease the secondary muscular tightness that builds up, but it does not treat the underlying inflammation and is not offered as though it does.

A note for Sarasota

PMR is a condition of later life, and this is a county with a large population in exactly that age group. It is also one that gets attributed to ageing for months before anyone tests for it, because stiff shoulders at seventy sound unremarkable.

Stiffness that arrives over a fortnight, hits both sides, and is dramatically worse in the morning is not ordinary ageing, and it is worth saying so to a physician rather than waiting it out.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Upper trapezius: Up the side of the neck, behind the ear and into the temple (a question-mark shape).
  • Lateral deltoid: Outside of the shoulder cap and the side of the upper arm toward the deltoid's insertion.
  • Gluteus medius: Along the belt line, over the SI joint, the buttock and the top of the hip.
  • Psoas: Vertical band along the low back, plus the front of the thigh and groin.
  • Supraspinatus: Deep ache at the top and side of the shoulder that can run down the outside of the arm to the elbow.
  • Quadratus lumborum: Low back and flank, over the SI joint and buttock, and the point of the hip.

When to get medical care first

  • New severe headache, scalp tenderness, jaw ache while chewing, or any change or loss of vision: this is an emergency, seek medical care immediately and call 911 for sudden vision loss.
  • New stiffness across both shoulders and both hips lasting more than an hour each morning in someone over fifty, which needs a physician promptly.
  • Fever, unexplained weight loss, or night sweats alongside the stiffness.
  • Symptoms that return or worsen while reducing medication, which your physician needs to know about.
  • Genuine weakness rather than stiffness, particularly if it is worsening, which needs medical assessment.

Frequently asked questions

Can massage or exercise treat polymyalgia rheumatica?

No. It is an inflammatory condition treated medically, and that treatment is managed by your physician. Movement and hands-on work are useful alongside it for stiffness, strength and balance, not as a substitute for it.

How is it different from frozen shoulder?

Frozen shoulder is usually one shoulder with a hard block to movement even when someone else moves the arm for you. PMR affects both shoulders and both hips together, comes with marked morning stiffness, and often general unwellness.

I have been told it is just my age. Should I push for a test?

If the stiffness arrived over days or weeks, affects both sides, and lasts more than an hour each morning, that pattern is worth raising specifically with your physician. Inflammatory markers are a simple blood test.

Can I be seen here while under medical treatment?
