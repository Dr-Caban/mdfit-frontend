Why the first stop is a physician, not a massage table

Whiplash is a sudden whip of the head forward and back, most often in a rear-end crash on I-75 or US-41, but also from a fall, a wave at the beach, or a collision on the pickleball court. The force can injure bone, ligament, disc and nerve as well as muscle, and some of those injuries are not obvious in the first hours. That is why I ask every person to be seen by a physician or an emergency department before they come to the clinic. Imaging and a neurological exam are their job. My job starts after that, with the soft tissue.

If you have already been cleared and the pain is still there two or more weeks later, that is the stage where hands-on care and graded movement tend to help most.

What a whiplash neck usually feels like

People often describe a neck that felt fine at the scene and then stiffened overnight. Common patterns include:

  • Stiffness that makes it hard to check a blind spot or look up.
  • A deep ache at the base of the skull that creeps into a headache behind the eyes.
  • Pain where the neck meets the shoulder, sometimes down to the shoulder blade.
  • Dizziness, jaw tightness, trouble concentrating, or a sense that the head is heavy.

These symptoms are real, and most of them are muscular once the serious causes have been excluded. The neck muscles contracted hard in a fraction of a second to protect the spine, and they tend to stay guarded long after the danger has passed.

The muscles that stay guarded after a whiplash

In clinic the same few muscles show up again and again after a collision:

  • Sternocleidomastoid (SCM): the front-of-neck strap that absorbs the backward snap. It refers pain to the forehead, around the eye and the ear, and can cause dizziness and ear fullness.
  • Suboccipitals: tiny muscles at the top of the neck that produce the band-like headache people mistake for a migraine.
  • Levator scapulae and upper trapezius: the stiff neck that will not turn, and the heavy ache on top of the shoulder.
  • Splenius: a deep ache inside the head or on top of the skull.
  • Scalenes: side-neck breathing muscles that can send tingling into the arm and hand.

How I work with whiplash at MD Fit

The first visit is mostly listening and testing. I want to know how the crash happened, what the physician found, which movements hurt, and whether anything about the pain is changing. Then I check how each neck muscle moves and how it responds to pressure, because a protective muscle feels very different from a torn one.

Treatment is gentle at first. Hands-on release of the SCM, suboccipitals and levator usually eases the headache and gives back some rotation in the same session. From there we correct the things keeping the muscles on guard, often shallow upper-chest breathing and a head that sits forward, and I assign a short daily routine in the free MD Fit app. As the neck calms, we add deep neck flexor and shoulder blade strengthening so the recovery holds.

Safe steps you can take today

  1. Keep moving within comfort. Slow, small head turns and nods several times a day beat a collar or complete rest once your physician has cleared you.
  2. Breathe low. Rest a hand on your belly and let it rise before your chest does. This takes work off the scalenes and SCM.
  3. Use heat before you move and ice only if something feels freshly inflamed.
  4. Sleep with one supportive pillow that keeps your neck level, and avoid sleeping on your stomach.

Stop any movement that produces sharp pain, numbness, or dizziness and tell your physician.

When it is more than muscle

Not every post-collision neck is a muscle problem. A physician should re-evaluate you if there is arm weakness, numbness that follows a clear strip down the arm, pain that is worse at night, trouble swallowing, or a headache that keeps escalating. Concussion symptoms such as memory gaps, light sensitivity and nausea need their own care. Educational information on this page is not a substitute for that evaluation.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Sternocleidomastoid: Forehead, around and behind the eye, cheek, ear and top of the head.
  • Suboccipitals: A band of headache from the base of the skull wrapping to behind the eye and the forehead.
  • Levator scapulae: The angle where the neck meets the shoulder, down the inner edge of the shoulder blade.
  • Upper trapezius: Up the side of the neck, behind the ear and into the temple (a question-mark shape).
  • Splenius capitis & cervicis: Pain on the top of the head, and deep ache inside the head behind the eye.
  • Scalenes: Chest, front and back of the upper arm, inner shoulder blade and down the forearm to the thumb and index finger.

When to get medical care first

  • Any neck pain after a collision, fall or blow to the head needs a physician or emergency room evaluation before soft-tissue care.
  • Severe headache, confusion, repeated vomiting, slurred speech or loss of consciousness after the injury: call 911.
  • Weakness, numbness or tingling in the arms or legs, or loss of bladder or bowel control: emergency care now.
  • Pain that is getting worse week by week instead of better, or pain with fever.
  • Difficulty swallowing, a change in your voice, or a pulsing sensation in the neck.

Frequently asked questions

I was in a fender bender last week and my neck still hurts. Can I book directly with MD Fit?

Please see a physician or urgent care first so fractures, ligament injuries and concussion are excluded. Bring that clearance and we will start with hands-on release and gentle movement.

Why do I have headaches when the accident hurt my neck?

The suboccipitals, SCM and upper trapezius refer pain into the head. Releasing them often reduces the headache before the neck itself feels fully normal.

Should I wear a soft collar?

Follow your physician's advice. For most muscular whiplash, gentle movement within comfort recovers faster than prolonged immobilization, but that decision belongs to the doctor who examined you.

How long does whiplash take to settle?

It varies with the force of the injury and how long the muscles have been guarding. Dr. Jared discusses a realistic timeline at your first visit rather than quoting an average.
