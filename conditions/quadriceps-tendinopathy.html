Above the kneecap, not below it

The defining feature is location. Quadriceps tendon pain sits in the soft tissue immediately above the top edge of the kneecap. Patellar tendon pain sits below it. Both hurt with the same activities, so the only reliable way to tell them apart is to find the tender spot.

  • A localised ache or sharpness just above the kneecap, tender to press.
  • Worse with squatting, descending stairs, getting out of a low chair, and the bottom of a lunge.
  • Stiffness after sitting with the knee bent, easing after a few steps.
  • Pain that warms up during activity, then returns worse hours later or the next morning.
  • Occasionally a thickened, slightly swollen feel to the tendon compared with the other side.

It tends to appear in people who have recently increased squatting, weightlifting, cycling with a low saddle, or stair and hill work, and in middle-aged and older adults where the tendon has been quietly degenerating for some time.

How it differs from patellar tendinopathy

Patellar tendinopathy, often called jumper's knee, involves the tendon below the kneecap running to the shin. The distinctions matter because the loading that helps each is slightly different.

  • Position. Quad tendon above the patella; patellar tendon below. Press both edges and compare.
  • Who gets it. Patellar tendinopathy skews younger and toward jumping sports. Quadriceps tendinopathy is more common in older athletes, weightlifters and cyclists.
  • Deep flexion. Quadriceps tendon pain is often provoked more by very deep knee bend, where the tendon is compressed against the thigh bone.
  • What eases it. Both respond to graded loading, but quadriceps tendon pain usually needs the depth of squat limited at first, while the patellar tendon often tolerates a decline position better.

Front-of-knee pain that moves around, is worse sitting for long periods and has no single tender spot is more likely patellofemoral pain, which is a joint-surface problem rather than a tendon one.

The muscles behind it

Four muscles feed into this tendon, and the hip above decides how much work the knee has to do.

  • Rectus femoris is the only one of the four that crosses the hip as well as the knee, so it tightens with long sitting and then pulls hard on the tendon.
  • Vastus lateralis is typically dominant and tight, dragging the kneecap outward as well as upward.
  • Vastus medialis is often the weak link, particularly after any period of knee swelling or rest.
  • Vastus intermedius lies deep and attaches directly into the tendon and the joint capsule.
  • Gluteus maximus matters because a squat driven by the hips spreads the load, while a squat driven by the knees sends all of it through this tendon.

What you can do this week

  1. Cap your squat depth. Stop above the point where the pain begins rather than pushing through it, and keep training in the range that does not provoke it.
  2. Take stairs one at a time going down, leading with the better leg, until the tendon settles.
  3. Try a wall sit at a shallow angle, around 30 to 45 degrees of knee bend, held for thirty to forty-five seconds. Tendon pain that eases afterwards is a useful sign.
  4. Raise your bike saddle slightly if cycling provokes it, which reduces how far the knee bends at the top of the pedal stroke.

Other sources of pain above the knee

A sudden pop with inability to straighten the knee or a visible gap above the kneecap suggests a tendon rupture and is a surgical emergency. Knee osteoarthritis produces deeper, more diffuse ache with morning stiffness. A suprapatellar bursitis causes a softer, more fluid swelling. Referred pain from the vastus intermedius or from the hip can be felt at the front of the thigh and knee without any local tenderness. Pain at the front of the thigh with night pain or weight loss belongs with a physician.

Sarasota notes

Two groups bring this in: people who have taken up strength training later in life and progressed depth faster than the tendon could adapt, and golfers and walkers dealing with hills, stairs and getting in and out of low seating. A tendon rewards patience and steady loading more than rest, and most people keep training through this with the range adjusted rather than stopping entirely.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Rectus femoris: A deep ache around and behind the kneecap, and the front of the hip bone.
  • Vastus lateralis: Down the outer thigh to the outside of the knee; can make lying on that side painful.
  • Vastus medialis: Inner knee and inside of the kneecap; can make the knee feel like it buckles.
  • Vastus intermedius: Front of the mid-thigh, worse climbing stairs or standing up from a chair.
  • Gluteus maximus: Buttock and the tailbone; aches with long sitting and walking uphill.

What to expect

Tendon pain commonly begins easing within a few weeks of starting isometric work and capping provocative depth. Restoring full tolerance for deep squatting, hills and heavy lifting is usually a matter of months. Progress is guided by how the knee feels the morning after loading, not by the calendar.

When to get medical care first

  • A pop above the kneecap with inability to straighten the leg or a visible gap in the tendon: possible rupture, seek emergency care.
  • A hot, red, swollen knee with fever or feeling unwell.
  • Knee that locks and will not straighten, or gives way causing falls.
  • Rapid swelling of the knee within hours of an injury.
  • Night pain that does not change with position, unexplained weight loss, or a history of cancer.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if it is the quad tendon or the patellar tendon?

Press just above the top edge of the kneecap, then just below the bottom edge. The tender one tells you which tendon is involved. Dr. Jared confirms it alongside strength and movement testing.

Should I rest it completely?

Usually not. Tendons lose capacity quickly with rest and then hurt more when you return. Reducing the provocative range while continuing to load the tendon generally works better than stopping.

Can I keep lifting weights?

Most people can, with the depth limited and the load adjusted. Dr. Jared sets a range that loads the tendon without compressing it and progresses from there.

Will an injection help?

That is a physician's decision. Steroid injection into or around a tendon carries risk of weakening it, so most clinicians try graded loading first.
