VO2 max is a measure of how much oxygen your body can use at maximum effort. That sounds like an athlete's concern, and it gets discussed as one. In practice it is a measure of how much of your ordinary life you can do without being limited — which is why it becomes more interesting, not less, as people get older.

What it actually measures

Oxygen gets into your lungs, moves into the blood, gets pumped by the heart, and is extracted and used by the muscles. VO2 max is the ceiling of that whole chain working together. It is why it is a better summary of overall physical capacity than almost any single number — it depends on the lungs, the heart, the blood, the circulation and the muscle all at once.

In everyday terms it shows up as:

  • How you feel at the top of a flight of stairs.
  • Whether a hill on the course is a nuisance or the end of the round.
  • Whether you can hold a conversation while walking with someone faster than you.
  • How much reserve you have when something goes wrong — an illness, an operation, a hospital stay.

That last point is the one most people have never had explained. Cardiorespiratory fitness is reserve capacity, and reserve is what you draw on in a bad week.

Why it matters more with age

Cardiorespiratory fitness declines gradually through adult life if nothing is done about it, and the rate tends to steepen later. The practical consequence is a creeping narrowing of what feels comfortable: the hill gets avoided, then the stairs, then the long walk from the far end of the car park.

Most people experience this as circumstance — the garden got harder, the course got longer — rather than as a measurable thing that is changing and could be changed back. Giving it a number makes it visible.

It also responds. Trained appropriately, cardiorespiratory fitness improves at any age. The improvement is slower later in life than earlier, but the direction is reversible, and for someone who has been sedentary the early gains are usually the largest of their adult life.

How it gets assessed here

A true maximal VO2 test requires a mask, a metabolic cart and an all-out effort to exhaustion, usually in a laboratory or sports science setting. It is the gold standard, and for most people it is neither necessary nor appropriate — particularly for anyone with a cardiac, pulmonary or metabolic history.

What is useful and practical is submaximal assessment: structured walking or cycling protocols with heart rate, blood pressure and perceived effort monitored, which give a reliable estimate and a repeatable baseline without pushing anyone to their limit. These are standard tools for a Clinical Exercise Physiologist and they are what I use.

If your history suggests you need a formal exercise stress test under cardiology supervision, I will tell you that rather than test around it. That is a cardiology procedure, not something this practice performs or replaces.

How you actually improve it

Mostly through accumulated easy aerobic work, with a small amount of harder work added once the base is there. The common mistakes are the opposite of each other:

  • Doing everything at a moderate, slightly-uncomfortable pace — hard enough to be unpleasant, not hard enough to drive adaptation, and too hard to accumulate much volume.
  • Jumping straight to intervals because they are efficient, with no base underneath, and either getting injured or quitting.

So the plan usually starts with building duration at an easy conversational effort, with intensity added later and sparingly. Strength work supports it, because the quadriceps, glutes and calves are what carry you, and weak legs cap what the heart and lungs can be asked to do.

For people on cardiac medication, heart rate targets can be misleading, so we use effort-based methods that still work.

Why this is a CEP thing

This is the clearest example of the difference in scope. A physical therapy clinic treats the injury; a gym trains the fit. Assessing and prescribing for cardiorespiratory fitness in someone with a medical history sits between the two, and it is specifically what the Clinical Exercise Physiologist credential covers — the dosing, the contraindications, what to watch for.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Vastus lateralis: Down the outer thigh to the outside of the knee; can make lying on that side painful.
  • Gluteus maximus: Buttock and the tailbone; aches with long sitting and walking uphill.
  • Gastrocnemius: Upper calf, back of the knee and the arch of the foot; deep cramp-like tightness.
  • Soleus: Heel and Achilles tendon, lower calf; can contribute to plantar fasciitis.
  • Rectus femoris: A deep ache around and behind the kneecap, and the front of the hip bone.

When to get medical care first

  • Chest pain or pressure during exertion, particularly if it is new or has changed in pattern: stop and call 911.
  • Breathlessness that is out of proportion to the effort, or that is worsening over weeks.
  • Fainting, near-fainting or an irregular heartbeat during exercise: stop and seek medical care.
  • Breathlessness when lying flat, or new swelling in the ankles.
  • If you have a cardiac or pulmonary history, start with your physician's clearance rather than with a fitness test.

Frequently asked questions

Do you do a real VO2 max test with a mask?

No. A true maximal test needs a metabolic cart and an all-out effort, usually in a laboratory setting. Submaximal protocols give a reliable, repeatable estimate without pushing anyone to their limit, and that is what is used here.

Is this only relevant for athletes?

The opposite, in practice. For most people it is a measure of reserve capacity: stairs, hills, recovery from illness, how much of ordinary life feels comfortable.

Can it improve at my age?

Yes. Improvement is slower later in life than earlier, but it responds at any age, and the early gains for someone who has been sedentary are often the largest of their adult life.

Is this an exercise stress test?

No. A diagnostic stress test is a cardiology procedure performed under medical supervision. If your history suggests you need one, you will be told so rather than tested around it.
