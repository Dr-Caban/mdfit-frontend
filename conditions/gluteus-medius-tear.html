Tendinopathy or tear

Both produce pain over the bony point on the outside of the hip, and both hurt to lie on at night. The features that raise the possibility of a tear are about weakness and function rather than pain level.

  • A limp, or a hip that drops on the opposite side when you stand on the painful leg.
  • Difficulty standing on one leg for thirty seconds without the pelvis tilting or needing support.
  • Weakness lifting the leg sideways against resistance, rather than just pain doing it.
  • Pain and weakness that have not improved at all after a sustained, well-run strengthening programme.
  • Sometimes a history of a fall onto the hip, or a sudden giving-way moment.

Many tears are partial and degenerate slowly rather than tearing in one event, particularly in adults past middle age. A tear is also not automatically a surgical problem, and a good number of people do well with strengthening regardless.

How this relates to greater trochanteric pain

MD Fit's page on outer hip pain and greater trochanteric pain syndrome covers the far more common picture: irritated gluteal tendons, with the bursa usually an innocent bystander. This page is the smaller subset where the tendon has structurally torn.

They exist on a continuum. Tendons that have been overloaded for a long time become thickened and weakened, and a partial tear is the far end of that process rather than a separate disease. The treatment overlaps considerably, which is why Dr. Jared starts the same way in both cases. What changes is how long you persist before involving a specialist, and what is expected along the way.

The muscles around it

  • Gluteus medius is the main stabiliser of the pelvis when you stand on one leg. Its tendon attaches to the outer point of the hip, and this is where tears occur.
  • Gluteus minimus sits underneath and is frequently torn alongside it. It refers pain down the outside of the thigh, sometimes as far as the ankle, which gets confused with sciatica.
  • Tensor fasciae latae takes over when the glutes fail, becoming tight and overworked, and it contributes to the compression over the bony point.
  • Quadratus lumborum hitches the pelvis up to compensate for a weak hip, producing flank pain alongside.
  • Vastus lateralis attaches just below and often becomes tender in sympathy.

How MD Fit approaches it

Assess. Dr. Jared tests single-leg stance, strength lifting the leg sideways, gait, and tenderness over the attachment, and compares both sides carefully. Genuine weakness, rather than pain-limited effort, is the finding that prompts a conversation about imaging. Release. Hands-on work on tensor fasciae latae, quadratus lumborum and the lateral thigh, which reduces compression over the tendon. Correct. Positions that compress the tendon are removed first: crossing the legs, standing hanging on one hip, and sleeping on the painful side without a pillow between the knees. Homework. Isometric holds and controlled sideways loading from the free MD Fit app. Strengthen. Progressive hip abduction work in positions that load the tendon without compressing it, building toward single-leg control, stairs and walking distance.

Where weakness does not improve despite consistent work, Dr. Jared will recommend you see a physician or orthopaedic specialist. Imaging answers the question, and the options then range from continued rehabilitation to injection or surgical repair. That is a physician's decision, and conservative care continues alongside it either way.

What you can do now

  1. Sleep with a pillow between your knees, and avoid lying on the painful side. If you must, pad under the hip.
  2. Stop crossing your legs and stop standing with your weight hanging on one hip, both of which compress the tendon against the bone.
  3. Avoid stretches that pull the knee across the body. They compress exactly the structure that is irritated.
  4. Practise single-leg stance near a kitchen counter, keeping the pelvis level, and note whether it is the pain or the weakness that limits you.

Why this matters for falls

Gluteus medius is the muscle that keeps your pelvis level when you are on one leg, which is the position you are in for roughly half of every step you take. When it is weak or torn, walking becomes less stable, and in older adults that connects directly to falls. That is a large part of why Dr. Jared takes genuine hip abductor weakness seriously rather than treating it only as a pain problem, and why strength work continues regardless of what imaging shows.

Other causes of outer hip pain

Hip osteoarthritis produces groin pain with stiffness rather than point tenderness on the outside. Sciatica and lumbar nerve irritation refer down the leg with burning or numbness. Sacroiliac joint pain sits further back. A stress fracture of the hip in an older adult, particularly with pain on weight bearing after a fall, needs urgent imaging. Any hip pain with fever or inability to bear weight belongs with a physician promptly.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Gluteus medius: Along the belt line, over the SI joint, the buttock and the top of the hip.
  • Gluteus minimus: Outer hip down the side of the thigh and leg to the ankle, or down the back of the thigh and calf.
  • Tensor fasciae latae: Front-outer hip down the outside of the thigh, usually stopping at the knee.
  • Quadratus lumborum: Low back and flank, over the SI joint and buttock, and the point of the hip.
  • Vastus lateralis: Down the outer thigh to the outside of the knee; can make lying on that side painful.

When to get medical care first

  • Inability to bear weight on the leg after a fall: possible hip fracture, seek urgent medical care.
  • Sudden marked weakness lifting the leg sideways, or a hip that gives way and causes falls.
  • A hot, red, swollen hip with fever or feeling generally unwell.
  • Numbness in the groin or inner thighs, or new changes in bladder or bowel control: go to the emergency room.
  • Night pain that does not change with position, unexplained weight loss, or a history of cancer.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need an MRI?

Not usually at the outset. Imaging becomes useful when genuine weakness does not improve with consistent strengthening, because that is the point where the answer might change the plan. Your physician orders and interprets it.

Does a tear always need surgery?

No. Many partial tears are managed well with progressive strengthening. Surgical repair is considered where weakness is significant and has not responded, and that decision belongs with an orthopaedic specialist.

Why does it hurt so much at night?

Lying on the painful side compresses the tendon against the bone. Lying on the other side with the top leg falling across the body does the same thing, which is why a pillow between the knees often helps immediately.

Is it worth strengthening if the tendon is torn?

Yes. The surrounding muscles still support the pelvis, and strength directly affects walking stability and fall risk. Rehabilitation also improves outcomes if surgery is eventually chosen.
