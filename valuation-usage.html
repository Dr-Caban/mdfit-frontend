2. The levers, in plain terms

Each slider is one workbench field. The box at the top follows them live. The figures under each slider say what one step of it is worth.

3. What raises the number, in order

  1. Paying members. The platform has 83 members and 2 of them pay. Every member moved to a card subscription adds the amount shown under the first slider to the income and market approaches, and a few of them switch the weighting from "asset only" to "earning", which is when the number starts to move with the business rather than with the rebuild cost. Break-even on the platform cost is shown under the slider too.
  2. Software replacement cost. The appraiser sets this; what moves it is evidence. Everything in the inventory that could be checked from here was checked (section 4). Each $50,000 of defensible replacement cost adds $35,000 at 30% obsolescence.
  3. Company-specific risk. This is where the open items in the inventory bite: public repositories, no hosting BAA on file, developer IP carve-out unstated. Closing them lowers the rate; each point off the rate is worth about 4% more on the income approach once there are earnings.
  4. Obsolescence. Each 5 points less adds $7,500 at $150,000. Current runtimes and a passing test suite are the argument.
  5. Developer share. A fixed 15% off online net revenue while active. It only matters once there are earnings, and it is a contract term, not a slider the business controls.

What does not raise it: line counts (57% of the website's HTML is generated from data by scripts/seo/build.js), page counts, or endpoint counts on their own. They support the replacement-cost anchor; they are not value by themselves.
