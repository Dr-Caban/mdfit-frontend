Where the biceps femoris sends pain

Back of the knee on the outside and up the back of the thigh. People usually come in describing pain at the back of the thigh, outside of the knee or back of the knee.

What the biceps femoris does

Bends the knee, extends the hip and turns the lower leg outward.

  • Starts at: Long head: sitting bone (ischial tuberosity). Short head: back of the thigh bone.
  • Attaches to: Head of the fibula on the outside of the knee.
  • Nerve supply: Sciatic nerve: tibial part (long head) and common fibular part (short head), L5–S2.

Symptoms people describe

  • Ache at the back of the knee on the outside.
  • Pain up the back-outside of the thigh.
  • Tightness with sprinting or long strides.
  • Ache sitting for long periods.

What makes it worse

Sitting · Running or sports. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the biceps femoris is involved:

  • Is the pain on the outer back of the knee or the outer back of the thigh?
  • Does sprinting or long striding bring it on?
  • Is there a tender cord on the outside of the back of the knee?
  • Does sitting for a long time make it ache?
  • Does straightening the knee with the hip bent pull on the outside of the thigh?

Straight-leg raise with outward turn. Lie on your back and raise the straight leg with the foot turned slightly in, compared with the other side. Suggests this muscle if: Pulling or ache along the outer back of the thigh before the other side.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Hamstring strain
  • Lateral knee ligament injury
  • Fibular nerve irritation
  • Lumbar radiculopathy (S1)

Common causes

  • Sprinting and kicking.
  • Long sitting with knees bent.
  • Weak glutes making the hamstrings overwork.
  • Running without warm-up.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Outer hamstring release · 60–90 sec, twice daily
    Sit with a ball under the outer back of the thigh and slowly straighten and bend the knee.
  2. Hip Neuroglide (video) · 2 x 10, daily
    Keep the sciatic nerve gliding without stretching hard.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Pike Forward Fold (video) · 2 x 30 sec, daily
    Gentle fold with soft knees.
  2. Dowel Hip Hinge (video) · 2 x 10, daily
    Learn to hinge so the hamstrings lengthen under control.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Romanian Deadlift (video) · 3 x 8, twice weekly
    Slow lowering over 3 seconds.
  2. Hip Thrust (video) · 3 x 10, twice weekly
    Strong glutes take load off the hamstrings.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Romanian Deadlift, Dowel Hip Hinge, Pike Forward Fold.

Habits that help

  • Warm up before sprinting.
  • Break up long sitting every 30 minutes.

What to expect

Mild cases improve in 2 to 3 weeks; return to sprinting in 4 to 6 weeks.

When to see a professional

Book an evaluation after any pop or bruising, or if pain persists past 3 weeks. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • A sudden pop and bruising at the back of the thigh.
  • Foot drop or numbness on the top of the foot.
  • Calf swelling and warmth (possible clot).

Frequently asked questions

Can a tight biceps femoris cause pain at the back of the thigh?

Often, yes. Back of the knee on the outside and up the back of the thigh. That referred pain is why it gets blamed on the joint or the spot that hurts instead of the muscle.

How long does biceps femoris pain take to settle?

Mild cases improve in 2 to 3 weeks; return to sprinting in 4 to 6 weeks.

When should I see someone about it?

Book an evaluation after any pop or bruising, or if pain persists past 3 weeks.

What else can feel like biceps femoris pain?

Hamstring strain, Lateral knee ligament injury, Fibular nerve irritation. An evaluation separates muscle pain from these, and anything that needs a physician is referred on.
