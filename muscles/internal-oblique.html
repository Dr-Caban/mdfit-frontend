Where the internal oblique sends pain

Groin and lower abdomen, and across the front of the hip; can produce a deep groin ache mistaken for a hernia or hip problem. People usually come in describing pain at the belly or side of the abdomen.

What the internal oblique does

Rotates the trunk toward the same side, side-bends the trunk and compresses the abdomen. It works with the opposite external oblique as a rotational pair.

  • Starts at: The thoracolumbar fascia, the front two thirds of the iliac crest and the outer half of the inguinal ligament.
  • Attaches to: The lower borders of the last three or four ribs, the abdominal aponeurosis and the pubic crest.
  • Nerve supply: Anterior rami of thoracic nerves T7 to T12, plus the iliohypogastric and ilioinguinal nerves.

Symptoms people describe

  • Deep ache in the groin that is hard to localise.
  • Pain across the lower abdomen above the groin crease.
  • Discomfort on twisting toward the painful side.
  • Pain with coughing, sneezing or deep breathing.
  • Soreness in the side of the abdomen after rotational sport.
  • A pulling sensation at the front of the hip with no hip joint findings.

What makes it worse

Turning your head or body · Deep breaths or coughing · Running or sports · Bending or lifting. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the internal oblique is involved:

  • Do you have a deep groin ache that imaging of the hip has not explained?
  • Is it worse when you twist toward the sore side?
  • Does coughing or sneezing provoke it?
  • Do you play a rotational sport such as golf, tennis or pickleball?
  • Is the side of your abdomen tender to press?

Resisted rotation. Sitting upright, cross your arms over your chest and try to rotate toward the painful side while a hand resists at the shoulder. Suggests this muscle if: Pain in the side of the abdomen or groin rather than in the back or hip.

Side wall palpation. Press into the side of the abdomen between the lower ribs and the top of the pelvis, with the abdomen relaxed. Suggests this muscle if: Tender bands reproduce the groin or lower abdominal pain.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Inguinal hernia, which must be excluded.
  • Hip joint problems including labral tear.
  • Adductor-related groin pain.
  • Nerve entrapment of the ilioinguinal nerve.
  • Gynaecological or urinary causes.

Common causes

  • Rotational sport, especially an increase in volume.
  • Heavy lifting with a twist.
  • Prolonged coughing.
  • Pregnancy and the postpartum period.
  • Sustained sitting with the trunk rotated, as at a poorly arranged desk.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Reduce rotational load · One to two weeks
    Pause the sport or lift that provokes it rather than working through it.
  2. Rib and abdominal breathing (video: 90/90 Breathing) · 5 minutes daily
    Reduces the constant abdominal bracing that keeps the side wall loaded.
  3. Gentle side opening (video: Sit Turn Lean) · Ten slow repetitions each way
    Small, pain-free rotation to keep the tissue moving.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Open book rotation (video: Thread The Needle) · Ten each side, daily
    Restores rotation through the trunk rather than forcing it from the low back.
  2. Thoracic rotation (video: Thoracic Rotation Seated) · Ten each side
    Most rotation should come from the upper back; when it does not, the abdominal wall takes the load.
  3. Hip mobility (video: 90/90 Hip Switch) · Ten each side
    A stiff hip forces the trunk to rotate more than it should.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Anti-rotation holds (video: Pallof Press) · Three holds of 20 seconds each side
    Builds the capacity to resist rotation before producing it.
  2. Rotational press (video: Pallof Press With Rotation) · Three sets of eight each side
    Adds controlled rotation under load.
  3. Progressive rotational power (video: Rotational Medicine Ball Throw) · Three sets of six each side, twice weekly
    Only once the controlled work is pain free; this is the return-to-sport step.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Pallof Press With Rotation, Thread The Needle, Sit Turn Lean.

Habits that help

  • Build rotational sport volume gradually, especially at the start of a season.
  • Warm up rotation before golf, tennis or pickleball.
  • Avoid lifting and twisting in one movement.
  • Have any groin bulge assessed before returning to sport.

What to expect

Abdominal wall strains usually settle in two to four weeks with relative rest and graded rotational loading. Returning to full rotational sport too early is the common reason they recur.

When to see a professional

Groin pain needs a physician first if there is a bulge, fever, urinary or testicular symptoms. Book an evaluation for groin pain that has not settled in three weeks. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • A bulge in the groin that appears on straining or coughing.
  • Groin pain with fever or urinary symptoms.
  • Testicular pain or swelling, which needs urgent assessment.
  • Abdominal pain with vomiting or a rigid abdomen, which needs emergency care.
  • Groin pain following a significant fall, which may indicate a fracture.
