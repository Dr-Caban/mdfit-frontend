Where the soleus sends pain

Heel and Achilles tendon, lower calf; can contribute to plantar fasciitis. People usually come in describing pain at the lower calf, inner ankle and arch or sole of the foot. It is also a common hidden driver behind Plantar Fasciitis & Heel Pain and Achilles Tendon Pain.

What the soleus does

Plantarflexes the foot (points the toes down). It is a powerful postural muscle, active whenever you are standing to prevent you from falling forward.

  • Starts at: Posterior (back) surface of the tibia and fibula, underneath the gastrocnemius.
  • Attaches to: Calcaneus (heel bone) via the Achilles tendon.
  • Nerve supply: Tibial nerve (L5, S1, S2).

Symptoms people describe

  • A deep, aching pain in the lower half of the calf.
  • Pain that refers directly into the heel and Achilles tendon area.
  • Pain that feels like 'plantar fasciitis' but is located more in the heel pad.
  • Stiffness and pain that is worse after long periods of standing or walking.
  • Pain that is often a constant, low-grade ache rather than a sharp, acute pain.

What makes it worse

Standing or walking · Running or sports · Stairs, squats or hills. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the soleus is involved:

  • Is your pain a deep ache in your *lower* calf, heel, or Achilles tendon?
  • Does your pain get significantly worse after prolonged standing or walking, like a day of shopping or sightseeing?
  • Does the pain feel similar to plantar fasciitis, but more in the back of the heel than the bottom of the foot?
  • Is the pain more of a constant, nagging ache than a sharp, stabbing pain?
  • Do you feel pain when you press deeply into the sides of your Achilles tendon, into the fleshy part of your lower calf?

Bent-Knee Calf Raise Test. Stand on one leg with the knee significantly bent (about 45 degrees), holding a wall for balance. Try to lift your heel off the ground. This isolates the soleus. Suggests this muscle if: Pain in the lower calf or heel, or significant weakness/inability to lift the heel, points to a soleus issue.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Achilles tendinopathy
  • Plantar fasciitis
  • Heel spur or stress fracture of the calcaneus
  • Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT) - a medical emergency
  • Lumbar radiculopathy (S1/S2 nerve root)

Common causes

  • Jobs or activities that require prolonged standing on hard surfaces.
  • Running long distances, as the soleus is a primary endurance muscle.
  • Wearing unsupportive footwear with poor shock absorption.
  • Driving for long hours, keeping the foot on the pedals.
  • A gait pattern with excessive forward lean or a heavy heel strike.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Bent-Knee Calf Stretch · 2 x 30 sec per side, twice daily
    Face a wall and step one foot back. Keeping both heels on the floor, bend both knees until you feel a deep stretch in the lower calf of the back leg.
  2. Self-Massage Lower Calf · 1-2 minutes per side, daily
    Sit on the floor and use your thumbs or a massage ball to apply firm, sustained pressure to tender spots in the lower, fleshy part of your calf, to the sides of the Achilles tendon.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Ankle Neuroglide (video) · 2 x 15 reps per side, daily
    Follow the video instructions for this gentle nerve mobilization exercise. This helps ensure the nerves in the lower leg are moving freely.
  2. Seated Calf Stretch · 2 x 30 sec per side, daily
    Sit on the floor with one leg extended. Loop a towel or band around the ball of your foot and gently pull back, keeping your knee slightly bent to target the soleus.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Seated Calf Raises · 3 x 15-20, every other day
    Sit on a chair with your feet flat on the floor. Place a weight (e.g., a heavy book or dumbbell) on your knees. Slowly lift your heels as high as possible, then lower them back down.
  2. Eccentric Calf Drops (Bent Knee) (video: Eccentric Calf Drops) · 3 x 15 per side, every other day
    Perform as shown in the video, but maintain a bent knee throughout the movement to place more emphasis on the soleus muscle during the lowering phase.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Eccentric Calf Drops, Ankle Neuroglide, Heel Walk.

Habits that help

  • Wear supportive shoes, especially if you stand or walk for long periods.
  • Use an anti-fatigue mat if you have a standing desk or work on a hard floor.
  • Avoid standing with your knees locked back.
  • On long drives, use cruise control when safe to do so and move your ankle around periodically.

What to expect

Soleus issues can be persistent due to their constant use in standing and walking. Expect gradual improvement over 3-6 weeks with consistent stretching and strengthening. Full resolution may take longer.

When to see a professional

Seek immediate medical attention for any red flag symptoms. Book an appointment if pain is preventing you from walking normally or if there is no improvement after 3-4 weeks of dedicated self-care. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Sudden, severe calf pain with swelling, redness, and warmth (could be a DVT - go to ER).
  • A 'pop' or tearing sensation in the Achilles area (possible Achilles rupture).
  • Inability to bear weight or walk.
  • Pain that is present and severe even at rest, especially at night.

Often, yes. Heel and Achilles tendon, lower calf; can contribute to plantar fasciitis. That referred pain is why it gets blamed on the joint or the spot that hurts instead of the muscle.

Soleus issues can be persistent due to their constant use in standing and walking. Expect gradual improvement over 3-6 weeks with consistent stretching and strengthening. Full resolution may take longer.

When should I see someone about it?
