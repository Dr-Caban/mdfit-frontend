Where the longus colli sends pain

Deep front of the neck and throat, sometimes a sore-throat feeling or discomfort swallowing. Weakness here shows up as aching elsewhere in the neck. People usually come in describing pain at the front of the neck.

What the longus colli does

Flexes the neck and, more importantly, holds the cervical curve steady so the head is balanced rather than held up by the muscles at the back.

  • Starts at: The front of the vertebral bodies and transverse processes from the atlas down to T3.
  • Attaches to: The front of the vertebral bodies and transverse processes several levels above, in three overlapping parts.
  • Nerve supply: Anterior rami of the cervical spinal nerves C2 to C6.

Symptoms people describe

  • Deep discomfort at the front of the neck that is hard to point to.
  • A sore or tight throat feeling with no infection.
  • Neck fatigue rather than sharp pain, especially late in the day.
  • Aching at the back of the neck and shoulders, because the extensors are doing this muscle's job.
  • A head that feels heavy after screen work.
  • Difficulty holding a chin tuck for more than a few seconds.

What makes it worse

Desk, phone or screens · Turning your head or body · Stress. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the longus colli is involved:

  • Does your neck feel tired and heavy rather than sharply painful?
  • Can you hold a gentle chin tuck for ten seconds without the front of your neck shaking?
  • Do you get a throat tightness that no one can find a cause for?
  • Does your head drift forward without you noticing at a screen?
  • Have you had whiplash or a neck injury in the past?

Chin nod endurance. Lie on your back with knees bent. Gently nod the chin as if saying yes, lift the head a centimetre off the floor and hold. Suggests this muscle if: You cannot hold for ten seconds, the chin pokes forward, or the front of the neck shakes or burns.

Wall stand check. Stand with heels, buttocks and upper back against a wall and try to bring the back of the head to the wall without lifting the chin. Suggests this muscle if: The head will not reach without tipping back, suggesting the deep flexors are not holding the head position.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Throat or thyroid problems.
  • Cervical disc or joint irritation.
  • Thoracic outlet symptoms.
  • Swallowing disorders.
  • Reflux, which can cause throat discomfort.

Common causes

  • Long hours with the head forward at a screen or phone.
  • Whiplash, which commonly inhibits these muscles.
  • Prolonged bracing with stress.
  • A rounded upper back.
  • Long periods in a collar or immobilised after injury.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Support the head during rest · Daily
    Use a pillow that keeps the neck in line with the spine, so these muscles are not loaded overnight.
  2. Reduce sustained holding (video: Walk Break Protocol Seated) · Every 30 minutes
    Stand and walk briefly. The deep flexors fatigue with static holding more than with movement.
  3. Breathing reset (video: 90/90 Breathing) · 5 minutes daily
    Quiet breathing reduces the accessory neck muscle activity that masks weakness here.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Chin nods, supported (video: Chin Nod) · Ten slow repetitions, twice daily
    Lying down, nod gently without lifting the head. Quality matters far more than range.
  2. Seated chin glide (video: Chin Glide Seated) · Two sets of ten, several times daily
    Bring the head back over the shoulders without tilting.
  3. Thoracic mobility (video: Thoracic Open Book) · Ten each side, daily
    The neck cannot sit well on a stiff upper back.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Deep flexor endurance holds (video: Deep Cervical Flexor Nods Seated) · Ten second holds, build to ten repetitions, daily
    This is the main exercise for this muscle. Progress by holding longer, not by lifting higher.
  2. Scapular support (video: Floor Slide Scapular Retract) · Two sets of ten, daily
    The shoulder blades give the neck something to work from.
  3. Loaded carries (video: Farmer Carry) · Three carries of 30 seconds, twice weekly
    Teaches the whole trunk and neck to hold position under load.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Deep Cervical Flexor Nods Seated, Chin Nod, Chin Glide Seated.

Habits that help

  • Check your head position whenever you catch yourself at a screen.
  • Keep phones at eye level.
  • Sleep with one pillow that supports the neck rather than pushing the head forward.
  • Return to deep flexor work after any neck injury, since these muscles switch off easily.

What to expect

Endurance in these muscles builds slowly. Most people notice less neck fatigue within three to four weeks of daily work, with fuller change over two to three months.

When to see a professional

Book an evaluation if neck fatigue is not improving after a month of consistent work, or sooner if there is arm numbness, weakness or any swallowing or voice change. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Difficulty swallowing that is getting worse.
  • A lump in the neck or thyroid area.
  • Hoarseness lasting more than three weeks.
  • Neck pain with fever and a stiff neck.
  • Numbness, weakness or clumsiness in the arms or hands.
