Where the masseter sends pain

Jaw, cheek, in front of the ear and the back teeth. Can feel like an earache or toothache. People usually come in describing pain at the ear area. It is also a common hidden driver behind Jaw Pain & TMJ Dysfunction.

What the masseter does

The primary muscle for closing your jaw. It is the strongest muscle in the body by weight, used for chewing and clenching.

  • Starts at: The zygomatic arch (your cheekbone).
  • Attaches to: The outer surface of the angle and ramus of your mandible (your jawbone).
  • Nerve supply: Trigeminal Nerve (Cranial Nerve V, Mandibular Division).

Symptoms people describe

  • Deep ache or pain directly over the jaw joint, in front of the ear.
  • Pain in the cheek that can radiate up to the temple or down into the jaw.
  • A feeling of fullness or pain inside the ear, like an earache.
  • Aching in the lower back teeth (molars), often leading to unnecessary dental visits.
  • Limited ability to open your mouth, or pain when yawning.
  • A 'clicking' or 'popping' sound in the jaw joint (TMJ).

What makes it worse

Clenching or chewing · Stress. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the masseter is involved:

  • Do you have pain right in front of your ear, especially when you chew?
  • Does it feel like you have an earache or toothache in your back molars, but the doctor/dentist can't find a cause?
  • Do you wake up with a sore, tired jaw?
  • Is your jaw pain accompanied by a clicking or popping sound when you open your mouth?
  • Do you find yourself clenching your teeth during the day when you are stressed or concentrating?

Masseter Palpation Test. Place your fingers on your cheek, just above the angle of your jaw. Clench your teeth to feel the muscle contract, then relax. Press deeply into the muscle belly to find tender, ropy bands. Suggests this muscle if: Pressing on a tender spot reproduces your jaw, cheek, ear, or tooth pain.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Temporomandibular Joint (TMJ) Dysfunction.
  • Dental problems (cavities, abscess, cracked tooth).
  • Ear infection (otitis media).
  • Parotid gland inflammation (salivary gland).
  • Trigeminal neuralgia.

Common causes

  • Bruxism (clenching or grinding teeth), often related to stress.
  • Habitual gum chewing, nail biting, or chewing on pens.
  • Malocclusion (a bad bite) or recent extensive dental work.
  • Direct trauma to the jaw.
  • Forward head posture, which changes the resting position of the jaw.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Masseter Self-Massage · 2-3 minutes per side, as needed
    With your jaw relaxed, use your knuckles or fingertips to apply deep, slow stripping strokes from your cheekbone down to your jawline. You can also do this from inside your mouth (with a clean finger or glove) to release the deeper part of the muscle.
  2. Stress Reduction Breathing (video: Box Breathing) · 5 minutes, 1-2 times daily or when stressed
    Calming your nervous system is key to reducing clenching. Inhale for 4 seconds, hold for 4, exhale for 4, and hold for 4.
  3. Gentle Jaw Mobility · 10 reps, several times daily
    Place your tongue on the roof of your mouth. Slowly open and close your jaw without letting your tongue lose contact. This promotes a healthy hinge motion.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Postural Correction (video: Chin Glide Seated) · 2 x 10 reps, several times daily
    A neutral head position is crucial for a relaxed jaw. Gently glide your chin straight back to stack your head over your shoulders.
  2. Tongue Posture Training · Throughout the day
    Consciously rest your tongue on the roof of your mouth, with teeth slightly apart and lips closed. This is the 'freeway space' that allows the masseter to relax.
  3. Jaw Lateral Movement · 2 x 10 reps per side, daily
    Look in a mirror. Slowly glide your lower jaw from side to side without pain. Keep the movement smooth and controlled.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Deep Neck Flexor Activation (video: Deep Cervical Flexor Nods Seated) · 2 x 10 sec holds, 5 reps, daily
    Strengthening the supporting neck muscles helps unload the jaw. Perform a gentle chin tuck and hold.
  2. Postural Endurance (video: Seated Desk Posture Reset) · Every 30 minutes at your desk
    Use this sequence to combat the forward head posture that contributes to jaw tension.
  3. Core Stability (video: Dead Bug) · 3 x 10 reps per side, every other day
    A stable trunk provides a solid base for your head and neck, reducing the need for compensatory tension in the jaw.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Box Breathing, Chin Glide Seated.

Habits that help

  • Adopt the 'lips together, teeth apart' mantra throughout the day.
  • Avoid chewing gum and tough foods like bagels or steak while the jaw is painful.
  • Try not to rest your chin in your hand.
  • If you grind your teeth at night, consult your dentist about a custom night guard.

What to expect

Pain can decrease significantly within 1-2 weeks with massage and habit changes. Full recovery and preventing recurrence depends on managing stress and postural habits, which can take 4-8 weeks to solidify.

When to see a professional

Book an evaluation if you have significant clicking, locking, or deviation of your jaw, or if pain persists for more than 3 weeks despite your best efforts. A TMJ specialist or physical therapist can provide more specific care. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Inability to open or close your jaw (a 'locked' jaw).
  • Sudden, severe swelling in the jaw or cheek area.
  • Pain accompanied by fever, which could indicate an infection.
  • Numbness in your jaw, chin, or lip.
