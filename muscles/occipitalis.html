Where the occipitalis sends pain

Ache at the back of the head that can shoot through the skull to behind the eye, with scalp tenderness. People usually come in describing pain at the back of the head, forehead or behind or around the eye.

What the occipitalis does

Pulls the scalp backward. Works with the frontalis muscle to raise the eyebrows and express surprise.

  • Starts at: Occipital bone and mastoid process.
  • Attaches to: Galea aponeurotica (a tough layer of fibrous tissue covering the top of the skull).
  • Nerve supply: Facial nerve (CN VII).

Symptoms people describe

  • Aching pain at the very back of the head.
  • Pain that feels like it shoots through the skull to behind one eye.
  • Tenderness of the scalp, making it uncomfortable to brush hair or wear a hat.
  • A feeling of pressure or a 'skull cap' of pain.
  • Pain is often triggered or worsened by stress and clenching.

What makes it worse

Stress · Desk, phone or screens. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the occipitalis is involved:

  • Is your scalp at the back of your head very tender to the touch?
  • Does the pain sometimes feel like it's shooting from the back of your head straight through to your eye?
  • Do you notice the pain more when you are stressed or clenching your jaw?
  • Does the pain feel superficial, like it's in the scalp itself, rather than deep in the neck joints?
  • Does wearing a tight hat, ponytail, or headband make the pain worse?

Scalp Palpation. Use your fingertips to gently but firmly press and move the skin over the bone at the back of your head. Explore the area for any exquisitely tender spots. Suggests this muscle if: Finding a specific point of tenderness that, when pressed, reproduces your familiar head or eye pain.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Occipital neuralgia
  • Tension-type headache
  • Suboccipital muscle pain
  • Giant cell arteritis (rare, but important)

Common causes

  • Chronic forward head posture, which puts tension on the entire back line of the body.
  • Emotional stress leading to unconscious scalp muscle tension.
  • Clenching the jaw or grinding teeth (bruxism).
  • Prolonged desk work or screen time.
  • Whiplash or other head/neck trauma.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Occipitalis Release · 2-3 minutes, once or twice daily
    Place a lacrosse ball or your fingertips on the tender spots at the back of your head. Apply gentle, sustained pressure or make small circles without sliding on the skin.
  2. Chin Glides (video: Chin Glide Seated) · 2 x 10 reps, several times daily
    Gently glide your head backward over your shoulders, keeping your eyes level. This reduces the tension on the back of the scalp.
  3. Box Breathing (video) · 5 minutes, once or twice daily
    Inhale for 4 seconds, hold for 4, exhale for 4, hold for 4. This technique helps lower stress and reduce unconscious muscle clenching.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Neck Neuroglide (video: Neck Neuroglide Seated) · 1 x 10 slow reps each side, daily
    This gentle nerve mobilization helps reduce tension patterns that can affect the scalp and neck.
  2. Seated Posture Reset (video: Seated Desk Posture Reset) · Hold for 30 seconds, every hour
    Actively correcting your posture reduces the chronic strain on the muscles at the back of your head and neck.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Deep Neck Flexor Activation (video: Deep Cervical Flexor Nods Seated) · 3 x 10 reps, every other day
    Strengthening the muscles at the front of your neck helps to balance the tension at the back, improving head posture.
  2. Dumbbell Row (video) · 3 x 10-12 reps, twice a week
    Building strength in your upper back creates a strong foundation for your neck and head, offloading the scalp muscles. Use a light weight.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Chin Glide Seated, Neck Neuroglide Seated.

Habits that help

  • Be mindful of jaw clenching during the day; let your jaw hang loose with teeth slightly apart.
  • Avoid tight hairstyles, hats, or headbands that put pressure on the scalp.
  • Ensure your desk setup promotes a neutral head position.
  • Practice stress-reduction techniques like meditation or short walks.

What to expect

Scalp tenderness and referred pain often improve within 1-2 weeks with consistent release work and postural correction. Full resolution depends on managing underlying stress and habits.

When to see a professional

If your headache is severe, doesn't respond to these measures, or is accompanied by vision changes, please book an appointment for a thorough evaluation. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Sudden, severe 'thunderclap' headache.
  • Scalp tenderness with fever, weight loss, or vision changes (especially in older adults).
  • Headache that worsens with coughing or straining.
  • Pain that started after a significant head injury.

Frequently asked questions

Can a tight occipitalis cause pain at the back of the head?

Often, yes. Ache at the back of the head that can shoot through the skull to behind the eye, with scalp tenderness. That referred pain is why it gets blamed on the joint or the spot that hurts instead of the muscle.

How long does occipitalis pain take to settle?

Scalp tenderness and referred pain often improve within 1-2 weeks with consistent release work and postural correction. Full resolution depends on managing underlying stress and habits.

When should I see someone about it?

If your headache is severe, doesn't respond to these measures, or is accompanied by vision changes, please book an appointment for a thorough evaluation.

What else can feel like occipitalis pain?

Occipital neuralgia, Tension-type headache, Suboccipital muscle pain. An evaluation separates muscle pain from these, and anything that needs a physician is referred on.
