Where the gluteus medius sends pain

Along the belt line, over the SI joint, the buttock and the top of the hip. People usually come in describing pain at the upper buttock at the belt line, outside of the hip, si joint and back of the pelvis or middle of the buttock. It is also a common hidden driver behind Low Back Pain.

What the gluteus medius does

Abducts the hip (moves leg out to the side) and stabilizes the pelvis. It's crucial for walking, running, and single-leg balance.

  • Starts at: Outer surface of the ilium (pelvic bone), between the posterior and anterior gluteal lines.
  • Attaches to: Greater trochanter of the femur (the bony point on the outside of your hip).
  • Nerve supply: Superior gluteal nerve (L4, L5, S1).

Symptoms people describe

  • A persistent, nagging ache along the belt line or top of the buttock.
  • Pain that can wrap around to the bony point on the side of the hip.
  • Pain that refers over the sacroiliac (SI) joint, often mistaken for an SI joint problem.
  • Difficulty and pain when lying on the affected side at night.
  • A feeling of weakness or instability in the hip, especially when climbing stairs or running.

What makes it worse

Standing or walking · Lying on it at night · Running or sports · Stairs, squats or hills. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the gluteus medius is involved:

  • Is your pain located high on your buttock, right along your belt line?
  • Does it hurt to lie on that side at night, forcing you to switch positions?
  • Do you feel pain or a sense of your hip 'giving way' when you stand on one leg to put on pants?
  • Does walking, standing for a long time, or climbing stairs make the ache worse?
  • Have you been told you have 'SI joint pain' but treatments for it haven't helped?

Single Leg Stance Test. Stand in front of a mirror and lift one foot off the ground, balancing on the painful leg for 30 seconds. Watch your hips in the mirror. Suggests this muscle if: If the hip of your lifted leg drops down, or if your familiar pain is reproduced in the standing leg's buttock within 30 seconds, it suggests gluteus medius weakness.

Resisted Hip Abduction. Lie on your good side with your legs straight. Have a partner gently press down on your top ankle as you try to lift the leg toward the ceiling. Suggests this muscle if: Significant weakness or reproduction of your pain compared to the other side points to gluteus medius involvement.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Sacroiliac (SI) joint dysfunction
  • Lumbar facet joint syndrome
  • Greater trochanteric pain syndrome (hip bursitis)
  • Lumbar radiculopathy (sciatica)

Common causes

  • Sitting for long periods with legs crossed.
  • Standing with all your weight shifted onto one hip ('hip-sitting').
  • Running on a banked surface (like the edge of a road) or always running the same direction on a track.
  • Weakness from inactivity, leading to overuse when activity is resumed.
  • Sleeping on one side without a pillow between the knees.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Glute Medius Release (Ball) · 2-3 minutes per side, daily
    Lie on your side with a lacrosse or tennis ball under the fleshy part of your upper buttock, just below the pelvic bone. Gently roll to find tender spots and hold with steady pressure.
  2. Clamshells (Isometric) · 3 x 10 sec holds, twice daily
    Lie on your side with knees bent and hips stacked. Without moving your pelvis, gently lift your top knee. Hold at the top without pain, focusing on the muscle contraction.
  3. 90/90 Hip Switch (video) · 2 x 10 reps, daily
    Perform this movement slowly and gently to improve hip mobility without stressing the irritated muscle.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Standing Clamshell (video) · 2 x 15 reps per side, every other day
    This exercise activates the gluteus medius in a functional, weight-bearing position. Focus on keeping your pelvis level.
  2. Glute Walk (video) · 2 x 10 steps each direction, every other day
    Place a resistance band around your ankles or knees. Take slow, controlled side steps, keeping your toes pointed forward and your torso upright.
  3. Figure-4 Stretch · 2 x 30 sec per side, daily
    Lie on your back and cross one ankle over the opposite knee. Gently pull the bottom thigh toward your chest until you feel a stretch in the buttock of the crossed leg.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Standing Hip Abduction/Adduction (video) · 3 x 12 reps per side, every other day
    Use a cable or band to add resistance as you move your leg out to the side. Maintain a stable torso and pelvis throughout the movement.
  2. Single Leg Glute Bridge (video) · 3 x 10 reps per side, every other day
    This variation challenges pelvic stability, forcing the gluteus medius to work harder to prevent the hip from dropping.
  3. Step Up (video) · 3 x 10 reps per side, every other day
    Focus on keeping your knee aligned over your foot and your pelvis level as you step up. This is a key functional strengthener for the glute medius.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Standing Clamshell, Glute Walk, Standing Hip Abduction/Adduction.

Habits that help

  • Avoid 'hip-sitting'; stand with your weight evenly distributed on both feet.
  • When sitting, avoid crossing your legs for long periods.
  • Sleep with a pillow between your knees if you are a side-sleeper.
  • When carrying a heavy bag or child, switch sides frequently.

What to expect

You should notice less pain at night and with standing within 2-3 weeks. Consistent strengthening for 6-8 weeks is often needed to build the endurance to prevent the pain from returning.

When to see a professional

Book an evaluation if you have a 'snapping' sensation in your hip, if pain persists beyond 3-4 weeks, or if you have significant weakness that makes you feel unstable. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Numbness or tingling that travels down the leg.
  • Loss of bowel or bladder control.
  • Pain that is constant and severe, even at rest.
  • History of a recent fall or trauma to the hip.

Frequently asked questions

Can a tight gluteus medius cause pain at the upper buttock at the belt line?

Often, yes. Along the belt line, over the SI joint, the buttock and the top of the hip. That referred pain is why it gets blamed on the joint or the spot that hurts instead of the muscle.

How long does gluteus medius pain take to settle?

You should notice less pain at night and with standing within 2-3 weeks. Consistent strengthening for 6-8 weeks is often needed to build the endurance to prevent the pain from returning.

When should I see someone about it?

Book an evaluation if you have a 'snapping' sensation in your hip, if pain persists beyond 3-4 weeks, or if you have significant weakness that makes you feel unstable.

What else can feel like gluteus medius pain?

Sacroiliac (SI) joint dysfunction, Lumbar facet joint syndrome, Greater trochanteric pain syndrome (hip bursitis). An evaluation separates muscle pain from these, and anything that needs a physician is referred on.
