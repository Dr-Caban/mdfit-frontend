Where the supraspinatus sends pain

Deep ache at the top and side of the shoulder that can run down the outside of the arm to the elbow. Worse reaching overhead. People usually come in describing pain at the top of the shoulder, outside of the shoulder or upper arm, deep inside the shoulder joint or on the shoulder blade. It is also a common hidden driver behind Tennis Elbow (Lateral Epicondylitis).

What the supraspinatus does

Initiates lifting your arm out to the side (abduction) for the first 15-30 degrees. It also holds the 'ball' of your arm bone securely in the shoulder 'socket'.

  • Starts at: The top surface of your shoulder blade (supraspinous fossa).
  • Attaches to: The top of your upper arm bone (greater tubercle of the humerus).
  • Nerve supply: Suprascapular nerve (C5, C6).

Symptoms people describe

  • A deep, nagging ache felt on the top or outer part of your shoulder.
  • Pain that refers down the side of your arm, often stopping around the mid-arm or elbow.
  • A sharp pinch or pain when you try to lift your arm overhead, like reaching into a high cabinet.
  • Weakness when trying to lift your arm out to the side.
  • Pain at night, especially when you lie on the affected shoulder.
  • Difficulty with tasks like brushing your hair or putting on a coat.

What makes it worse

Reaching overhead · Lying on it at night. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the supraspinatus is involved:

  • Do you feel a sharp pain in your shoulder when you start to lift your arm out to the side, especially between 60 and 120 degrees (a 'painful arc')?
  • Is the pain a deep ache on the outside of your shoulder that sometimes travels down your arm?
  • Does it hurt to lie on that shoulder at night?
  • Do you feel weakness when trying to hold a heavy object out to your side?
  • Is it difficult to reach behind your back, for example, to tuck in a shirt?

Empty Can Test. Lift your affected arm forward and out to the side about 45 degrees, keeping your elbow straight. Turn your thumb down as if emptying a can. Gently try to resist as you use your other hand to push down on your arm. Suggests this muscle if: Significant pain or weakness in the shoulder compared to the other side.

Drop Arm Test. Use your good arm to help lift your painful arm straight out to the side, parallel to the floor. Now, try to slowly and smoothly lower it back down to your side. Suggests this muscle if: You are unable to lower the arm slowly, or it suddenly drops. This can indicate a more significant tear and warrants a professional evaluation.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Subacromial bursitis (often occurs alongside supraspinatus issues).
  • Biceps tendonitis.
  • Cervical radiculopathy (a pinched nerve in the neck referring pain to the shoulder).
  • Shoulder joint arthritis.
  • Acromioclavicular (AC) joint sprain.

Common causes

  • Repetitive overhead activities (e.g., painting, swimming, throwing).
  • Lifting an object that is too heavy or with poor technique.
  • A fall onto an outstretched arm or directly onto the shoulder.
  • Poor posture (rounded shoulders) which narrows the space for the tendon.
  • Age-related degeneration or 'wear and tear' of the tendon.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Pendulum Swings · 2 x 30 sec in each direction, several times daily
    Lean over, supporting your good arm on a table. Let the painful arm hang down and use your body to create small, gentle circles. This provides gentle traction and movement without using the muscle.
  2. Supraspinatus Pressure Release · 60-90 seconds, daily
    Place a lacrosse ball between your shoulder blade and a wall. Find the muscle just above the 'spine' of your shoulder blade and apply sustained, gentle pressure to any tender spots.
  3. Scapular Wall Slides (video: Floor Slide Scapular Retract) · 2 x 10 reps, daily
    Perform this standing against a wall. Keep your forearms on the wall and slide them up and down, focusing on moving your shoulder blades without shrugging. Only go as high as you can without pain.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Isometric External Rotation · 3 x 10 sec holds, daily
    Stand in a doorway with your elbow bent to 90 degrees. Gently press the back of your hand into the door frame as if rotating outward. You should feel the muscle contract without any joint movement.
  2. Banded External Rotation (video: Rotator Cuff External Rotation) · 2 x 15 reps, every other day
    Keep your elbow pinned to your side at a 90-degree angle. Rotate your forearm outward against light band resistance. The movement should come from your shoulder, not your wrist.
  3. Scapular Mobility (video: Scapular CARs) · 1 x 5 circles each way, daily
    Move your shoulder blade through its full range of motion—up, back, down, and forward—while keeping your arm relatively still. This improves shoulder blade control.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Scaption Raises · 3 x 10-12 reps, twice weekly
    With a very light weight (or no weight), raise your arm with your thumb up at a 30-45 degree angle in front of your body. Only go to shoulder height. This is the natural plane of the shoulder blade.
  2. Rotator Cuff Endurance (video: Rotator Cuff 90 Band Vibration) · 3 x 20 sec holds, twice weekly
    Hold your arm in the 'stop sign' position with a band providing tension. Perform small, quick vibrations to challenge the stability of the rotator cuff.
  3. Push-Up Plus (video: Scap Push Ups) · 3 x 10 reps, twice weekly
    In a push-up or quadruped position, keep your elbows straight and just move your shoulder blades. Push the floor away to spread them apart, then let them sink together. This strengthens the serratus anterior, a key shoulder stabilizer.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Rotator Cuff External Rotation, Abducted External Rotation, Rotator Cuff 90 Band Vibration.

Habits that help

  • Avoid sleeping directly on the painful shoulder. Try sleeping on your back or other side with a pillow to hug.
  • When lifting, keep your elbows close to your body to reduce strain on the shoulder.
  • Break up overhead tasks with frequent rest periods.
  • Strengthen your upper back muscles (rows, face pulls) to improve posture and shoulder mechanics.

What to expect

Minor strains can feel better in 2-4 weeks. More significant tendinopathy or irritation can take 6-12 weeks of consistent, progressive rehabilitation to see significant improvement in strength and function.

When to see a professional

Book an evaluation if you have significant weakness (e.g., a positive Drop Arm Test), if the pain resulted from a specific trauma, or if there is no improvement after 3-4 weeks of dedicated rehab. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Inability to lift your arm at all after an injury.
  • A visible deformity or 'lump' on your shoulder or collarbone after a fall.
  • Pain accompanied by fever, redness, or swelling, suggesting an infection.
  • Pain that is constant and severe, even at rest.
