Where the suboccipitals sends pain

A band of headache from the base of the skull wrapping to behind the eye and the forehead. Often mistaken for migraine. People usually come in describing pain at the behind or around the eye, base of the skull, back of the neck, forehead or back of the head.

What the suboccipitals does

Tilts the head back, turns the head side-to-side, and makes fine adjustments to head position. Key for head posture and balance.

  • Starts at: Top two vertebrae in the neck (C1 and C2).
  • Attaches to: Occipital bone at the base of the skull.
  • Nerve supply: Suboccipital nerve (C1).

Symptoms people describe

  • A headache that feels like a tight band starting at the base of your skull.
  • Pain that wraps forward to behind your eye or into your forehead.
  • The headache can feel deep, achy, and persistent, sometimes mistaken for a migraine.
  • Stiffness and reduced ability to nod or tuck your chin.
  • Pain is often worse when sitting at a desk or looking down at a phone.
  • May disrupt sleep if pillows don't support the neck properly.

What makes it worse

Desk, phone or screens · Stress · Turning your head or body. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the suboccipitals is involved:

  • Does your headache feel like a tight band wrapping from the back of your head to behind your eye?
  • Is the pain worse when you spend a long time looking down at a screen or book?
  • Do you feel stiffness mostly when trying to tuck your chin to your chest?
  • Does pressing gently into the hollows at the very base of your skull reproduce the headache feeling?
  • Does your pain feel different from a typical 'stiff neck' on the side, and more like a deep headache?

Chin Tuck Test. Sit or stand tall. Gently tuck your chin towards your throat as if making a double chin, without looking down. Keep the back of your neck long. Suggests this muscle if: Pain or a strong pulling sensation at the base of your skull, or an inability to perform the movement smoothly.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Migraine headache
  • Tension-type headache
  • Cervicogenic headache (from upper neck joints)
  • Occipital neuralgia

Common causes

  • Forward head posture from prolonged screen time ('tech neck').
  • Sleeping with too many pillows or on your stomach.
  • Whiplash injury from a car accident or fall.
  • Clenching your jaw or grinding your teeth (bruxism).
  • Eye strain causing you to jut your chin forward.
  • Stress and emotional tension.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Suboccipital Release · 2-3 minutes, once or twice daily
    Lie on your back with knees bent. Place two lacrosse balls (or a peanut) under the base of your skull. Gently nod 'yes' and shake your head 'no' to massage the area.
  2. Chin Tucks (video: Deep Cervical Flexor Nods Seated) · 2 x 10 slow reps, several times daily
    Gently nod your chin towards your throat as if making a double chin. This is a very small, subtle movement to activate the deep neck muscles.
  3. Diaphragmatic Breathing (video: 90/90 Breathing) · 5 minutes, once or twice daily
    Lie on your back and focus on breathing into your belly, not your chest. This helps calm the nervous system and reduce overall muscle tension.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Chin Glides (video: Chin Glide Seated) · 2 x 10 reps, twice daily
    Sit tall and gently glide your head backward over your shoulders, keeping your eyes level. This reverses the forward head posture that strains these muscles.
  2. Upper Cervical Mobility · 2 x 5 reps each way, once daily
    Tuck your chin slightly. Gently turn your head as far as you can to one side, then the other. The movement should feel like it's coming from the very top of your neck.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Isometric Neck Holds · 3 x 10-second holds in each direction, daily
    Sit tall. Place your palm on your forehead and gently press your head forward into your hand without moving. Repeat pressing into your hand on the side and back of your head.
  2. Seated Posture Reset (video: Seated Desk Posture Reset) · Hold for 30 seconds, every hour
    Use this video to actively correct your posture throughout the day, strengthening the muscles that support a neutral head position.
  3. Scapular Retraction (video: Floor Slide Scapular Retract) · 3 x 10 reps, every other day
    Strengthening your mid-back muscles provides a stable base for your neck and head, reducing the strain on the suboccipitals.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Chin Glide Seated, Deep Cervical Flexor Nods Seated, Seated Desk Posture Reset.

Habits that help

  • Adjust your monitor so the top third is at eye level to prevent looking down.
  • Take frequent micro-breaks from your desk to stand up and reset your posture.
  • Use a supportive pillow that keeps your neck in a neutral line with your spine.
  • Avoid cradling your phone between your ear and shoulder.

What to expect

With consistent exercises and habit changes, most people feel a noticeable reduction in headache frequency and intensity within 2-4 weeks.

When to see a professional

If your headaches are severe, worsening, or accompanied by dizziness, numbness, or weakness, please book an appointment for a full evaluation. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Sudden, severe 'thunderclap' headache.
  • Headache accompanied by fever, stiff neck (can't touch chin to chest), and confusion.
  • Headache after a head injury, especially with loss of consciousness or vomiting.
  • New headache over age 50, or a significant change in your usual headache pattern.

Frequently asked questions

Can a tight suboccipitals cause pain at the behind or around the eye?

Often, yes. A band of headache from the base of the skull wrapping to behind the eye and the forehead. Often mistaken for migraine. That referred pain is why it gets blamed on the joint or the spot that hurts instead of the muscle.

How long does suboccipitals pain take to settle?

With consistent exercises and habit changes, most people feel a noticeable reduction in headache frequency and intensity within 2-4 weeks.

When should I see someone about it?

If your headaches are severe, worsening, or accompanied by dizziness, numbness, or weakness, please book an appointment for a full evaluation.

What else can feel like suboccipitals pain?

Migraine headache, Tension-type headache, Cervicogenic headache (from upper neck joints). An evaluation separates muscle pain from these, and anything that needs a physician is referred on.
