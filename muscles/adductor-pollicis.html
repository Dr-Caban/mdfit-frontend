Where the adductor pollicis sends pain

The web and base of the thumb and across the thumb side of the palm; pain with pinching and writing. People usually come in describing pain at the palm side of the wrist and hand.

What the adductor pollicis does

Draws the thumb toward the palm and is the main power source for pinch grip.

  • Starts at: Two heads: the transverse head from the shaft of the third metacarpal, the oblique head from the capitate and the bases of the second and third metacarpals.
  • Attaches to: The ulnar side of the base of the proximal phalanx of the thumb.
  • Nerve supply: Ulnar nerve, deep branch.

Symptoms people describe

  • Pain and tightness in the web of the thumb.
  • Aching at the base of the thumb and across the thumb side of the palm.
  • Pain with pinching, writing, or using scissors.
  • Difficulty opening the hand wide to span a large object.
  • Cramping in the thumb web after sustained gripping.
  • A sense that the thumb web will not relax.

What makes it worse

Gripping, lifting or typing · Desk, phone or screens. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the adductor pollicis is involved:

  • Is the web between your thumb and index finger tight and tender?
  • Does pinching, writing or using scissors provoke it?
  • Is it hard to open your hand wide to span a large object?
  • Do you get cramping in the thumb web?
  • Do you do repetitive hand work or use tools?

Web pinch palpation. Pinch the web between your thumb and index finger with the other hand, feeling for the muscle deep within it. Suggests this muscle if: Tender, tight bands in the web reproduce the thumb or palm pain.

Thumb span test. Open your hand as wide as possible and compare the span between thumb and index finger on both sides. Suggests this muscle if: A noticeably smaller span on the painful side, with tightness felt in the web.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Thumb base arthritis, which is at the joint rather than in the web.
  • Carpal tunnel syndrome.
  • Ulnar nerve problems at the elbow or wrist.
  • Trigger thumb.
  • Cervical radiculopathy.

Common causes

  • Repetitive pinching, writing or tool use.
  • Sustained phone gripping.
  • Craft work such as knitting, sewing or gardening.
  • Compensating for a weak or painful thumb joint.
  • Playing a musical instrument.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Web self-release · Two minutes, as needed
    Pinch and slowly release the thumb web between the thumb and finger of the other hand, working across the tight bands.
  2. Reduce sustained pinching · Ongoing
    Use thicker pens, lighter tools and break up long tasks.
  3. Warmth before movement · Ten minutes
    Warm water or a warm pack makes the hand more willing to open.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Thumb span stretch · Thirty seconds, three times daily
    Open the hand wide against a flat surface, feeling a gentle stretch in the web, never a sharp one.
  2. Wrist and forearm mobility (video: Weighted Wrist Circles) · Ten each way
    The hand works better when the forearm moves freely.
  3. Forearm flexor lengthening (video: Forearm Flexor Stretch) · Thirty seconds
    Reduces the upstream pull into the hand.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Graded pinch work · Ten repetitions, twice daily
    Light putty or a soft ball pinch, building only once pain free.
  2. Grip endurance (video: Farmer Carry) · Three carries of 30 seconds
    Whole-hand grip without isolated pinching.
  3. Forearm strength (video: Weighted Wrist Circles) · Three sets of ten each way
    Builds the forearm so the small hand muscles do less.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Forearm Flexor Stretch, Weighted Wrist Circles, Eccentric Wrist Pronator.

Habits that help

  • Use thicker-handled pens and tools.
  • Rest the phone in the palm rather than pinching it.
  • Break up long craft or tool sessions.
  • Open and stretch the hand between tasks.

What to expect

Thumb web tightness usually eases within two to three weeks with regular release and task changes, provided the pinching load is genuinely reduced.

When to see a professional

Book an evaluation if pain persists beyond three weeks, or sooner for any visible muscle wasting, progressive weakness, or constant numbness. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Visible wasting of the thumb web muscle, which suggests a nerve problem.
  • Progressive weakness of pinch grip.
  • Constant numbness in the hand.
  • Marked swelling, redness or warmth.
  • Pain following a significant hand injury.
