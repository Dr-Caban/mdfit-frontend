Where the anconeus sends pain

A small, well-localised area of pain over the outer point of the elbow, close to the muscle itself. It is also a common hidden driver behind Tennis Elbow (Lateral Epicondylitis).

What the anconeus does

Assists the triceps in straightening the elbow and helps steady the ulna during forearm rotation.

  • Starts at: The back of the lateral epicondyle of the humerus.
  • Attaches to: The lateral surface of the olecranon and the upper quarter of the back of the ulna.
  • Nerve supply: Radial nerve.

Symptoms people describe

  • A small area of pain over the outer point of the elbow.
  • Discomfort on straightening the elbow against resistance.
  • Pain that sits just behind the usual tennis elbow spot.
  • Tenderness that is well localised rather than spreading.
  • Aching after repeated pushing or locking out the elbow.

What makes it worse

Gripping, lifting or typing · Bending or lifting. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the anconeus is involved:

  • Is your pain in a small spot on the outer point of your elbow?
  • Is it just behind where tennis elbow is usually felt?
  • Does straightening your elbow against resistance provoke it?
  • Did it start after repeated pushing, pressing or locking out?
  • Does it stay in one small area rather than spreading down the forearm?

Point palpation. Find the bony point of the elbow and the outer elbow bone, then press in the small triangle between them. Suggests this muscle if: Well-localised tenderness in that triangle reproduces the pain.

Resisted extension. With the elbow slightly bent, try to straighten it against resistance. Suggests this muscle if: Pain at the outer point of the elbow rather than in the triceps or down the forearm.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Tennis elbow, which sits slightly forward of this.
  • Olecranon bursitis, which produces visible swelling.
  • Radial tunnel syndrome.
  • Elbow joint arthritis.
  • Triceps tendinopathy at its insertion.

Common causes

  • Repeated pushing or pressing, especially locking out the elbow under load.
  • A sudden increase in triceps training.
  • Direct impact to the outer elbow.
  • Repetitive forearm rotation with the elbow extended.
  • Compensation after tennis elbow has changed how the arm is used.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Reduce lockout loading · One to two weeks
    Pause dips, heavy pressing and any movement that forcefully locks the elbow out.
  2. Extensor lengthening (video: Forearm Extensor Stretch) · Thirty seconds, three times daily
    Eases the neighbouring forearm extensors, which usually share the problem.
  3. Light elbow movement (video: Arm Circles) · Ten slow repetitions
    Keeps the joint moving without loading it.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Controlled elbow extension (video: Tricep Extension) · Two sets of ten, light
    Restore range and control with light load, avoiding a forceful lockout.
  2. Eccentric wrist work (video: Eccentric Wrist Drops) · Ten slow repetitions
    Addresses the forearm extensors that commonly accompany this.
  3. Shoulder control (video: Scapular CARs) · Ten each way
    A poorly controlled shoulder loads the elbow.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Progressive triceps load (video: Overhead Tricep Extension) · Three sets of ten, twice weekly
    Build back to pressing strength gradually.
  2. Grip work (video: Weighted Wrist Circles) · Two sets of ten each way
    Spreads load across the forearm.
  3. Press progression (video: Push Up) · Three sets of ten
    Return to pushing load once pain free through range.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Tricep Extension, Forearm Extensor Stretch, Eccentric Wrist Drops.

Habits that help

  • Avoid snapping the elbow into a hard lockout under load.
  • Build pressing and triceps volume gradually.
  • Protect the point of the elbow from direct impact.
  • Address tennis elbow properly rather than working around it.

What to expect

This is a small muscle and usually settles within one to three weeks once the provoking load is reduced.

When to see a professional

Book an evaluation if pain persists beyond three weeks. Swelling, redness, fever or inability to straighten the elbow needs a physician promptly. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Visible swelling at the point of the elbow, which may be bursitis or infection.
  • Redness and warmth with fever.
  • Inability to straighten the elbow actively.
  • Pain after a fall onto the elbow, which may indicate a fracture.
  • Progressive numbness in the little and ring fingers.
