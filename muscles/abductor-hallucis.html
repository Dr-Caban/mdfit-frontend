Where the abductor hallucis sends pain

The inner heel and along the inner border of the foot; a frequent companion to plantar heel pain. People usually come in describing pain at the sole of the foot or inner ankle and arch.

What the abductor hallucis does

Abducts and flexes the great toe and supports the medial longitudinal arch. It is the most medial of the first-layer foot muscles.

  • Starts at: The medial process of the calcaneal tuberosity, the flexor retinaculum and the plantar aponeurosis.
  • Attaches to: The medial side of the base of the proximal phalanx of the great toe.
  • Nerve supply: Medial plantar nerve, a branch of the tibial nerve.

Symptoms people describe

  • Pain along the inner edge of the sole, from the heel toward the big toe.
  • Inner heel pain that is often grouped with plantar fasciitis.
  • Burning or tingling along the inner arch, as the nerve runs beneath this muscle.
  • Pain with the first steps in the morning.
  • Discomfort after long periods standing or walking.
  • An arch that feels tired or cramped by the end of the day.

What makes it worse

Standing or walking · Running or sports. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the abductor hallucis is involved:

  • Is your pain along the inner edge of your sole rather than the middle of the heel?
  • Do you get burning or tingling in the arch as well as aching?
  • Are the first steps in the morning the worst?
  • Does it worsen with long periods standing?
  • Have you increased walking or running recently, or changed footwear?

Inner border palpation. Press along the inner edge of the sole, from just in front of the heel toward the base of the big toe. Suggests this muscle if: Tenderness along this band reproduces the inner heel or arch pain.

Resisted big toe abduction. Try to move the big toe away from the second toe against light resistance. Many people find this hard to do at all. Suggests this muscle if: Pain along the inner arch, or an inability to produce the movement, suggesting the muscle is not working well.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Plantar fasciitis.
  • Tarsal tunnel syndrome, where the nerve is compressed near this muscle.
  • Tibialis posterior tendinopathy.
  • Calcaneal stress fracture.
  • Nerve entrapment of the first branch of the lateral plantar nerve.

Common causes

  • A sudden increase in walking or running.
  • Footwear with a narrow toe box that holds the big toe inward.
  • Flat or collapsing arches.
  • Long periods standing on hard surfaces.
  • Compensating for weak calf or foot muscles.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Footwear check · Once, then review
    A wider toe box lets the big toe sit straight. Narrow shoes keep this muscle shortened and loaded.
  2. Plantar lengthening (video: Kneeling Plantar Fascia Stretch) · Thirty seconds, three times daily
    Gentle lengthening of the sole, including the first steps routine before getting out of bed.
  3. Reduce standing load · Two weeks
    A cushioned mat if you stand at work, and shorter walks while it settles.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Big toe control · Ten repetitions, twice daily
    Practise pressing the big toe down while keeping the other toes lifted, then the reverse. Most people have lost this control.
  2. Calf lengthening (video: Eccentric Calf Drops) · Ten slow repetitions
    A tight calf increases the load the foot has to absorb.
  3. Ankle mobility (video: Ankle Circles) · Ten each way
    Restores motion above the foot.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Arch and foot strength (video: Toe Walk) · Three sets of 20 metres
    Loads the foot muscles directly.
  2. Balance work (video: Single Leg Balance) · Three holds of 30 seconds each side
    The foot muscles work constantly in single leg stance.
  3. Calf strength (video: Eccentric Calf Drops) · Three sets of twelve, twice weekly
    Builds the capacity that protects the foot.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Kneeling Plantar Fascia Stretch, Toe Walk, Single Leg Balance.

Habits that help

  • Choose shoes with a wide enough toe box.
  • Build walking and running volume gradually.
  • Do the first steps stretch before getting out of bed.
  • Use a cushioned mat if you stand on hard floors.

What to expect

Foot muscle pain usually improves within three to six weeks with footwear changes and graded loading. Heel pain that involves the plantar fascia often takes longer.

When to see a professional

Book an evaluation if heel or arch pain persists beyond three weeks. Night pain, constant numbness, or any foot problem in someone with diabetes needs prompt assessment. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Heel pain that is present at rest and at night, which may indicate a stress fracture.
  • Numbness in the sole that is constant rather than intermittent.
  • Redness, warmth and swelling with fever.
  • Foot pain in someone with diabetes, which needs prompt assessment.
  • Inability to bear weight after an injury.
