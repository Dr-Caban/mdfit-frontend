Where the obturator internus sends pain

Deep in the buttock and the back of the hip, sometimes into the back of the thigh. Pain is often felt deeper than any muscle you can press. People usually come in describing pain at the middle of the buttock.

What the obturator internus does

Externally rotates the extended hip and abducts the flexed hip, and helps hold the femoral head in the socket. Its tendon makes a right-angle turn around the pelvic bone, which is unusual and makes it vulnerable to irritation at that corner.

  • Starts at: The inner surface of the obturator membrane and the pelvic bone around it, inside the pelvis.
  • Attaches to: The medial surface of the greater trochanter, after turning sharply around the lesser sciatic notch.
  • Nerve supply: Nerve to obturator internus (L5 to S2).

Symptoms people describe

  • Deep buttock pain that feels further in than you can reach.
  • Pain in the back of the hip, sometimes spreading into the back of the thigh.
  • Discomfort sitting for long periods.
  • Pain on turning the leg outward.
  • A deep ache that has not responded to piriformis treatment.
  • Occasional pelvic floor discomfort, because the muscle forms part of the side wall of the pelvis.

What makes it worse

Sitting · Stairs, squats or hills · Running or sports. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the obturator internus is involved:

  • Is your buttock pain deeper than anything you can press on?
  • Has piriformis-focused treatment failed to change it?
  • Does turning your leg outward provoke it?
  • Is prolonged sitting uncomfortable?
  • Does the pain spread into the back of your thigh?

Resisted external rotation in flexion. Sit with the hip and knee bent to 90 degrees and try to turn the thigh outward against resistance. Suggests this muscle if: Deep pain in the buttock that is not reproduced by pressing on the surface.

Deep rotator stretch. Lie on your back, bring the knee toward the opposite shoulder and let the hip rotate. Suggests this muscle if: A deep pull in the buttock that reproduces the familiar ache.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Piriformis syndrome.
  • Sciatic nerve irritation.
  • Hip joint problems including labral tear.
  • Proximal hamstring tendinopathy.
  • Sacroiliac joint pain.

Common causes

  • Prolonged sitting.
  • Sports with rotation and direction change.
  • A sudden increase in running volume.
  • Compensation for weak gluteal muscles.
  • Pelvic or hip surgery and the adaptations that follow.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Reduce sustained sitting (video: Walk Break Protocol Seated) · Every 30 minutes
    Deep rotators tolerate movement better than static positions.
  2. Breathing and pelvic release (video: 90/90 Breathing) · 5 minutes daily
    This muscle forms part of the pelvic side wall, and settles better when breathing is relaxed.
  3. Gentle hip opening (video: Figure Four Stretch) · Thirty seconds each side
    Easy range only; forcing it can irritate the sciatic nerve.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Hip rotation range (video: 90/90 Hip Switch) · Ten each side, daily
    Restores rotation in both directions, which this muscle controls.
  2. Controlled hip circles (video: Standing Hip CARs) · Five each way
    Full controlled range rather than end-range stretching.
  3. Nerve glide if symptoms travel (video: Hip Neuroglide) · Ten slow repetitions
    Gentle gliding where there is any leg symptom.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. External rotation strength (video: Standing Hip ER Liftoff) · Two sets of twelve each side
    Direct loading of the deep rotator group.
  2. Glute strength (video: Hip Thrust) · Three sets of ten
    Takes the stabilising demand off the deep rotators.
  3. Single leg control (video: Single Leg Balance) · Three holds of 30 seconds each side
    Hip stability under real-world load.

Habits that help

  • Change position regularly rather than sitting still for hours.
  • Build running and rotational sport volume gradually.
  • Strengthen the glutes rather than repeatedly stretching the buttock.
  • Mention any pelvic floor symptoms to your clinician; they are relevant here.

What to expect

Deep rotator problems usually take three to five weeks to settle, longer than superficial muscles, because they cannot be reached directly and respond mainly to load and position change.

When to see a professional

Book an evaluation if deep buttock pain persists beyond three weeks. Leg weakness, foot drop, saddle numbness or loss of bladder or bowel control needs emergency care. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Progressive leg weakness or foot drop.
  • Loss of bladder or bowel control, or saddle numbness, which needs emergency care.
  • Fever with deep pelvic or buttock pain.
  • Buttock pain after a fall, especially with thinning bones.
  • Night pain that is not relieved by position change.
