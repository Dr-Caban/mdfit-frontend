Where the hypothenar muscles sends pain

The little finger side of the palm and the little finger itself; often sore after leaning on the heel of the hand. People usually come in describing pain at the ring and little finger.

What the hypothenar muscles does

A group of three short muscles that abduct, flex and oppose the little finger, cupping the palm for grip.

  • Starts at: The pisiform bone and the hook of the hamate, by way of the flexor retinaculum.
  • Attaches to: The base of the proximal phalanx of the little finger and the shaft of the fifth metacarpal.
  • Nerve supply: Ulnar nerve, deep branch.

Symptoms people describe

  • Aching in the pad on the little finger side of the palm.
  • Pain or tingling in the little finger and the adjacent half of the ring finger.
  • Soreness after leaning on the heel of the hand, as when cycling or using a desk.
  • Weakness spreading the fingers apart.
  • Difficulty cupping the hand to hold water or a round object.
  • Numbness that follows the ring and little fingers rather than the thumb side.

What makes it worse

Gripping, lifting or typing · Desk, phone or screens. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the hypothenar muscles is involved:

  • Is the pad on the little finger side of your palm tender?
  • Do your little and ring fingers tingle rather than your thumb side?
  • Did it start after cycling, or after leaning on the heel of your hand?
  • Is it hard to spread your fingers apart strongly?
  • Do you lean on your wrists at a desk or keyboard?

Hypothenar palpation. Press into the muscular pad along the little finger side of the palm, from the wrist crease toward the base of the little finger. Suggests this muscle if: Tenderness reproduces the palm or little finger symptoms.

Finger spread test. Spread your fingers apart against light resistance and compare sides. Suggests this muscle if: Noticeable weakness on the symptomatic side, which raises the possibility of ulnar nerve involvement.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Cubital tunnel syndrome at the elbow.
  • Ulnar nerve compression at the wrist.
  • Cervical radiculopathy at the C8 nerve root.
  • Thoracic outlet symptoms.
  • Wrist joint problems on the little finger side.

Common causes

  • Prolonged pressure on the heel of the hand, classically from cycling handlebars.
  • Leaning on the wrist at a desk or keyboard.
  • Repetitive gripping with tools.
  • Using the heel of the hand as a hammer.
  • Ulnar nerve compression at the wrist or elbow.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Remove the pressure source · Ongoing
    Padded gloves and varied hand position on handlebars, and a wrist rest that supports the forearm rather than the heel of the hand.
  2. Gentle self-release · Two minutes
    Slow pressure along the muscular pad, avoiding pressing directly into the nerve at the wrist crease.
  3. Nerve gliding (video: Full Neuroglide Flow Seated) · Ten slow repetitions
    Gentle gliding where tingling is present.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Finger spread and cup · Ten repetitions, twice daily
    Spread the fingers wide, then cup the palm, slowly and under control.
  2. Wrist mobility (video: Weighted Wrist Circles) · Ten each way
    Restores motion without compressing the wrist.
  3. Forearm flexor lengthening (video: Forearm Flexor Stretch) · Thirty seconds
    Reduces upstream tension into the hand.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Graded grip work · Ten repetitions, twice daily
    Soft ball squeezes, building gradually.
  2. Grip endurance (video: Farmer Carry) · Three carries of 30 seconds
    Builds whole-hand capacity.
  3. Upper limb strength (video: Cable Row) · Three sets of ten
    Shares the load away from the hand.

Habits that help

  • Use padded gloves and change hand position regularly when cycling.
  • Support the forearm rather than resting on the heel of the hand at a desk.
  • Avoid using the heel of the hand to strike or push.
  • Have any numbness that becomes constant assessed.

What to expect

Symptoms from pressure usually ease within two to three weeks once the pressure is removed. Nerve-related symptoms take longer and depend on addressing the compression.

When to see a professional

Book an evaluation if symptoms persist beyond three weeks. Visible wasting, progressive weakness or clawing of the fingers needs prompt medical assessment. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Visible wasting between the fingers or in the palm.
  • Progressive weakness of finger spreading or grip.
  • Fingers beginning to claw.
  • Constant numbness rather than intermittent.
  • Symptoms after a fall or wrist injury.

What else can feel like hypothenar muscles pain?
