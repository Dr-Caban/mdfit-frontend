Where the piriformis sends pain

Mid-buttock and back of the hip, down the back-outside of the thigh toward the calf. People usually come in describing pain at the middle of the buttock, back of the thigh or outer calf. It is also a common hidden driver behind Sciatica-Type Pain.

What the piriformis does

Externally rotates the hip (turns the leg outward) when the hip is extended, and abducts the hip when it is flexed.

  • Starts at: Anterior surface of the sacrum.
  • Attaches to: Superior aspect of the greater trochanter of the femur.
  • Nerve supply: Nerve to piriformis (S1, S2).

Symptoms people describe

  • A deep, aching pain in the center of the buttock.
  • Pain that refers down the back or back-outside of the thigh, often stopping at the knee.
  • Numbness or tingling down the back of the leg if the muscle is compressing the sciatic nerve.
  • Pain that is significantly worse with sitting, especially on hard surfaces or while driving.
  • Difficulty crossing the affected leg over the other one.

What makes it worse

Sitting · Running or sports · Stairs, squats or hills. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the piriformis is involved:

  • Is your pain focused in the dead center of one buttock?
  • Does sitting for more than 15-20 minutes dramatically increase your buttock pain?
  • Does the pain travel down the back of your thigh, but usually not past your knee?
  • Do you get relief by standing up and walking around after sitting?
  • Do you feel pain when you try to pull your knee towards your opposite shoulder?

Piriformis Stretch Test (FAIR Test). Lie on your back, cross the ankle of the painful side over the opposite knee. Gently pull the bottom knee toward your chest and slightly toward the opposite shoulder. Suggests this muscle if: Reproduction of your specific buttock and/or posterior thigh pain indicates piriformis involvement.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Lumbar radiculopathy (true sciatica from a disc herniation)
  • Sacroiliac (SI) joint dysfunction
  • Hamstring tendinopathy
  • Ischial bursitis ('weaver's bottom')
  • Gluteus minimus trigger points

Common causes

  • Prolonged sitting, which compresses and shortens the muscle.
  • Sitting on a wallet in a back pocket ('wallet neuritis').
  • Overuse from activities like running or cycling without proper glute strength.
  • Anatomical variation where the sciatic nerve passes through the piriformis muscle.
  • Direct trauma or fall onto the buttock.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Piriformis Release (Ball) · 2-3 minutes, daily
    Sit on the floor with your knees bent. Place a tennis or lacrosse ball under the center of the painful buttock. Support yourself with your hands and gently roll to find the tender spot, then hold with steady pressure.
  2. 90/90 Hip Switch (video) · 2 x 10 reps, twice daily
    This is an excellent gentle mobilizer for the deep hip rotators. Move slowly and within a pain-free range of motion.
  3. Figure-4 Stretch (Gentle) · 3 x 20 sec holds, twice daily
    Lie on your back, cross the ankle of the painful side over the opposite knee. Do NOT pull yet. Just hold this position and breathe, allowing the muscle to relax.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Figure-4 Stretch (Active) · 2 x 30 sec per side, daily
    From the gentle Figure-4 position, now reach through and gently pull the bottom thigh toward your chest until you feel a comfortable stretch in the buttock.
  2. Hip Neuroglide (video) · 2 x 10 reps per side, daily
    This is crucial for piriformis issues. It helps the sciatic nerve slide more freely relative to the piriformis muscle, reducing irritation and referred symptoms.
  3. Clamshells (video: Standing Clamshell) · 2 x 15 reps per side, every other day
    Lie on your side with knees bent and hips stacked. Lift your top knee without rocking your pelvis back. This strengthens other hip rotators to take the load off the piriformis.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Standing Hip ER Liftoff (video) · 3 x 10 reps per side, every other day
    This directly strengthens the piriformis and other external rotators in a functional, standing position. Focus on control, not height.
  2. Glute Bridge · 3 x 15 reps, every other day
    Strengthening the main gluteus maximus muscle provides stability to the pelvis and can reduce the over-activity and strain on the smaller piriformis.
  3. Hip Thrust (video) · 3 x 8 reps, every other day
    Shoulders on a bench, drive the hips up by squeezing the glutes, ribs down. Lower slowly; add load only when pain-free.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: 90/90 Hip Switch, Standing Hip ER Liftoff, Hip Neuroglide.

Habits that help

  • Take your wallet out of your back pocket permanently.
  • Set a timer to get up and walk every 20-30 minutes when sitting.
  • When driving, use a seat cushion with a cutout for the tailbone/piriformis area.
  • Try to sit with your feet flat on the floor rather than with your legs crossed.

What to expect

Relief from sitting-related pain can often be felt within the first 1-2 weeks. Reducing nerve irritation and building enough glute strength to prevent recurrence is a longer process, typically taking 6-12 weeks of consistent work.

When to see a professional

Schedule an in-person evaluation if you experience true numbness, tingling, or weakness that doesn't improve with nerve glides, or if your pain is not significantly better after 4 weeks. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Loss of bowel or bladder control (cauda equina syndrome).
  • Progressive leg weakness, such as a 'foot drop'.
  • Numbness in the 'saddle' area (groin and inner thighs).
  • Pain that is severe, constant, and not relieved by any change in position.

Frequently asked questions

Can a tight piriformis cause pain at the middle of the buttock?

Often, yes. Mid-buttock and back of the hip, down the back-outside of the thigh toward the calf. That referred pain is why it gets blamed on the joint or the spot that hurts instead of the muscle.

How long does piriformis pain take to settle?

Relief from sitting-related pain can often be felt within the first 1-2 weeks. Reducing nerve irritation and building enough glute strength to prevent recurrence is a longer process, typically taking 6-12 weeks of consistent work.

When should I see someone about it?

Schedule an in-person evaluation if you experience true numbness, tingling, or weakness that doesn't improve with nerve glides, or if your pain is not significantly better after 4 weeks.

What else can feel like piriformis pain?

Lumbar radiculopathy (true sciatica from a disc herniation), Sacroiliac (SI) joint dysfunction, Hamstring tendinopathy. An evaluation separates muscle pain from these, and anything that needs a physician is referred on.
