Where the transversus abdominis sends pain

A band across the low back and the lower abdomen. Weakness here shows up as low back pain rather than pain in the muscle itself. People usually come in describing pain at the belly or side of the abdomen. It is also a common hidden driver behind Low Back Pain.

What the transversus abdominis does

Compresses the abdomen and raises intra-abdominal pressure. Its fibres run horizontally, so it does not bend or twist the trunk; it stiffens it. In most people it activates slightly before the limb moves, giving the spine a stable base.

  • Starts at: The inner surfaces of the lower six costal cartilages, the thoracolumbar fascia, the inner lip of the iliac crest and the outer third of the inguinal ligament.
  • Attaches to: The abdominal aponeurosis, the linea alba and the pubic crest.
  • Nerve supply: Anterior rami of thoracic nerves T7 to T12, plus the iliohypogastric and ilioinguinal nerves.

Symptoms people describe

  • Low back pain that has no single injury behind it.
  • A back that feels unstable or 'gives way' with ordinary movements.
  • Pain on standing up, lifting or reaching overhead.
  • A band of discomfort across the low back and lower abdomen.
  • Back pain that is worse late in the day as endurance runs out.
  • A sense of needing to hold the stomach in constantly to feel supported.

What makes it worse

Bending or lifting · Standing or walking · Sitting · Deep breaths or coughing. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the transversus abdominis is involved:

  • Does your low back ache without a clear injury behind it?
  • Does your back feel unstable, or like it might give way?
  • Is it worse late in the day rather than first thing?
  • Have you had a baby, abdominal surgery, or a long period of inactivity?
  • Do you hold your stomach in to feel supported?

Abdominal draw-in check. Lie on your back with knees bent and fingers just inside the front of each hip bone. Gently draw the lower abdomen away from your fingers without holding your breath or tilting the pelvis. Suggests this muscle if: You cannot do it without bracing the whole abdomen, holding your breath, or the belly pushing outward instead.

Breathing under load. Hold a dead bug position with one arm and the opposite leg extended and try to breathe normally for 20 seconds. Suggests this muscle if: You have to hold your breath, or the low back arches off the floor.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Lumbar disc or joint problems.
  • Sacroiliac joint pain.
  • Hip-driven low back pain.
  • Abdominal organ or gynaecological problems.
  • Quadratus lumborum pain.

Common causes

  • Prolonged inactivity or bed rest.
  • Pregnancy and the postpartum period.
  • Abdominal surgery.
  • A history of low back pain, which inhibits this muscle.
  • Training that only loads the outer abdominal layers.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Breathing before bracing (video: 90/90 Breathing) · 5 minutes daily
    This muscle works with the diaphragm. Restoring the breathing pattern comes before any strengthening.
  2. Stop gripping the abdomen · Throughout the day
    Constant holding-in is not the same as control, and it tires the system out.
  3. Position change (video: Walk Break Protocol Seated) · Every 30 minutes
    Frequent position changes reduce the sustained load on a system that is not holding well.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Dead bug (video) · Three sets of ten each side, daily
    The core exercise for this muscle. Keep the low back lightly in contact with the floor and keep breathing throughout.
  2. Core brace introduction (video: Core Brace Intro Seated) · Ten repetitions, twice daily
    Learn a brace that still allows breathing.
  3. Pelvic position control (video: Pillow Belt Reset) · Ten repetitions daily
    Teaches a neutral pelvis as the starting point for everything else.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Anti-rotation holds (video: Pallof Press) · Three holds of 20 seconds each side
    Progressive load on the stabilising role.
  2. Loaded carries (video: Farmer Carry) · Three carries of 30 seconds, twice weekly
    The most direct way to build trunk endurance under real load.
  3. Hip hinge with control (video: Dowel Hip Hinge) · Three sets of ten
    Puts the trunk control into the movement pattern that matters most for the back.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Dead Bug, 90/90 Breathing, Pallof Press.

Habits that help

  • Keep breathing during any core exercise; breath-holding defeats the purpose.
  • Change position regularly rather than sitting still for hours.
  • Return to this work gradually after pregnancy or abdominal surgery.
  • Build load slowly; endurance matters more than maximum effort here.

What to expect

Deep trunk control builds over weeks rather than days. Most people notice their back feeling steadier within three to four weeks of daily practice, with fuller change over two to three months.

When to see a professional

Book an evaluation if back pain is not improving after a month, or sooner for leg weakness, numbness or any loss of bladder or bowel control, which needs emergency care. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Loss of bladder or bowel control, or numbness in the saddle area, which needs emergency care.
  • Progressive leg weakness.
  • Back pain with fever or unexplained weight loss.
  • Back pain following a significant fall, particularly with thinning bones.
  • A visible midline abdominal bulge on straining.
