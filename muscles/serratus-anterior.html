Where the serratus anterior sends pain

Side of the rib cage and the bottom tip of the shoulder blade; a side "stitch" that hurts with deep breaths. People usually come in describing pain at the side of the ribs, along one rib or bottom tip of the shoulder blade.

What the serratus anterior does

Pulls the shoulder blade forward and around the rib cage (protraction), like when you punch or reach forward. It also holds the shoulder blade against the ribs and helps rotate it upward when you lift your arm overhead.

  • Starts at: Outer surfaces of the upper 8 or 9 ribs.
  • Attaches to: The front side of the inner border of the scapula (shoulder blade).
  • Nerve supply: Long thoracic nerve (from cervical roots C5, C6, and C7).

Symptoms people describe

  • A sharp, nagging ache on the side of your chest or ribs, often described as a 'side stitch' that won't go away.
  • Pain that feels like it's under the bottom tip of your shoulder blade.
  • Difficulty taking a full, deep breath without a sharp pain or 'catch'.
  • A feeling of weakness or instability when pushing a door or performing a push-up.
  • Pain that is worse when lying on the affected side.
  • A sensation that your shoulder blade is 'winging' or not sitting flat on your back.

What makes it worse

Deep breaths or coughing · Running or sports. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the serratus anterior is involved:

  • Do you feel a sharp 'stitch' in your side when you try to take a deep breath or run?
  • Does the pain feel like it's located under the bottom tip of your shoulder blade?
  • Do you feel a 'catch' or sharp pain when you reach forward or push something, like a heavy door?
  • Is it uncomfortable to lie on the painful side at night?
  • Does the pain get worse with a persistent cough?

Wall Push Test. Stand facing a wall, about arm's length away. Place your hands on the wall at shoulder height and lean forward slightly. Slowly perform a push-up against the wall. Suggests this muscle if: You feel pain on the side of your ribs, or you see the inside edge of your shoulder blade 'wing' out away from your back as you push.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Intercostal muscle strain
  • Rib fracture or stress fracture
  • Pleurisy (inflammation of the lung lining)
  • Gallbladder problems (if on the right side)
  • Heart-related pain (if on the left side)

Common causes

  • A sudden increase in strenuous exercise, especially running, swimming, or weightlifting.
  • Persistent, hard coughing from asthma, bronchitis, or allergies.
  • Repetitive pushing movements, like in push-ups or bench press with poor form.
  • Poor posture with shoulders rounded forward for long periods.
  • Sleeping in an awkward position on your side, compressing the muscle.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Side-Lying Rib Expansion (video: Serratus Stretch Breathing) · 2 x 10 deep breaths, twice daily
    Lie on your non-painful side with a small pillow under your waist. Focus on breathing into your painful side, feeling the ribs expand with each slow inhale.
  2. Gentle Torso Rotation (video: Thread The Needle) · 2 x 8 reps per side, once daily
    Start on your hands and knees. Gently reach one arm under your body, rotating your torso slightly. Move slowly and only within a pain-free range.
  3. Diaphragmatic Breathing (video: 90/90 Breathing) · 5 minutes, once or twice daily
    Lie on your back with your feet up on a chair. Focus on breathing deep into your belly, allowing your rib cage to expand gently without forcing it.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Scapular Push-Ups (video: Scap Push Ups) · 2 x 10-15 reps, every other day
    In a plank or hands-and-knees position, keep your arms straight and pinch your shoulder blades together, then push them apart to round your upper back. The movement should only come from your shoulder blades.
  2. Wall Slides (video: Floor Slide Scapular Retract) · 2 x 10 reps, once daily
    Stand with your back against a wall. Place your forearms on the wall in a 'goalpost' position. Slowly slide your arms up the wall, keeping your shoulder blades engaged, then return to the start.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Wall Push Up (video) · 3 x 8-12 reps, every other day
    Stand facing a wall and perform a push-up, focusing on feeling the muscles on the side of your ribs engage as you push away from the wall. Keep your core tight.
  2. Single Arm Dumbbell Press · 3 x 8 reps per side, every other day
    Lie on your back holding a light dumbbell in one hand, arm extended toward the ceiling. Punch the dumbbell straight up, lifting your shoulder blade off the floor. Control the movement back down.

Habits that help

  • When sitting, use a chair with good back support to prevent slouching and rounding your shoulders.
  • Avoid sleeping on the painful side; try sleeping on your back with pillows for support.
  • Practice deep belly breathing throughout the day to avoid shallow chest breathing patterns.
  • When lifting, engage your core and push through your legs, not just your arms and shoulders.

What to expect

With consistent gentle movement and strengthening, you should notice a significant decrease in pain within 2-3 weeks. Full recovery and return to strenuous activity may take 4-6 weeks.

When to see a professional

Book an appointment if the pain is severe, doesn't improve after 1-2 weeks of self-care, or if you have any of the 'Red Flag' symptoms. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Pain accompanied by severe shortness of breath, dizziness, or sweating (could be a heart attack).
  • Pain that started after a significant fall or impact to your chest (could be a rib fracture).
  • Fever, chills, and a productive cough along with the chest pain (could be pneumonia).
  • Pain that doesn't change at all with movement or position (could be an organ issue).
