Where the vastus medialis sends pain

Inner knee and inside of the kneecap; can make the knee feel like it buckles. People usually come in describing pain at the inside of the knee, kneecap or just below it or inner thigh.

What the vastus medialis does

Part of the quadriceps group, its primary role is to extend the knee. The lower fibers (often called the VMO) are crucial for the final degrees of extension and for keeping the kneecap tracking correctly in its groove.

  • Starts at: Medial lip of the linea aspera and intertrochanteric line of the femur (inner back and top of the thigh bone).
  • Attaches to: Medial border of the patella (inner edge of the kneecap) and tibial tuberosity.
  • Nerve supply: Femoral nerve (L2, L3, L4).

Symptoms people describe

  • Aching or sharp pain on the inner side of the knee and kneecap.
  • A feeling that the knee might 'give way' or buckle, especially on stairs.
  • Swelling or puffiness on the inside of the knee, just above the kneecap.
  • A 'crunching' or 'grinding' sound (crepitus) under the kneecap with movement.
  • Pain that is worse when going up or down stairs, squatting, or running.
  • Difficulty fully straightening the leg with a 'locking' sensation.

What makes it worse

Stairs, squats or hills · Running or sports. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the vastus medialis is involved:

  • Is your pain located specifically on the inner side of your knee or kneecap?
  • Does your knee ever feel like it's going to buckle or give out, especially on stairs?
  • Do you see or feel a 'teardrop' shaped muscle on the inside of your thigh just above the knee, and is it tender to press on?
  • Is the pain worse after activities that involve repeated knee bending, like running or squatting?
  • Does it hurt to sit for a long time with your knee bent?

VMO Contraction Test. Sit on the floor with your leg straight out in front of you. Place your fingers on the 'teardrop' muscle on the inside of your thigh, just above the kneecap. Try to tighten only this muscle. Suggests this muscle if: You feel pain under your fingers, or you are unable to contract this muscle without contracting the entire thigh.

Terminal Knee Extension Test. Stand with a resistance band looped around the back of your painful knee and anchored to something in front of you. Let the band pull your knee into a slight bend, then straighten your leg by squeezing your quad. Suggests this muscle if: This movement reproduces your specific inner knee pain.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Medial meniscus tear.
  • Medial collateral ligament (MCL) sprain.
  • Patellofemoral pain syndrome.
  • Medial plica syndrome.
  • Knee joint arthritis.

Common causes

  • An imbalance between the vastus medialis and the vastus lateralis (outer quad), causing the kneecap to pull sideways.
  • Weakness or poor activation of the VMO, often following a knee injury or surgery.
  • Flat feet (overpronation), which can cause the lower leg to rotate inward and affect kneecap tracking.
  • Weak hip and gluteal muscles, leading to poor leg alignment.
  • A sudden increase in squatting, lunging, or running volume.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. VMO Soft Tissue Release · 60-90 seconds, daily
    Sit with your leg slightly bent. Use your thumbs or a massage ball to apply gentle but firm pressure to the tender muscle fibers on the inside of your thigh, just above the knee.
  2. Quad Sets with Medial Focus · 3 x 10 sec holds, 10 reps, daily
    Sit with your leg straight. Place a small rolled towel under your knee. Squeeze your quad to straighten the leg and lift your heel, focusing on the inner quad muscle.
  3. Knee Neuroglide (video) · 2 x 10 reps, daily
    Gently perform this nerve glide to ensure the nerves supplying the quad are not restricted, which can inhibit muscle function.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Terminal Knee Extensions (TKEs) · 3 x 15 per side, every other day
    Loop a resistance band around the back of your knee, anchored in front of you. Step back to create tension. Bend and straighten the last 20-30 degrees of knee extension, focusing on the VMO.
  2. Wall Sit with Ball Squeeze · 3 x 30-45 sec holds, every other day
    Perform a wall sit at a comfortable height. Place a small ball between your knees and maintain a gentle squeeze to increase VMO activation.
  3. Single Leg Balance (video) · 3 x 30 sec holds per side, daily
    This improves proprioception and stability around the knee joint, which is crucial for VMO function.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Step Up (video) · 3 x 10-12 per side, twice weekly
    Use a low step. Focus on keeping your knee aligned over your second toe and avoid letting it collapse inward. Control the movement on the way down.
  2. Bodyweight Squat (Heels Elevated) (video: Bodyweight Squat) · 3 x 12-15, twice weekly
    Place your heels on a small plate or book. This allows for a more upright torso and can help you focus on quad activation, including the VMO.
  3. Step Up (video) · 3 x 8-10 per side, twice weekly
    Step up onto a low box, keeping the knee lined up over the second toe; lower slowly over 3 seconds.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Step Up, Knee Neuroglide, Bodyweight Squat.

Habits that help

  • Avoid letting your knee collapse inward during daily activities like climbing stairs or standing up.
  • Consider footwear or orthotics if you have significantly flat feet.
  • When sitting, try to avoid positions that twist the knee, like sitting with one foot tucked under you.
  • Focus on squeezing your quad to fully 'lock out' your knee at the top of each step when walking.

What to expect

A reduction in buckling sensations and daily pain can often be felt in 2-4 weeks. Rebuilding the VMO's strength and coordination to prevent recurrence during sports can take 8-12 weeks.

When to see a professional

Book an appointment if your knee is locking, if it swells up significantly after activity, or if the feeling of instability persists despite 3-4 weeks of consistent exercise. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • The knee is locked in a bent position and you cannot straighten it.
  • A 'pop' sound at the time of injury followed by significant, rapid swelling.
  • Inability to put any weight on the leg.
  • Redness, heat, and extreme tenderness in the knee joint (could be infection or gout).
