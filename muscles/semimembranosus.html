Where the semimembranosus sends pain

The buttock crease and the back of the thigh, concentrating deep in the inner back of the knee. People usually come in describing pain at the buttock crease, back of the knee or back of the thigh.

What the semimembranosus does

Extends the hip, flexes the knee and rotates the lower leg inward. Through its attachments into the knee capsule it also helps protect the back of the knee joint.

  • Starts at: The ischial tuberosity, by a broad flat tendon lying deep to the semitendinosus origin.
  • Attaches to: The back of the medial condyle of the tibia, with expansions into the knee capsule and the oblique popliteal ligament.
  • Nerve supply: Sciatic nerve, tibial division.

Symptoms people describe

  • Deep pain at the back of the inner knee.
  • Pain at the sit bone and down the inner back of the thigh.
  • A deep ache rather than the cord-like tenderness of semitendinosus.
  • Discomfort straightening the knee fully.
  • Pain with deep squatting or lunging.
  • Swelling at the back of the knee in some cases.

What makes it worse

Sitting · Running or sports · Stairs, squats or hills. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the semimembranosus is involved:

  • Is your pain deep at the back of your inner knee?
  • Is it a deep ache rather than a tender rope you can feel?
  • Is it painful to straighten your knee fully?
  • Does deep squatting or lunging provoke it?
  • Is the sit bone also tender?

Deep inner knee palpation. With the knee bent, press deep into the inner side at the back of the knee, behind and beneath the prominent semitendinosus cord. Suggests this muscle if: Deep tenderness here reproduces the back-of-knee pain.

Resisted flexion near full extension. With the knee almost straight, bend it against resistance. Suggests this muscle if: Deep pain at the back of the inner knee.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Baker's cyst at the back of the knee.
  • Medial meniscus tear.
  • Pes anserine bursitis.
  • Popliteus strain.
  • Deep vein thrombosis, which must be excluded if there is swelling, warmth and calf tenderness.

Common causes

  • Sprinting and deceleration.
  • A sudden increase in hill or speed work.
  • Deep squatting and lunging volume.
  • Prolonged sitting.
  • Weak glutes shifting hip extension work to the hamstrings.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Reduce sitting and deep knee bend · Two weeks
    Both positions load the attachment at the back of the knee.
  2. Isometric loading · Five holds of 30 seconds, daily
    Gentle pain-free heel press into the floor with the knee slightly bent.
  3. Avoid stretching into pain · Ongoing
    This attachment responds to graded load, not to pulling it longer.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Hip hinge (video: Dowel Hip Hinge) · Three sets of ten
    Restores the pattern that loads the hamstring safely.
  2. Controlled lengthening (video: Eccentric Toe Reach) · Ten slow repetitions
    Builds tolerance through range.
  3. Knee mobility (video: Knee Circles) · Ten each way
    Restores full knee movement, including the last few degrees of straightening.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Romanian deadlift (video) · Three sets of eight, twice weekly
    Progressive hamstring loading, kept pain free.
  2. Glute strength (video: Hip Thrust) · Three sets of ten
    Reduces the hamstring's share of hip extension.
  3. Single leg strength (video: Bulgarian Split Squat) · Three sets of eight each side
    Loads the leg in a position closer to running.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Romanian Deadlift, Dowel Hip Hinge, Eccentric Toe Reach.

Habits that help

  • Warm up before speed and hill work.
  • Build deep squatting and lunging volume gradually.
  • Load the hamstring rather than stretching it when sore.
  • Have any calf swelling or warmth assessed urgently rather than treated as muscular.

What to expect

Muscle strains here usually settle in three to six weeks. Pain at the attachment behind the knee or at the sit bone often involves tendon and takes longer, commonly three months with graded loading.

When to see a professional

Calf swelling with warmth and tenderness needs urgent assessment to exclude a clot. Book an evaluation for back-of-knee pain persisting beyond three weeks, or any knee locking or giving way. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Calf swelling, warmth and tenderness, which may indicate a clot and needs urgent assessment.
  • A sudden pop at the sit bone with extensive bruising.
  • A knee that locks or gives way.
  • Progressive leg weakness or foot drop.
  • Night pain not relieved by position change.
