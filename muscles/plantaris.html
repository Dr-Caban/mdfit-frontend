Where the plantaris sends pain

The back of the knee and the upper inner calf; a sudden sharp pain here is sometimes called tennis leg. People usually come in describing pain at the upper calf or back of the knee.

What the plantaris does

Weakly assists knee flexion and plantarflexion of the ankle. It is absent in a proportion of people and is functionally minor, but is richly supplied with sensory receptors.

  • Starts at: The lateral supracondylar line of the femur, just above the lateral head of gastrocnemius.
  • Attaches to: The calcaneus, alongside or blended with the Achilles tendon, by a long slender tendon.
  • Nerve supply: Tibial nerve.

Symptoms people describe

  • A sudden sharp pain at the back of the knee or upper inner calf during activity, often described as feeling like being kicked.
  • Aching at the back of the knee.
  • Bruising appearing in the calf over the following days.
  • Pain on pushing off or rising onto the toes.
  • Tenderness between the two heads of the calf muscle.
  • Difficulty walking normally in the first days after the injury.

What makes it worse

Running or sports · Stairs, squats or hills. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the plantaris is involved:

  • Did you feel a sudden sharp pain in your calf, like being kicked or struck?
  • Did bruising appear in your calf over the following days?
  • Is the pain at the back of the knee or the upper inner calf?
  • Did it happen pushing off suddenly in sport?
  • Is pushing off or rising onto your toes painful?

Upper calf palpation. Press into the upper inner calf, between the two heads of the gastrocnemius, with the knee slightly bent. Suggests this muscle if: Focal tenderness, sometimes with a palpable gap or swelling.

Calf raise test. Try to rise onto the toes of the affected leg, holding support. Suggests this muscle if: Pain in the upper calf. An inability to rise at all raises the possibility of an Achilles rupture and needs urgent assessment.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Medial gastrocnemius tear, which is more common and presents similarly.
  • Deep vein thrombosis, which must be excluded.
  • Baker's cyst, which can rupture and cause calf pain.
  • Achilles tendon rupture.
  • Popliteus strain.

Common causes

  • A sudden push-off or lunge in racquet sports, which is why it is called tennis leg.
  • Sprinting without adequate warm-up.
  • A sudden increase in hill running.
  • Landing awkwardly from a jump.
  • Returning to sport after a period of inactivity.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Relative rest and support · First week
    Reduce walking distance and consider a small heel raise in both shoes to shorten the calf while it settles.
  2. Gentle ankle movement (video: Ankle Circles) · Ten each way, several times daily
    Keeps the ankle moving and helps circulation; avoid stretching into pain.
  3. Avoid early stretching · First two weeks
    A healing calf tear does not need stretching; it needs protected movement and then load.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Progressive calf loading (video: Eccentric Calf Drops) · Ten slow repetitions, building gradually
    Start with both feet and partial range, progressing as pain allows.
  2. Knee mobility (video: Knee Circles) · Ten each way
    This muscle crosses the knee, so knee movement matters.
  3. Walking progression · Daily
    Rebuild walking distance gradually before adding any running.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Calf strength (video: Eccentric Calf Drops) · Three sets of twelve, twice weekly
    Build to single leg work as tolerated.
  2. Hopping progression (video: Single Leg Farmer Hop) · Three sets of ten
    Reintroduces impact once strength work is comfortable.
  3. Return to sport (video: 40m Dash) · Build gradually
    Straight-line running before direction change, and direction change before full sport.

Habits that help

  • Warm up thoroughly before racquet sports and sprinting.
  • Build hill and speed volume gradually.
  • Do not stretch a fresh calf tear; load it progressively instead.
  • Have calf swelling with warmth assessed urgently rather than assuming it is muscular.

What to expect

Calf tears involving this muscle usually settle in four to six weeks with graded loading. Returning to sprinting too early is the most common reason for recurrence.

When to see a professional

Calf swelling with warmth, redness or tenderness needs urgent assessment to exclude a clot. Inability to rise onto the toes needs same-day assessment for an Achilles rupture. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Calf swelling with warmth, redness and tenderness, which may indicate a clot and needs urgent assessment.
  • Inability to rise onto the toes at all, which may indicate an Achilles rupture.
  • Shortness of breath or chest pain with calf symptoms, which is a 911 call.
  • Calf pain with no clear injury in someone recently immobile or travelling.
  • Severe swelling with numbness, which needs emergency assessment.
