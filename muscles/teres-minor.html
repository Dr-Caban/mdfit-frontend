Where the teres minor sends pain

A localized deep ache at the back of the shoulder. People usually come in describing pain at the back of the shoulder.

What the teres minor does

Rotates your arm outward (external rotation) and helps pull the head of the arm bone into the socket, stabilizing the shoulder.

  • Starts at: Upper two-thirds of the lateral border of the scapula (the outer edge of your shoulder blade).
  • Attaches to: Greater tubercle of the humerus (just below the infraspinatus insertion on your upper arm bone).
  • Nerve supply: Axillary nerve (C5, C6).

Symptoms people describe

  • A distinct, localized, deep ache at the back of the shoulder.
  • Pain is often described as a 'silver dollar' sized spot of tenderness.
  • Pain is sharp when reaching overhead or across the body.
  • Unlike infraspinatus, the pain rarely travels far down the arm.
  • Tenderness to direct pressure on the muscle, just below the bony ridge of the shoulder blade.

What makes it worse

Reaching overhead · Lying on it at night. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the teres minor is involved:

  • Do you feel a very specific, deep ache at the back of your shoulder, about the size of a coin?
  • Does pressing on the fleshy part just below the bony point of your shoulder reproduce your exact pain?
  • Is the pain mostly localized to the back of the shoulder, without traveling down your arm?
  • Does it hurt when you reach up and across your body, like putting on a seatbelt?
  • Is it painful to lie on that shoulder at night?

Hornblower's Sign. Lift your affected arm to the side to 90 degrees (parallel to the floor) and bend your elbow to 90 degrees. Try to hold this position; a positive test occurs if your arm drops into internal rotation. Suggests this muscle if: Inability to hold the externally rotated position, indicating significant weakness.

Palpation Test. Reach across your body with your opposite hand to feel the back of your painful shoulder. Find the outer edge of your shoulder blade and press firmly into the muscle just below the bony spine. Suggests this muscle if: Pressing on this spot reproduces your specific, deep ache.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Infraspinatus strain (often occur together)
  • Posterior shoulder impingement
  • Labral tear (SLAP lesion)
  • Thoracic outlet syndrome

Common causes

  • Overuse in throwing sports or racquet sports.
  • Working with arms overhead for long periods (e.g., electrician, painter).
  • A sudden forceful outward rotation of the arm.
  • Chronic poor posture with rounded shoulders.
  • Imbalance between the strong internal rotators (chest/lats) and weaker external rotators.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Teres Minor Ball Release · 30-60 seconds, 3-5 times daily
    Place a lacrosse ball between your shoulder blade and the wall, targeting the tender spot on the outer edge. Lean into the ball and hold steady pressure or make small movements.
  2. Pendulum Swings · 2 x 10 circles each way, twice daily
    Lean over, supporting your good arm on a table. Let the affected arm hang down and gently swing it in small circles to decompress the joint.
  3. Scapular CARs (video) · 2 x 5 circles each way, twice daily
    Keeping your arms straight, gently move your shoulder blades through their full range of motion: up, forward, down, and back. This promotes gentle movement and awareness.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Doorway Chest Stretch · 2 x 30 seconds, twice daily
    Place your forearms on a door frame with elbows slightly below shoulder height. Step through gently to feel a stretch across your chest, which helps improve shoulder posture.
  2. Isometric External Rotation · 3 x 10-second holds, twice daily
    Stand in a doorway with your elbow bent to 90 degrees, tucked at your side. Press the back of your hand into the door frame to activate the muscle without movement.
  3. Floor Slide Scapular Retract (video) · 2 x 10 reps, once daily
    Lie on your back with knees bent. Press your forearms into the floor and slide them overhead, focusing on keeping your shoulder blades down and back.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Rotator Cuff External Rotation (video) · 3 x 15 reps, every other day
    With a light band and elbow at your side, rotate your forearm outward. Focus on the small muscles at the back of your shoulder doing the work.
  2. Face Pull (video) · 3 x 12-15 reps, every other day
    Using a cable or band at chest height, pull the handles toward your face, driving your elbows back and externally rotating your shoulders at the end of the movement.
  3. Dumbbell Row (video) · 3 x 10-12 reps, every other day
    Strengthen the supporting back muscles by rowing a dumbbell, focusing on squeezing the shoulder blade toward the spine.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Rotator Cuff External Rotation, Scapular CARs.

Habits that help

  • Strengthen your mid-back muscles (rhomboids, mid-traps) to create a stable base for your shoulder.
  • Be mindful of your posture when sitting or standing; avoid letting your shoulders slump forward.
  • When reaching overhead, engage your core and avoid arching your lower back.
  • Warm up properly before any sport or activity involving overhead arm movements.

What to expect

Because this is a small muscle, targeted exercises can bring relief within 1-3 weeks. Building robust strength to prevent recurrence typically takes 4-6 weeks.

When to see a professional

If you have significant weakness (your arm drops in the Hornblower's test) or the localized pain doesn't improve with 2 weeks of care, schedule an evaluation to rule out a tear. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Significant weakness or inability to rotate your arm outward after an injury.
  • A 'popping' or 'clicking' sensation deep within the joint during movement.
  • Pain associated with numbness or tingling in the hand, especially the pinky and ring finger.
  • Visible atrophy or wasting of the muscles on the back of the shoulder.

Often, yes. A localized deep ache at the back of the shoulder. That referred pain is why it gets blamed on the joint or the spot that hurts instead of the muscle.
