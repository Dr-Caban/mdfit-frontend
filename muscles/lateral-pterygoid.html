Where the lateral pterygoid sends pain

Deep in the jaw joint just in front of the ear, and up into the cheek and sinus area. The most common muscular source of true TMJ pain. People usually come in describing pain at the ear area. It is also a common hidden driver behind Jaw Pain and TMJ Dysfunction.

What the lateral pterygoid does

Opens the jaw and protrudes it forward; one side acting alone swings the jaw to the opposite side. Because the upper head attaches to the joint disc, it guides the disc during opening and closing.

  • Starts at: Two heads: the upper from the greater wing of the sphenoid bone, the lower from the outer surface of the lateral pterygoid plate.
  • Attaches to: The neck of the mandible, and uniquely the capsule and articular disc of the temporomandibular joint itself.
  • Nerve supply: Trigeminal nerve (cranial nerve V, mandibular division).

Symptoms people describe

  • Deep pain directly in the jaw joint, just in front of the ear.
  • Pain referred up into the cheek and the maxillary sinus area, often mistaken for sinus trouble.
  • Clicking or popping on opening, because the muscle pulls directly on the joint disc.
  • The jaw deviating to one side as it opens.
  • Pain when protruding the jaw or biting into something thick.
  • Discomfort that is worse first thing in the morning after a night of clenching.

What makes it worse

Clenching or chewing · Stress. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the lateral pterygoid is involved:

  • Is your pain right in the joint, immediately in front of your ear?
  • Does your jaw click, pop or deviate to one side when you open?
  • Do you get cheek or sinus pain with no congestion or infection?
  • Is it painful to push your lower jaw forward?
  • Is the jaw at its worst when you wake up?

Opening path check. Open and close slowly in front of a mirror, watching the midline between your lower front teeth. Suggests this muscle if: The jaw swings to one side during opening, or traces an S-shaped path rather than a straight line.

Resisted protrusion. Place a fist gently against your chin and try to push your lower jaw forward against it, with light resistance only. Suggests this muscle if: This reproduces deep pain in the joint rather than in the muscle surface.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Temporomandibular joint internal derangement, where the disc itself is displaced.
  • Sinus inflammation.
  • Dental problems in the upper molars.
  • Trigeminal neuralgia.
  • Ear joint or middle ear problems.

Common causes

  • Night clenching and grinding.
  • Habitually holding the jaw slightly forward, often with stress.
  • A blow to the jaw or a whiplash injury.
  • Prolonged wide opening during dental treatment.
  • Chewing hard or chewy food on one side.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Stop forward jaw habits · Throughout the day
    Notice whether you rest with the lower jaw pushed slightly forward, and let it drop back with teeth apart. This muscle works whenever the jaw is held forward.
  2. Soft diet while it settles · One to two weeks
    Smaller bites, softer food, nothing that needs a wide opening or sustained chewing.
  3. Nervous system down-shift (video: Box Breathing) · 5 minutes, once or twice daily
    Slow breathing reduces the background clenching drive that keeps this muscle loaded.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Guided opening with tongue contact · Ten slow repetitions, several times daily
    Tongue on the roof of the mouth, open only while contact is kept. This limits the forward translation that irritates the joint.
  2. Controlled lateral glide · Ten each way, daily
    Small, even side-to-side movements in front of a mirror, stopping well short of pain.
  3. Head and neck alignment (video: Chin Glide Seated) · Two sets of ten, several times daily
    Restores the head position that lets the jaw hang in neutral.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Deep neck flexor endurance (video: Deep Cervical Flexor Nods Seated) · Ten second holds, five repetitions, daily
    Supports the head without recruiting the jaw.
  2. Desk posture resets (video: Seated Desk Posture Reset) · Every 30 minutes at a desk
    Repeated short resets work better than one long correction.
  3. Trunk control (video: Dead Bug) · Three sets of ten each side, every other day
    A stable base reduces compensatory neck and jaw tension.

Habits that help

  • Keep teeth apart unless you are chewing.
  • Avoid wide yawning by supporting the chin with a hand.
  • Avoid resting your chin in your hand, which pushes the jaw sideways.
  • Raise clenching with your dentist, who can assess whether a night guard would help.

What to expect

Joint-related jaw pain often improves within two to four weeks once clenching and forward jaw habits change. Clicking may persist even after pain settles, which is common and not by itself a reason for concern.

When to see a professional

Book an evaluation if the jaw locks, if opening is limited beyond three weeks, or if clicking is accompanied by pain that is getting worse. Jaw pain brought on by exertion needs a physician the same day. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • A jaw that locks open or locks shut.
  • A sudden change in how your teeth meet.
  • Numbness in the chin, lip or jaw.
  • Pain with fever or swelling, which suggests infection.
  • Jaw pain brought on by exertion, which needs a physician the same day, because jaw pain can be a symptom of a heart problem.
