Where the semitendinosus sends pain

The buttock crease and the back of the thigh, into the inner back of the knee and sometimes the upper inner calf. People usually come in describing pain at the buttock crease, inside of the knee or back of the thigh.

What the semitendinosus does

Extends the hip, flexes the knee and rotates the lower leg inward when the knee is bent. Its long, rope-like tendon is easy to feel on the inner side behind the knee.

  • Starts at: The ischial tuberosity, the sit bone, by a tendon shared with the long head of biceps femoris.
  • Attaches to: The upper inner surface of the tibia as part of the pes anserinus, alongside sartorius and gracilis.
  • Nerve supply: Sciatic nerve, tibial division.

Symptoms people describe

  • Pain at the buttock crease, right on the sit bone.
  • Aching down the back of the thigh toward the inner knee.
  • A cord-like tender band on the inner side behind the knee.
  • Pain that is worse sitting on a hard surface.
  • Discomfort with hill running or accelerating.
  • A pulling sensation on bending forward with straight legs.

What makes it worse

Sitting · Running or sports · Stairs, squats or hills. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the semitendinosus is involved:

  • Is your pain right at the sit bone where you sit down?
  • Can you feel a tender rope-like tendon on the inner side behind your knee?
  • Is sitting on a hard surface painful?
  • Is it worse with hill running or accelerating?
  • Does bending forward with straight legs pull at the back of your thigh?

Inner hamstring tendon palpation. Bend your knee to 90 degrees and feel behind the inner side of the knee for the prominent cord. Press along it and up the inner back of the thigh. Suggests this muscle if: Tenderness along the cord or the inner thigh reproduces the pain.

Resisted knee flexion with inward rotation. Lying face down with the knee bent, turn the foot inward and resist as the knee is straightened. Suggests this muscle if: Pain felt on the inner side of the back of the thigh rather than the outer.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Proximal hamstring tendinopathy.
  • Sciatic nerve irritation.
  • Ischial bursitis.
  • Pes anserine bursitis at the inner knee.
  • Deep hip rotator pain.

Common causes

  • Sprinting and acceleration, particularly without a proper warm-up.
  • A sudden increase in hill running.
  • Prolonged sitting on a hard surface.
  • Aggressive hamstring stretching.
  • Weak glutes, which make the hamstrings work as the main hip extensor.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Reduce sitting pressure · Ongoing
    A cushion and regular standing breaks take direct load off the sit bone.
  2. Stop aggressive stretching · Two weeks
    Hamstring tendon problems are usually made worse by stretching, not better.
  3. Gentle isometric load · Five holds of 30 seconds, daily
    Press the heel gently into the floor with the knee slightly bent. Pain-free isometrics settle tendons better than stretching.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Hip hinge pattern (video: Dowel Hip Hinge) · Three sets of ten
    Teaches the hips to move properly so the hamstrings are loaded in a position they tolerate.
  2. Controlled lengthening (video: Eccentric Toe Reach) · Ten slow repetitions
    Eccentric loading builds tendon tolerance.
  3. Hip rotation (video: 90/90 Hip Switch) · Ten each side
    Restores hip mobility so the hamstring is not compensating.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Romanian deadlift (video) · Three sets of eight, twice weekly
    The main loading exercise. Build weight gradually and keep it pain free.
  2. Glute strength (video: Hip Thrust) · Three sets of ten
    Strong glutes reduce the hamstring's workload.
  3. Return to speed (video: 40m Dash) · Build gradually
    Only once strength work is pain free; reintroduce speed in stages.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Romanian Deadlift, Dowel Hip Hinge, Eccentric Toe Reach.

Habits that help

  • Warm up properly before sprinting or hill work.
  • Use a cushion if you sit on hard surfaces.
  • Load the hamstrings rather than stretching them when they are sore.
  • Build speed and hill volume gradually.

What to expect

Hamstring muscle strains often settle in three to six weeks. Pain right at the sit bone usually means the tendon is involved, which commonly takes three months or more and needs patient loading.

When to see a professional

Book an evaluation if sit bone pain persists beyond three weeks. A sudden pop with bruising, inability to walk, or any leg weakness needs prompt assessment. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • A sudden pop at the sit bone with extensive bruising, which may be a tendon avulsion.
  • Inability to walk after a sudden hamstring injury.
  • Progressive leg weakness or foot drop.
  • Saddle numbness or loss of bladder or bowel control, which needs emergency care.
  • Night pain not relieved by position change.
