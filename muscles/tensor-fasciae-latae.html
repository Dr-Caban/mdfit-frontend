Where the tensor fasciae latae sends pain

Front-outer hip down the outside of the thigh, usually stopping at the knee. People usually come in describing pain at the outside of the hip, outside of the thigh, outside of the knee or front of the hip bone.

What the tensor fasciae latae does

Flexes, abducts, and internally rotates the hip. It helps stabilize the pelvis when you're standing on one leg.

  • Starts at: Anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS) - the bony point at the front of your hip.
  • Attaches to: Iliotibial (IT) band.
  • Nerve supply: Superior gluteal nerve (L4, L5, S1).

Symptoms people describe

  • A sharp or aching pain at the front and/or outer part of the hip.
  • Pain that refers down the outside of the thigh, often mistaken for IT band syndrome.
  • Discomfort that worsens when lying on the affected side at night.
  • Pain when standing up after prolonged sitting.
  • A feeling of tightness or restriction at the front of the hip pocket area.
  • Pain that is aggravated by running, especially on a banked surface.

What makes it worse

Sitting · Running or sports · Lying on it at night. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the tensor fasciae latae is involved:

  • Is your pain located at the very front and side of your hip, right where a front pocket would be?
  • Does the pain travel down the outside of your thigh, but usually stops before reaching your knee?
  • Is it painful to lie directly on the affected hip at night?
  • Do you feel a sharp 'catch' when you lift your knee towards your chest or swing your leg out to the side?
  • Does your pain get worse with prolonged walking or running, but feel better with rest?

Ober's Test (Modified). Lie on your pain-free side with the bottom leg bent for stability. Let the top (painful) leg drop down behind you towards the floor. A partner can help stabilize your pelvis. Suggests this muscle if: You feel a sharp pain or strong stretch at the front/side of your hip, or the leg is unable to drop below parallel to the floor.

Resisted Hip Flexion/Abduction. While seated, try to lift your knee up and out to the side against resistance from your own hand. Suggests this muscle if: This action reproduces the specific pain at the front-side of your hip.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Trochanteric bursitis (hip bursitis).
  • IT band syndrome (though TFL is often the root cause).
  • Hip labral tear or femoroacetabular impingement (FAI).
  • Gluteus medius tendonitis or tear.
  • Lumbar spine referral (L4-L5 nerve root).

Common causes

  • Prolonged sitting with hips flexed, which shortens the muscle.
  • Running or cycling with poor mechanics, causing the TFL to overwork.
  • Weak gluteus medius muscles, forcing the TFL to compensate for pelvic stability.
  • Sleeping in the fetal position, which keeps the muscle in a shortened state.
  • Wearing worn-out shoes or running on uneven surfaces.
  • Leg length discrepancy.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. TFL Pressure Release · 60-90 seconds, twice daily
    Lie on your side with a lacrosse ball or foam roller just below and in front of the bony point of your hip. Gently roll to find the tender spot and hold pressure.
  2. 90/90 Hip Switch (video) · 2 x 10 reps, daily
    Sit on the floor and gently rotate your hips from side to side. This mobilizes the hip joint without stressing the TFL.
  3. Pillow Belt Reset (video) · 1 x 5 minutes, daily
    Use this technique to gently reset the pelvis and reduce compensatory tension in the hip flexors.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Couch Stretch (video) · 2 x 30-45 sec per side, daily
    Perform this stretch gently, focusing on squeezing the glute of the back leg to open the front of the hip. Do not force it.
  2. Standing Clamshell (video) · 3 x 15 per side, every other day
    This exercise activates the gluteus medius, which helps reduce the workload on the TFL. Focus on rotating the hip, not just lifting the leg.
  3. Standing Hip CARs (video) · 2 x 5 circles each way, daily
    Perform slow, controlled circles with your hip to improve joint mobility and control, reducing strain on the TFL.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Side Crab Walk (video) · 3 x 10 steps each way, twice weekly
    Place a band around your knees or ankles. Stay low and take slow, controlled steps sideways, keeping tension on the band to strengthen your gluteus medius.
  2. Single Leg Glute Bridge (video) · 3 x 12 per side, twice weekly
    This strengthens the gluteus maximus, a key stabilizer that prevents the TFL from overworking.
  3. Step Up (video) · 3 x 10 per side, twice weekly
    Focus on driving through the heel of the front foot and keeping your pelvis level to build single-leg stability.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Standing Hip CARs, Couch Stretch, Side Crab Walk.

Habits that help

  • Avoid sitting with your legs crossed.
  • When sleeping on your side, place a pillow between your knees to keep your hips in a neutral alignment.
  • Stand up and stretch your hips into extension (like in the Couch Stretch) every 30-60 minutes.
  • Check your running form; ensure your feet don't cross the midline ('crossover gait').

What to expect

You should notice less pain with daily activities within 1-2 weeks. Full resolution, especially for athletes, may take 4-6 weeks as you retrain movement patterns and build glute strength.

When to see a professional

Schedule a visit if you have a painful clicking in the hip, if the pain doesn't improve with 2 weeks of care, or if it significantly impacts your ability to walk or sleep. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • A 'clicking' or 'locking' sensation deep inside the hip joint.
  • Inability to bear weight on the leg.
  • Pain accompanied by fever, chills, or unexplained weight loss.
  • Pain that is severe and constant, even at rest.
