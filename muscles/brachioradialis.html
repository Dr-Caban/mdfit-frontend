Where the brachioradialis sends pain

Outside of the elbow and the web of the thumb. People usually come in describing pain at the thumb and index finger or front of the elbow. It is also a common hidden driver behind Tennis Elbow (Lateral Epicondylitis).

What the brachioradialis does

Bends the elbow, especially with the forearm in a neutral, thumb-up position. Key movements include hammering, shaking hands, or lifting a grocery bag.

  • Starts at: The lower, outer edge of the upper arm bone (humerus).
  • Attaches to: The bony point on the thumb-side of the wrist (styloid process of the radius).
  • Nerve supply: Radial nerve (from neck roots C5, C6, C7).

Symptoms people describe

  • Aching pain on the top and outside of the forearm, near the elbow.
  • Pain that can travel down the forearm to the web of the thumb.
  • A feeling of weakness when gripping or lifting objects.
  • Sharp pain when turning a doorknob, using a screwdriver, or typing.
  • The 'meaty' part of the top of your forearm feels tender to the touch.
  • Difficulty holding a coffee mug or other heavy items.

What makes it worse

Gripping, lifting or typing. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the brachioradialis is involved:

  • Does your pain feel worse when you lift something with your palm facing sideways, like carrying a grocery bag?
  • Do you feel an ache on the outside of your elbow that seems to travel down toward your thumb?
  • Is it painful to shake hands firmly or turn a key in a lock?
  • Does your grip feel weak, making it hard to hold a coffee mug or a hammer?
  • Is the pain located more on the muscular part of your forearm, rather than directly on the bony point of the elbow?

Resisted Elbow Flexion Test. Rest your forearm on a table, thumb pointing to the ceiling. Use your other hand to gently press down on your wrist while you try to bend your elbow. Suggests this muscle if: Pain or a feeling of weakness in the muscle on top of your forearm near the elbow.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Lateral epicondylitis ('Tennis Elbow')
  • De Quervain's tenosynovitis (thumb tendon pain)
  • Radial nerve entrapment
  • C6 or C7 cervical radiculopathy (pinched nerve in the neck)

Common causes

  • Repetitive gripping activities like hammering, using tools, or extensive gardening.
  • Carrying heavy objects with straight arms, such as suitcases or shopping bags.
  • Sports involving forceful wrist and elbow movements like tennis, rock climbing, or rowing.
  • Typing for long hours with poor wrist posture.
  • A sudden, forceful pull on the arm, like catching a falling object.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Brachioradialis Pressure Release · 3 x 30-60 sec, daily
    Sit with your forearm resting on a table, palm down. Use the thumb of your other hand to apply firm, sustained pressure to tender spots in the muscle belly on top of your forearm.
  2. Gentle Wrist Circles (video: Weighted Wrist Circles) · 2 x 10 circles each way, twice daily
    Without any weight, gently rotate your wrist in slow, controlled circles. Focus on a pain-free range of motion to improve joint mobility and blood flow.
  3. Diaphragmatic Breathing (video: 90/90 Breathing) · 5 minutes, 1-2 times daily
    Lie on your back with feet up on a chair to relax the nervous system. Breathe in through your nose, letting your belly rise, and exhale slowly through your mouth.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Brachioradialis Stretch · 3 x 30 sec, daily
    Extend your arm straight out, palm down. Gently use your other hand to bend your wrist downward, then slightly turn your hand inward (like pouring a pitcher) to feel a stretch on top of the forearm.
  2. Eccentric Wrist Extension (video: Eccentric Wrist Drops) · 3 x 15, every other day
    Rest your forearm on your thigh, palm down, holding a very light weight. Use your other hand to lift the weight up, then slowly lower it down over 3-5 seconds.
  3. Radial Nerve Glide (video: Neck Neuroglide Seated) · 1 x 10 reps, daily
    Follow the video cues for the radial nerve portion: make a fist, bend your wrist, turn your arm in, and gently reach back while tilting your head away. Move slowly and without pain.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Hammer Curls (video: Bicep Curl) · 3 x 10-12, every other day
    Hold a dumbbell in each hand with your palms facing each other (thumb up). Bend your elbows to lift the weights toward your shoulders, keeping your wrists straight. Lower slowly.
  2. Farmer Carry (video) · 3 x 30 sec walks, every other day
    Hold a moderately heavy weight in each hand at your sides. Walk with tall posture, keeping your shoulders back and down, engaging your grip and core.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Eccentric Wrist Pronator, Eccentric Wrist Drops.

Habits that help

  • Take frequent breaks from typing or manual work to stretch your wrists and forearms.
  • Use ergonomic tools with thicker handles to reduce grip force.
  • Lift heavy objects with palms facing up or towards each other, and keep them close to your body.
  • Adjust your desk and chair height to keep wrists in a neutral position. See our 'Desk Setup Tutorial'.

What to expect

With consistent adherence to the protocol, most people feel a significant reduction in pain within 2-4 weeks. Full recovery and strength may take 6-8 weeks.

When to see a professional

Book an evaluation if your pain is severe, doesn't improve after 2 weeks of self-care, or if you experience significant numbness or weakness. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Sudden, severe swelling and bruising after an injury.
  • Inability to move your wrist or elbow through their full range of motion.
  • Numbness or tingling that is constant and does not change with position.
  • A visible deformity in the arm or wrist after a fall.

Frequently asked questions

Can a tight brachioradialis cause pain at the thumb and index finger?

Often, yes. Outside of the elbow and the web of the thumb. That referred pain is why it gets blamed on the joint or the spot that hurts instead of the muscle.

How long does brachioradialis pain take to settle?

With consistent adherence to the protocol, most people feel a significant reduction in pain within 2-4 weeks. Full recovery and strength may take 6-8 weeks.

When should I see someone about it?

Book an evaluation if your pain is severe, doesn't improve after 2 weeks of self-care, or if you experience significant numbness or weakness.

What else can feel like brachioradialis pain?

Lateral epicondylitis ('Tennis Elbow'), De Quervain's tenosynovitis (thumb tendon pain), Radial nerve entrapment. An evaluation separates muscle pain from these, and anything that needs a physician is referred on.
