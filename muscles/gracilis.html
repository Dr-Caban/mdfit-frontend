Where the gracilis sends pain

A hot, superficial, stinging line down the inner thigh, sometimes continuing to the inner knee. People usually come in describing pain at the inner thigh, inside of the knee or groin.

What the gracilis does

Adducts the hip and flexes the knee, and helps rotate the lower leg inward. It is the only adductor that crosses both the hip and the knee.

  • Starts at: The body and inferior ramus of the pubis.
  • Attaches to: The upper inner surface of the tibia as part of the pes anserinus, alongside sartorius and semitendinosus.
  • Nerve supply: Obturator nerve.

Symptoms people describe

  • A hot, stinging or superficial line of pain down the inner thigh.
  • Pain that feels close to the surface rather than deep.
  • Discomfort continuing down to the inner knee.
  • Pain with wide stance movements and sudden direction change.
  • A pulling sensation in the inner thigh when straightening the knee.
  • Soreness after breaststroke swimming or horse riding.

What makes it worse

Running or sports · Stairs, squats or hills · Sitting. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the gracilis is involved:

  • Is your inner thigh pain a hot or stinging line rather than a deep ache?
  • Does it run from your groin all the way toward your inner knee?
  • Do wide stance movements or direction changes provoke it?
  • Do you swim breaststroke, ride, or play a cutting sport?
  • Do you also get inner knee pain?

Inner thigh line palpation. Press along the inner thigh from the groin down toward the inner knee, following the most superficial band. Suggests this muscle if: Tenderness runs as a line along the muscle rather than in one spot.

Straight knee adductor stretch. Sit with legs wide and the knees straight, and lean gently forward. Suggests this muscle if: The familiar stinging line appears along the inner thigh. With the knee bent, gracilis is slackened and the pain usually eases, which helps distinguish it from the other adductors.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Adductor longus or magnus strain.
  • Osteitis pubis.
  • Pes anserine bursitis at the inner knee.
  • Obturator nerve irritation.
  • Hip joint problems.

Common causes

  • Breaststroke swimming.
  • Horse riding.
  • Cutting and changing direction in sport.
  • A sudden increase in wide stance training such as sumo squats.
  • Stretching too aggressively into the splits.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Reduce the provoking movement · Two weeks
    Pause breaststroke, wide stance squats or riding while it settles.
  2. Gentle adductor lengthening (video: Adductor Lean) · Thirty seconds each side
    Knees bent to begin with, which slackens this muscle specifically.
  3. Warmth before movement · Ten minutes
    A warm pack along the inner thigh before mobility work.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Progressive lateral range (video: Cossack Squat) · Ten each side, daily
    Load the inner thigh through range with control, moving from bent to straight knee as tolerated.
  2. Hip rotation (video: 90/90 Hip Switch) · Ten each side
    Restores rotation so the adductors are not absorbing it.
  3. Knee mobility (video: Knee Circles) · Ten each way
    This muscle crosses the knee, so the knee needs to move freely.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Adductor strength (video: Standing Hip Abduction/Adduction) · Three sets of twelve each side
    Build adductor capacity progressively.
  2. Wide stance loading (video: Goblet Sumo Squat) · Three sets of ten
    Reloads the wide position once pain free.
  3. Direction change (video: Side Crab Walk) · Three sets of ten metres each way
    Prepares for lateral movement in sport.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Adductor Lean, Cossack Squat, Standing Hip Abduction/Adduction.

Habits that help

  • Warm up the inner thigh before swimming or cutting sports.
  • Build wide stance training gradually.
  • Avoid forcing end-range splits stretching.
  • Strengthen the adductors rather than only stretching them.

What to expect

Gracilis strains usually settle in three to five weeks. Because it crosses two joints it is loaded in more positions than the other adductors, which makes a graded return important.

When to see a professional

Book an evaluation if inner thigh pain has not settled in three weeks. Groin pain with a bulge, fever or urinary symptoms needs a physician first. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • A bulge in the groin on straining.
  • Groin pain with fever or urinary symptoms.
  • Sudden severe pain with inability to bear weight.
  • Thigh pain after a fall, especially with thinning bones.
  • Progressive numbness in the inner thigh.

Frequently asked questions

Can a tight gracilis cause pain at the inner thigh?

Often, yes. A hot, superficial, stinging line down the inner thigh, sometimes continuing to the inner knee. That referred pain is why it gets blamed on the joint or the spot that hurts instead of the muscle.

How long does gracilis pain take to settle?

Gracilis strains usually settle in three to five weeks. Because it crosses two joints it is loaded in more positions than the other adductors, which makes a graded return important.

When should I see someone about it?

Book an evaluation if inner thigh pain has not settled in three weeks. Groin pain with a bulge, fever or urinary symptoms needs a physician first.

What else can feel like gracilis pain?

Adductor longus or magnus strain., Osteitis pubis., Pes anserine bursitis at the inner knee.. An evaluation separates muscle pain from these, and anything that needs a physician is referred on.
