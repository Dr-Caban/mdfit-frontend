Where the upper trapezius sends pain

Up the side of the neck, behind the ear and into the temple (a question-mark shape). Very common tension headache source. People usually come in describing pain at the temple, top of the shoulder, where the neck meets the shoulder, base of the skull, ear area, side of the neck or back of the neck.

What the upper trapezius does

Shrugs the shoulders up, tilts and turns the head, and helps stabilize the shoulder blade.

  • Starts at: Base of the skull (occiput), nuchal ligament, and spinous process of C7.
  • Attaches to: Outer third of the clavicle (collarbone) and acromion process of the scapula (shoulder blade).
  • Nerve supply: Spinal accessory nerve (CN XI) and cervical nerves (C3, C4).

Symptoms people describe

  • Pain that wraps up the side of the neck to the temple, like a question mark.
  • A very common source of 'tension headaches'.
  • A heavy, aching, or burning feeling on top of the shoulder.
  • Stiffness in the neck, especially when tilting your head to the side.
  • Pain can be constant and is often worse at the end of a stressful workday.

What makes it worse

Stress · Desk, phone or screens · Gripping, lifting or typing. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the upper trapezius is involved:

  • Does your pain start on top of your shoulder and travel up the side of your neck to your temple?
  • Do you feel a constant, heavy weight or ache on your shoulders, especially when stressed?
  • Does carrying a heavy bag or purse on your shoulder make the pain significantly worse?
  • Does tilting your head away from the painful side and gently pulling with your hand reproduce the pain?
  • Do you often find yourself shrugging your shoulders up toward your ears when you're concentrating or cold?

Pincer Grip Palpation. Use your opposite hand to grasp the thick muscle on top of your shoulder, between your neck and shoulder point. Squeeze gently and search for a tight, tender band of muscle. Suggests this muscle if: Finding a very tender spot that, when squeezed, refers pain up the side of your neck or into your temple.

Active Side Bend. Sit tall and slowly tilt your ear toward your shoulder without shrugging. Compare the range of motion and sensation on both sides. Suggests this muscle if: Significant tightness, pulling, or pain on the stretched side, or a noticeable difference in how far you can go compared to the other side.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Cervical disc issues (radiculopathy)
  • Neck joint (facet) arthritis
  • Thoracic Outlet Syndrome
  • Levator scapulae pain (more at the angle of the neck)

Common causes

  • Chronic stress, which leads to unconscious shoulder shrugging.
  • Poor posture, especially slouching with a forward head.
  • Carrying heavy bags, backpacks, or children on one side.
  • Repetitive arm movements with poor shoulder stability (e.g., typing with a high keyboard).
  • Chilling of the muscle, like sitting in a draft.
  • Trauma such as whiplash.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Upper Trap Release · 2-3 minutes per side, once or twice daily
    Place a lacrosse ball between your upper trap muscle and a wall. Lean into the ball and gently roll up and down and side-to-side to find and release tender spots.
  2. Upper Trap Stretch (video: Upper Trap Stretch Seated) · 2 x 30 seconds each side, twice daily
    Sit tall and gently tilt your ear toward your opposite shoulder. Use your hand for a light overpressure, but do not force it.
  3. Shoulder Shrugs & Drops · 2 x 15 reps, several times daily
    Inhale and shrug your shoulders up to your ears, then exhale with a sigh and let them drop completely. This helps reset muscle tension.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Eccentric Neck Stretch (video: Towel Neck Stretch Eccentric) · 2 x 5 slow reps each side, daily
    Use a towel to gently resist as you slowly return your head from a side-bent position to neutral. This builds control and resilience in the muscle.
  2. Scapular CARs (video) · 1 x 5 circles each direction, daily
    Controlled Articular Rotations help improve the movement and awareness of your shoulder blades, which can reduce the workload on the upper traps.
  3. Seated Posture Reset (video: Seated Desk Posture Reset) · Hold for 30 seconds, every hour
    Regularly resetting your posture helps train your body to stop over-relying on the upper traps for support.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Farmer Carry (video) · 3 sets of 30-second walks, twice a week
    Carry a moderate weight in each hand. Keep your chest up and shoulders pulled down and back. This teaches the traps to stabilize the shoulder girdle correctly.
  2. Face Pull (video) · 3 x 12-15 reps, twice a week
    This exercise strengthens the mid-back and external rotators, which helps to counterbalance the pull of tight upper traps and improve posture.
  3. Wall Slides (video: Floor Slide Scapular Retract) · 3 x 10 reps, every other day
    This can also be done standing against a wall. It activates the lower trapezius and serratus anterior, muscles that help depress the shoulder blades and offload the upper traps.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Upper Trap Stretch Seated, Towel Neck Stretch Eccentric, Seated Desk Posture Reset.

Habits that help

  • Adjust your chair and keyboard so your elbows are at a 90-degree angle and your shoulders are relaxed.
  • Use a backpack with two straps instead of a single-strap shoulder bag.
  • Be mindful of cradling your phone. Use a headset or speakerphone instead.
  • During stressful moments, take a deep breath and consciously drop your shoulders away from your ears.

What to expect

You can expect significant relief from pain and headaches within 1-2 weeks of consistent stretching and release work. Lasting relief comes from building strength and changing habits over 4-6 weeks.

When to see a professional

If you have persistent numbness or tingling in your arm, or if the pain doesn't improve with conservative care, book an appointment for a more detailed assessment. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Pain accompanied by numbness, tingling, or weakness that radiates down the entire arm.
  • Sudden, severe headache or neck pain after an injury.
  • Unexplained swelling or a lump in the neck/shoulder area.
  • Pain associated with fever, chills, or unexplained weight loss.
