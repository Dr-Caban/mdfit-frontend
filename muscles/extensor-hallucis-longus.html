Where the extensor hallucis longus sends pain

The top of the foot along the line of the big toe, and the front of the ankle. People usually come in describing pain at the front of the ankle or big toe.

What the extensor hallucis longus does

Extends the great toe and assists dorsiflexion and inversion of the foot. It lifts the big toe clear of the ground during the swing phase of walking.

  • Starts at: The middle portion of the anterior surface of the fibula and the interosseous membrane.
  • Attaches to: The base of the distal phalanx of the great toe.
  • Nerve supply: Deep fibular nerve, a branch of the common fibular nerve.

Symptoms people describe

  • Pain across the top of the foot following the line to the big toe.
  • Aching at the front of the ankle.
  • Pain worse with running, particularly uphill.
  • Discomfort from tight shoe laces pressing on the top of the foot.
  • A tendon that stands out prominently when the big toe is lifted.
  • Difficulty lifting the big toe, which may indicate nerve involvement.

What makes it worse

Running or sports · Stairs, squats or hills. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the extensor hallucis longus is involved:

  • Is your pain across the top of your foot toward the big toe?
  • Do tight shoe laces make it worse?
  • Is running, especially uphill, painful?
  • Is the front of your ankle tender?
  • Can you lift your big toe as strongly as on the other side?

Resisted big toe extension. Lift the big toe upward against resistance from a finger on top of the toe, comparing both sides. Suggests this muscle if: Pain along the top of the foot, or clear weakness compared with the other side, which needs assessment for nerve involvement.

Tendon palpation. Lift the big toe to make the tendon stand out across the top of the foot, then press along it toward the ankle. Suggests this muscle if: Tenderness along the tendon reproduces the pain.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Extensor tendinopathy across the top of the foot.
  • Stress fracture of a metatarsal.
  • Anterior tarsal tunnel syndrome, which compresses the deep fibular nerve.
  • Big toe joint arthritis.
  • Peroneal nerve problems causing foot drop.

Common causes

  • Tight shoe laces or footwear pressing on the top of the foot.
  • A sudden increase in running, especially uphill.
  • Downhill walking, which loads the front of the shin.
  • Direct trauma to the top of the foot.
  • Compression of the nerve at the front of the ankle.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Lacing change · Immediately
    Skip the eyelets over the painful area, or use a parallel lacing pattern to take the pressure off.
  2. Reduce uphill running · Two weeks
    Uphill running loads the front of the shin and the toe extensors heavily.
  3. Gentle ankle movement (video: Ankle Circles) · Ten each way
    Keeps the tendon gliding without loading it.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Tendon gliding (video: Ankle Neuroglide) · Ten slow repetitions
    Gentle movement through range to keep the tendon moving freely.
  2. Calf lengthening (video: Eccentric Calf Drops) · Ten slow repetitions
    A tight calf makes the front of the shin work harder to clear the foot.
  3. Big toe mobility · Ten repetitions, twice daily
    Move the big toe up and down by hand through its comfortable range.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Foot lifting strength (video: Heel Walk) · Three sets of 20 metres
    Loads the front of the shin and the toe extensors directly.
  2. Balance (video: Single Leg Balance) · Three holds of 30 seconds each side
    Builds whole-foot control.
  3. Graded running return · Build over weeks
    Return to flat running first, adding hills only once pain free.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Heel Walk, Ankle Neuroglide, Ankle Circles.

Habits that help

  • Check lacing pressure, especially in new shoes.
  • Build uphill and downhill running volume gradually.
  • Protect the top of the foot from direct pressure.
  • Have any weakness lifting the foot or toe assessed rather than trained through.

What to expect

Irritation from footwear pressure often settles within one to two weeks once the pressure is removed. Tendon irritation from training load takes four to six weeks.

When to see a professional

Any weakness lifting the big toe or the foot needs prompt assessment for nerve involvement. Book an evaluation for top-of-foot pain persisting beyond three weeks. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Weakness lifting the big toe or the foot, which may indicate nerve compression and needs assessment.
  • Foot drop, which needs prompt medical assessment.
  • Top-of-foot pain after a fall, which may indicate a fracture.
  • Swelling with redness, warmth and fever.
  • Numbness in the web between the first and second toes.

Can a tight extensor hallucis longus cause pain at the front of the ankle or big toe?

Often, yes. The top of the foot along the line of the big toe, and the front of the ankle. That referred pain is why it gets blamed on the joint or the spot that hurts instead of the muscle.

How long does extensor hallucis longus pain take to settle?

Irritation from footwear pressure often settles within one to two weeks once the pressure is removed. Tendon irritation from training load takes four to six weeks.

When should I see someone about it?

Any weakness lifting the big toe or the foot needs prompt assessment for nerve involvement. Book an evaluation for top-of-foot pain persisting beyond three weeks.

What else can feel like extensor hallucis longus pain?

Extensor tendinopathy across the top of the foot., Stress fracture of a metatarsal., Anterior tarsal tunnel syndrome, which compresses the deep fibular nerve.. An evaluation separates muscle pain from these, and anything that needs a physician is referred on.
