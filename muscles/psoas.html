Where the psoas sends pain

Vertical band along the low back, plus the front of the thigh and groin. Hard to stand up straight after sitting. People usually come in describing pain at the right beside the spine (low back), belly or side of the abdomen, front of the thigh or groin. It is also a common hidden driver behind Low Back Pain.

What the psoas does

A powerful hip flexor, it lifts your thigh toward your chest. It also helps stabilize your lower back.

  • Starts at: Sides of the lower thoracic and lumbar vertebrae (T12-L5) and their discs.
  • Attaches to: Lesser trochanter of the femur (a small knob on the inside of the upper thigh bone).
  • Nerve supply: Anterior rami of lumbar nerves L1, L2, and L3.

Symptoms people describe

  • A deep, vertical band of aching pain along one or both sides of your lower back.
  • Pain that refers to the front of your thigh and groin area.
  • Difficulty and pain when trying to stand up straight after sitting for a while.
  • A feeling of a 'catch' or 'click' deep in the front of the hip.
  • Pain that gets worse with prolonged sitting or walking.
  • Disrupted sleep, especially when trying to lie flat on your back.

What makes it worse

Sitting · Standing or walking. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the psoas is involved:

  • Do you feel a deep ache running vertically up your low back, right next to the spine?
  • Is it difficult or painful to stand up fully straight after sitting for more than 20-30 minutes?
  • Does your pain also show up in the front of your thigh or groin?
  • Does lying flat on your back feel uncomfortable or increase your back ache?
  • Do you feel better when you bring your knees toward your chest while lying down?

Thomas Test (Modified). Sit on the edge of a sturdy bed or table. Lie back, pulling both knees to your chest. Hold one knee tightly and let the other leg hang down. Your low back must stay flat on the surface. Suggests this muscle if: If the hanging thigh doesn't touch the surface or if the knee straightens out on its own, your psoas is likely tight.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Lumbar disc herniation
  • Hip joint arthritis or labral tear
  • Appendicitis (on the right side)
  • Kidney stone pain
  • Inguinal hernia

Common causes

  • Prolonged sitting with hips flexed (e.g., desk work, driving).
  • Sleeping in a fetal position.
  • Repetitive hip flexion activities like running, cycling, or sit-ups.
  • A sudden fall or slip that forces the leg back.
  • Stress and shallow breathing patterns, as the psoas is connected to the diaphragm.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Psoas Release (Ball) · 2-3 minutes, once daily
    Lie on your stomach. Place a soft ball (4-6 inches) just inside your hip bone. Gently sink your weight onto the ball, breathing deeply. Avoid pulsing or painful pressure.
  2. Constructive Rest Position · 5-10 minutes, once or twice daily
    Lie on your back with your knees bent and feet flat on the floor, hip-width apart. Rest your hands on your belly and focus on deep, diaphragmatic breathing.
  3. 90/90 Breathing (video) · 5 minutes, once daily
    Lie on your back with your lower legs on a chair or box, so your hips and knees are at 90 degrees. Breathe deeply into your belly and low back.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Couch Stretch (video) · 2 x 30-60 sec per side, daily
    Kneel in a lunge position with your back shin against a wall or couch. Keep your torso upright and gently squeeze your glute to feel a stretch in the front of the hip.
  2. Hip Neuroglide (video) · 2 x 10 reps per side, daily
    Follow the video to gently mobilize the nerves that run through the front of the hip, improving their ability to slide and glide.
  3. Quadruped Glute Kick (video) · 2 x 15 reps per side, every other day
    On all fours, engage your core and lift one leg back, squeezing the glute at the top. This activates the opposing muscle to help the psoas relax.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Dead Bug (video) · 3 x 10 reps (alternating), every other day
    Lie on your back with arms and legs in the air. Lower the opposite arm and leg slowly while keeping your low back flat on the floor. This builds core stability.
  2. Single Leg Glute Bridge (video) · 3 x 12 reps per side, every other day
    Lie on your back with one foot flat on the floor and the other leg extended. Drive through your heel to lift your hips, squeezing the glute.
  3. Step Up (video) · 3 x 10 reps per side, every other day
    Step onto a box or stair, driving through the heel of your front foot. Control the movement on the way down. This strengthens the glutes and quads.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Couch Stretch, Hip Neuroglide, 90/90 Hip Switch.

Habits that help

  • Use a standing desk or take frequent breaks from sitting every 30 minutes.
  • When sitting, ensure your hips are at or slightly higher than your knees.
  • Avoid sleeping curled up in a tight fetal position; try sleeping on your back with a pillow under your knees.
  • Practice deep belly breathing throughout the day to relax the diaphragm and psoas.

What to expect

With consistent adherence to the protocol, most people feel a significant reduction in pain and stiffness within 2-4 weeks. Full restoration of strength and function may take 6-8 weeks.

When to see a professional

Book an appointment if your pain is severe, doesn't improve after 2 weeks of self-care, or is accompanied by any of the 'Red Flags'. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Pain accompanied by fever, nausea, or unexplained weight loss.
  • Loss of bowel or bladder control.
  • Numbness or tingling in the groin or 'saddle' area.
  • Severe, unrelenting abdominal or back pain that doesn't change with position.

How long does psoas pain take to settle?

With consistent adherence to the protocol, most people feel a significant reduction in pain and stiffness within 2-4 weeks. Full restoration of strength and function may take 6-8 weeks.

When should I see someone about it?

What else can feel like psoas pain?
