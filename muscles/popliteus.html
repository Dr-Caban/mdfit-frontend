Where the popliteus sends pain

Back of the knee; worse crouching, walking downhill or running. People usually come in describing pain at the back of the knee.

What the popliteus does

Internally rotates the tibia on the femur to 'unlock' the knee from a straight position, allowing it to bend. It also helps stabilize the back of the knee.

  • Starts at: Lateral condyle of the femur (outer thigh bone at the knee).
  • Attaches to: Posterior surface of the tibia (back of the shin bone).
  • Nerve supply: Tibial nerve (L4, L5, S1).

Symptoms people describe

  • Sharp pain located directly in the back of the knee.
  • Pain that is worse when walking or running, especially downhill.
  • A feeling of instability or that the knee might 'give way' when twisting.
  • Difficulty fully straightening or 'locking' the knee.
  • Pain when crouching or squatting down low.

What makes it worse

Stairs, squats or hills · Running or sports. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the popliteus is involved:

  • Is your pain located precisely in the hollow at the back of your knee?
  • Is the pain significantly worse when you walk or run downhill?
  • Do you feel a sharp pain when you try to squat down low?
  • Does your knee feel unstable, especially when twisting or turning?
  • Do you have pain when you first 'unlock' your knee from a fully straight position to begin bending it?

Resisted Knee Flexion Test. Sit on a chair and cross the ankle of your good leg over the front of the painful leg's ankle. Try to bend the painful knee against the resistance of your other leg. Suggests this muscle if: Pain in the back of the knee during this resisted movement points to the popliteus or hamstring tendons.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Posterior horn of the meniscus tear
  • Baker's cyst (a fluid-filled sac behind the knee)
  • Hamstring tendinopathy
  • Posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) injury

Common causes

  • Overuse in sports that involve a lot of downhill running (e.g., trail running).
  • A sudden twist of the knee while the foot is planted.
  • Chronic over-pronation of the foot (foot rolling inward).
  • Muscle imbalances, particularly weak hamstrings or glutes.
  • Returning to activity too quickly after a previous knee injury.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Gentle Knee Rotations (video: Knee Circles) · 2 x 10 circles each way, twice daily
    Sit on a high chair so your feet don't touch the floor. Gently swing your lower leg to make small circles, moving from the knee joint.
  2. Pressure Release Behind Knee · 2 x 30-60 sec, daily
    Sit and place a lacrosse or tennis ball in the crease behind your knee. Gently bend and straighten your leg to apply pressure to the muscle. Avoid pressing on the very center of the knee pit.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Knee Neuroglide (video) · 2 x 10-15 reps, daily
    Follow the video to gently mobilize the nerves around the back of the knee. This is a gentle flossing motion, not an aggressive stretch.
  2. Heel Slides · 2 x 15 reps, daily
    Lie on your back and slowly slide the heel of your affected leg towards your buttock, bending the knee. Slide back to the start. Move only within a pain-free range.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Terminal Knee Extensions (Band) · 3 x 15, every other day
    Anchor a resistance band to a post. Loop the other end around the back of your knee. Step back to create tension, then straighten your knee against the band's pull.
  2. Wall Sits · 3 x 30-60 sec holds, every other day
    Lean your back against a wall and slide down until your knees are at a comfortable angle. Hold this position, ensuring your knees track over your ankles.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Knee Circles, Knee Neuroglide.

Habits that help

  • Avoid locking your knees when standing for long periods.
  • When walking downhill, take shorter steps and try to land with a slightly bent knee.
  • Ensure your footwear provides adequate support to prevent over-pronation.
  • Strengthen your glute muscles (e.g., with bridges, clamshells) to improve overall leg stability.

What to expect

Because it's a small stabilizer, popliteus pain can be stubborn. Consistent, gentle exercises should bring noticeable relief in 2-3 weeks, with a return to more demanding activities in 4-6 weeks.

When to see a professional

Book an appointment if you suspect a meniscus or ligament injury, if the knee is locking or giving way, or if pain does not improve with 2-3 weeks of conservative care. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • A 'pop' at the time of injury with immediate, significant swelling.
  • Inability to put any weight on the leg.
  • A feeling of the knee being 'locked' where you cannot bend or straighten it at all.
  • Visible swelling and redness in the back of the knee.

Frequently asked questions

Can a tight popliteus cause pain at the back of the knee?

Often, yes. Back of the knee; worse crouching, walking downhill or running. That referred pain is why it gets blamed on the joint or the spot that hurts instead of the muscle.

How long does popliteus pain take to settle?

Because it's a small stabilizer, popliteus pain can be stubborn. Consistent, gentle exercises should bring noticeable relief in 2-3 weeks, with a return to more demanding activities in 4-6 weeks.

When should I see someone about it?

Book an appointment if you suspect a meniscus or ligament injury, if the knee is locking or giving way, or if pain does not improve with 2-3 weeks of conservative care.

What else can feel like popliteus pain?

Posterior horn of the meniscus tear, Baker's cyst (a fluid-filled sac behind the knee), Hamstring tendinopathy. An evaluation separates muscle pain from these, and anything that needs a physician is referred on.
