Where the frontalis sends pain

Forehead pain spreading up from the eyebrow on the same side, and a sense of pressure above the eyes. People usually come in describing pain at the forehead.

What the frontalis does

Raises the eyebrows and wrinkles the forehead horizontally. It is a muscle of facial expression and has no joint action.

  • Starts at: The galea aponeurotica, the broad tendinous sheet over the top of the skull.
  • Attaches to: The skin and muscles of the eyebrows and the root of the nose.
  • Nerve supply: Facial nerve (cranial nerve VII), temporal branch.

Symptoms people describe

  • Forehead pain spreading upward from the eyebrow.
  • A sense of pressure or heaviness above the eyes.
  • Tenderness of the forehead to touch.
  • Pain on the same side as a habitual squint or brow raise.
  • Discomfort that worsens with concentration or poor lighting.

What makes it worse

Stress · Desk, phone or screens. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the frontalis is involved:

  • Is your pain in the forehead, spreading up from your eyebrow?
  • Is your forehead tender to touch?
  • Do you raise your eyebrows or frown when concentrating?
  • Is it worse in poor light or when your glasses prescription is out of date?
  • Does it build with stress through the day?

Brow raise. Raise your eyebrows firmly and hold for a few seconds, then relax fully. Suggests this muscle if: Holding reproduces the familiar forehead ache, or releasing gives brief relief.

Forehead palpation. Press gently along the forehead just above the eyebrow and upward toward the hairline. Suggests this muscle if: Tender spots reproduce the forehead pain.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Sinus inflammation of the frontal sinus.
  • Tension-type headache.
  • Migraine.
  • Eye strain or an uncorrected refractive error.
  • Giant cell arteritis in older adults.

Common causes

  • Habitual brow raising or frowning with concentration.
  • Squinting from an out-of-date glasses prescription or glare.
  • Stress and sustained facial tension.
  • Referred tension from the back of the neck and scalp.
  • Poor screen lighting.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Face and brow release (video: Box Breathing) · 5 minutes daily

    Slow strokes from the eyebrow up toward the hairline, with light pressure.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Neck position reset (video: Chin Glide Seated) · Two sets of ten, several times daily
    Forehead symptoms are frequently driven from the neck and base of the skull.
  2. Back-of-skull release (video: Neck Neuroglide Seated) · Ten slow repetitions
    The suboccipitals and semispinalis refer into the forehead; treating them often settles this.
  3. Eye breaks · Every 20 minutes
    Look at something 20 feet away for 20 seconds, which reduces the squint-and-brace pattern.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Deep neck flexor work (video: Deep Cervical Flexor Nods Seated) · Ten second holds, five repetitions, daily
    Supports the head so the scalp and face muscles stop compensating.
  2. Postural endurance (video: Seated Desk Posture Reset) · Every 30 minutes at a desk
    Reduces the forward head position that feeds forehead tension.
  3. General conditioning (video: Walk Break Protocol Seated) · Daily
    Regular movement reduces the overall tension load that shows up in the face.

Habits that help

  • Have your eyesight checked if you catch yourself squinting.
  • Reduce screen glare and raise the screen to eye level.
  • Notice and release brow tension when concentrating.
  • Take regular eye and movement breaks.

What to expect

Forehead tension usually eases within one to two weeks once squinting, lighting and neck position are addressed, provided the neck is treated alongside it.

When to see a professional

Book an evaluation if forehead pain persists beyond three weeks. A new persistent headache after 50, or any red flag above, needs a physician first. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • A new persistent headache over the age of 50, especially with scalp tenderness or jaw pain on chewing.
  • Headache with fever and a stiff neck.
  • Vision loss or double vision.
  • A sudden, severe headache.
  • A new headache after a head injury.
