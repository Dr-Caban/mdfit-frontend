Where the foot muscles sends pain

Heel and sole of the foot; first-step-in-the-morning pain like plantar fasciitis. People usually come in describing pain at the sole of the foot or inner ankle and arch. It is also a common hidden driver behind Plantar Fasciitis & Heel Pain.

What the foot muscles does

They support the arches of the foot, control fine movements of the toes, and provide your brain with sensory information about the ground.

  • Starts at: Various bones within the foot, primarily the heel bone (calcaneus) and metatarsals.
  • Attaches to: The toe bones (phalanges). These muscles start and end entirely within the foot.
  • Nerve supply: Medial and Lateral Plantar Nerves (S1, S2)

Symptoms people describe

  • Sharp, stabbing pain in the heel or arch, especially with the first few steps in the morning.
  • Aching, cramping, or burning pain in the sole of the foot.
  • Pain that feels like you are walking on a pebble or a bruise.
  • General foot fatigue or aching after standing or walking for a while.
  • Pain that gets much worse when walking barefoot on hard surfaces.
  • Difficulty or cramping when trying to spread your toes.

What makes it worse

Standing or walking · Running or sports. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the foot muscles is involved:

  • Is your pain worst with the very first steps you take after getting out of bed or after a long period of sitting?
  • Does your pain feel like a deep ache or cramp in the arch of your foot?
  • Does walking barefoot on tile or hardwood floors make your foot pain significantly worse?
  • Do you find that your feet just feel tired and achy at the end of a long day of standing?
  • Does wearing very flat, unsupportive shoes (like flip-flops) seem to trigger your pain?

Toe Spreading. While sitting with your foot flat on the floor, try to spread your toes apart as wide as possible, then bring them back together. Suggests this muscle if: A positive test is an inability to spread the toes, cramping in the arch, or pain during the movement.

Short Foot Exercise. With your foot flat on the floor, try to lift your arch by sliding the ball of your foot toward your heel without curling your toes or lifting them off the floor. Suggests this muscle if: A positive test is an inability to create an arch, cramping, or pain.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Plantar fasciitis (these two conditions are very closely related and often occur together)
  • Tarsal tunnel syndrome (nerve entrapment)
  • Heel pad atrophy (thinning of the fat pad under the heel)
  • Calcaneal (heel bone) stress fracture
  • Nerve pain from the lower back (radiculopathy)

Common causes

  • Weakness in the small muscles of the foot, leading to poor arch support.
  • Consistently wearing unsupportive footwear like flip-flops, flat sandals, or worn-out shoes.
  • A sudden increase in weight-bearing activity like running, walking, or standing for a new job.
  • Tight calf muscles, which increases the strain on the bottom of the foot.
  • Having either very flat feet or very high, rigid arches.
  • Gaining weight, which increases the load on the feet.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Foot Rolling · 2-3 minutes, twice daily
    Roll a frozen water bottle or a lacrosse ball under the entire sole of your foot, from heel to toes. This helps massage the muscles and reduce inflammation.
  2. Big Toe and Plantar Fascia Stretch · 3 x 30 seconds, twice daily
    While sitting, gently pull your big toe back towards your shin until you feel a stretch in the arch. This is especially helpful to do before taking your first steps in the morning.
  3. 90/90 Breathing (video) · 5 minutes, once daily
    Follow the video to get into a relaxed position. While breathing, consciously focus on relaxing the muscles in your feet and letting go of tension.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Short Foot Exercise · 5 reps of 10-second holds, twice daily
    With your foot flat on the floor, shorten your foot by pulling the ball of your foot toward your heel, creating an arch. Do this without curling your toes.
  2. Towel Curls · 3 x 15 reps, once daily
    Sit with your foot on a small towel. Use only your toes to scrunch the towel towards you. This is a foundational exercise for foot strength.
  3. Toe Yoga · 3 x 5 reps of each movement, daily
    Sit with your foot flat. First, try to lift just your big toe while keeping the others down. Then, try to lift the four smaller toes while keeping the big toe down.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Toe Walk (video) · 3 x 20 steps, every other day
    Walking on your tiptoes as shown in the video strengthens the intrinsic muscles and the entire calf complex, which work together to support your foot.
  2. Single Leg Balance (video) · 3 x 30-60 seconds, daily
    While balancing, focus on actively maintaining your arch and feeling the small muscles in your foot working to keep you stable.
  3. Eccentric Calf Drops (video) · 3 x 15 reps, every other day
    Strong calf muscles and a healthy Achilles tendon reduce the load on the bottom of the foot. Follow the video, focusing on the slow lowering phase.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Toe Walk, Single Leg Balance, Eccentric Calf Drops.

Habits that help

  • Wear supportive shoes and avoid going barefoot on hard surfaces, especially when your pain is active.
  • Keep a supportive slipper or shoe by your bed to wear for your first steps in the morning.
  • Gently stretch your foot by pulling your toes back before you get out of bed.
  • Maintain a healthy weight to decrease the daily load your feet have to support.

What to expect

Heel and arch pain can be persistent. With consistent daily exercises and better footwear choices, you should notice a gradual improvement in 4-6 weeks. Full recovery can often take 3-6 months.

When to see a professional

Book an evaluation if your pain is severe, you have constant numbness or tingling, or if you see no improvement after 4 weeks of consistent self-care. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Numbness, tingling, or burning pain in the sole of the foot that doesn't go away.
  • Pain at night that is severe enough to wake you from sleep.
  • Visible swelling, redness, and warmth in the heel or arch area.
  • Inability to put any weight on the foot.
