Where the rectus abdominis sends pain

A band across the low back at belt level from the upper part, and a band across the lower abdomen from the lower part. Can feel like period pain or indigestion. People usually come in describing pain at the belly or side of the abdomen. It is also a common hidden driver behind Low Back Pain.

What the rectus abdominis does

Flexes the trunk, tilts the pelvis backward and resists extension of the low back. It also raises abdominal pressure for coughing, lifting and breathing out forcefully.

  • Starts at: The pubic crest and the pubic symphysis.
  • Attaches to: The cartilages of the fifth, sixth and seventh ribs and the xiphoid process of the breastbone.
  • Nerve supply: Anterior rami of the thoracic spinal nerves T7 to T12.

Symptoms people describe

  • A horizontal band of low back pain at belt level, with nothing wrong in the back itself.
  • A band across the lower abdomen that can be mistaken for period pain.
  • Abdominal discomfort that feels like indigestion, bloating or a stitch.
  • Pain getting out of a chair or sitting up from lying.
  • Soreness that is worse after a heavy core session or a bout of coughing.
  • Discomfort with deep breathing or laughing.

What makes it worse

Bending or lifting · Deep breaths or coughing · Sitting · Turning your head or body. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the rectus abdominis is involved:

  • Do you have a band of low back pain at belt level with no back injury?
  • Does pressing on your abdominal wall reproduce the pain?
  • Is it worse sitting up from lying, or getting out of a chair?
  • Did it start after a hard core workout, a coughing illness or heavy lifting?
  • Does it feel like indigestion or period pain that nothing medical explains?

Abdominal wall tension test. Lie on your back and find the tender spot. Then lift your head and shoulders slightly to tense the abdominal wall, and press on the same spot again. Suggests this muscle if: Tenderness stays the same or increases when the muscle is tensed, which points to the muscle wall rather than an organ beneath it.

Upper and lower band palpation. With the abdomen relaxed, press along the muscle from just below the breastbone down to the pubic bone, either side of the midline. Suggests this muscle if: Tender bands reproduce the low back or lower abdominal pain.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Abdominal organ problems, which must be excluded first.
  • Hernia at the midline or through a surgical scar.
  • Gynaecological conditions.
  • Kidney or urinary problems.
  • Lumbar spine problems.

Common causes

  • A sudden increase in abdominal training, particularly sit-ups and crunches.
  • Prolonged coughing.
  • Sustained slumped sitting, which keeps the muscle shortened.
  • Pregnancy and the postpartum period.
  • Abdominal surgery and the scarring that follows.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Breathing reset (video: 90/90 Breathing) · 5 minutes daily
    Breathe into the lower ribs and back rather than bracing the abdominal wall. A permanently braced abdomen keeps this muscle loaded.
  2. Stop crunching while sore · One to two weeks
    Pause sit-ups, crunches and leg raises. The muscle needs to be loaded differently, not more.
  3. Gentle extension (video: Thoracic Extension Over Chair) · Ten repetitions daily
    Open the front of the trunk, which is the opposite of the position the muscle is stuck in.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Dead bug (video) · Three sets of ten each side
    Teaches the abdominal wall to hold position while the limbs move, rather than shortening repeatedly.
  2. Anti-rotation holds (video: Pallof Press) · Three holds of 20 seconds each side
    Trains the trunk to resist movement, which is what it actually does in life.
  3. Hip flexor length (video: Couch Stretch) · Thirty seconds each side, daily
    Tight hip flexors tip the pelvis and keep the abdominal wall working against it.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Loaded carries (video: Farmer Carry) · Three carries of 30 seconds, twice weekly
    Builds trunk endurance under load without repeated flexion.
  2. Dynamic plank (video) · Three sets of 30 seconds
    Position-holding strength rather than crunching.
  3. Rotational control (video: Pallof Press With Rotation) · Three sets of eight each side
    Adds controlled rotation once holding is comfortable.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Dead Bug, Pallof Press, 90/90 Breathing.

Habits that help

  • Breathe into the ribs rather than holding the stomach in all day.
  • Build abdominal training gradually after illness, surgery or pregnancy.
  • Support the abdomen with a hand or pillow when coughing.
  • Have any new abdominal bulge assessed before training through it.

What to expect

Abdominal wall pain usually settles within one to three weeks once the training that provoked it is adjusted. Pain that persists longer, or that is not reproduced by pressing on the muscle, deserves another look.

When to see a professional

Abdominal pain needs a physician first if there is fever, vomiting, a bulge, blood, or any change with eating. Book an evaluation once organ causes have been excluded. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Abdominal pain with fever, vomiting or a rigid, board-like belly, which needs emergency care.
  • A visible bulge in the abdominal wall that appears on straining, which suggests a hernia.
  • Blood in the stool or urine.
  • Pain with chest pressure or shortness of breath, which is a 911 call.
  • Severe pain that is unrelieved by position change.
