Where the temporalis sends pain

Temple headache, above the eyebrow and into the upper teeth. People usually come in describing pain at the temple. It is also a common hidden driver behind Jaw Pain and TMJ Dysfunction.

What the temporalis does

Closes your jaw (elevation) and pulls it backward (retraction). It's essential for chewing and clenching.

  • Starts at: The temporal fossa, a large fan-shaped area on the side of your skull.
  • Attaches to: A bony process on your lower jaw (coronoid process of the mandible).
  • Nerve supply: Trigeminal Nerve (Cranial Nerve V, Mandibular Division).

Symptoms people describe

  • A headache felt in the temple and side of the head.
  • Pain that feels like it's above your eye or behind your eyebrow.
  • Sensitivity or aching in your upper teeth, which can be mistaken for a dental problem.
  • Pain when chewing hard or chewy foods.
  • Difficulty opening your mouth wide.
  • Pain is often worse during times of stress or concentration.

What makes it worse

Clenching or chewing · Stress. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the temporalis is involved:

  • Do you get headaches primarily in your temples?
  • Do your upper teeth ache, even though your dentist says they are healthy?
  • Is your headache worse after chewing gum, eating a tough steak, or clenching your jaw?
  • Do you wake up with a headache or a sore jaw?
  • Does the pain feel like a constant, dull ache above your eyebrow?

Temporalis Palpation Test. Place your fingertips on your temples. Clench your teeth firmly and feel the muscle bulge. Now relax and press firmly into the muscle, searching for specific, tender spots. Suggests this muscle if: Pressing on a tender spot reproduces your temple headache or tooth sensitivity.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Tension-type headache or migraine.
  • Dental problems (cavities, abscesses).
  • Trigeminal neuralgia.
  • Temporal arteritis (a serious condition in older adults).

Common causes

  • Bruxism (clenching or grinding teeth), especially during sleep or concentration.
  • Excessive gum chewing or eating very hard/chewy foods.
  • Dental issues, such as a misaligned bite or recent dental work.
  • Emotional stress and anxiety.
  • Forward head posture, which can alter jaw mechanics.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Temporalis Self-Massage · 2-3 minutes per side, as needed
    With your jaw relaxed, use your fingertips to apply firm, slow, circular pressure to the tender areas in your temples. You can also try a 'pincer' grip on the muscle.
  2. Stress Reduction Breathing (video: Box Breathing) · 5 minutes, 1-2 times daily or when stressed
    Inhale for 4 seconds, hold for 4, exhale for 4, hold for 4. This helps calm the nervous system and reduce clenching.
  3. Gentle Jaw Mobility · 10 reps, several times daily
    With your tongue on the roof of your mouth, slowly open and close your jaw only to the point of mild tension, not pain. This encourages relaxed movement.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Postural Correction (video: Chin Glide Seated) · 2 x 10 reps, several times daily
    Correcting forward head posture reduces strain on the jaw muscles. Gently glide your chin straight back without tilting your head.
  2. Tongue Posture Training · Throughout the day
    Practice resting your tongue gently on the roof of your mouth, just behind your front teeth, with your teeth slightly apart and lips together. This is the jaw's natural resting position.
  3. Controlled Jaw Opening · 2 x 8 reps, daily
    Place a finger on your chin. As you slowly open your mouth, provide gentle resistance with your finger. Then, as you close, gently resist the closing motion.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Deep Neck Flexor Activation (video: Deep Cervical Flexor Nods Seated) · 2 x 10 sec holds, 5 reps, daily
    A stable neck provides a better foundation for the jaw. Gently tuck your chin and hold, feeling the deep muscles in the front of your neck work.
  2. Postural Endurance (video: Seated Desk Posture Reset) · Every 30 minutes at your desk
    Regularly resetting your posture prevents the slow creep into a forward-head position that strains the jaw.
  3. Core Stability (video: Dead Bug) · 3 x 10 reps per side, every other day
    A strong core helps stabilize the entire spine, reducing compensatory tension in the neck and jaw.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Box Breathing, Chin Glide Seated.

Habits that help

  • Avoid chewing gum, ice, or very hard foods.
  • Cut food into smaller pieces to reduce the effort of chewing.
  • Place small sticky notes on your computer or mirror that say 'Relax Jaw' as a reminder.
  • If you clench at night, speak to your dentist about a night guard.

What to expect

You can often feel relief from acute pain within a few days of starting massage and relaxation. Lasting improvement, especially if related to clenching, may take 4-6 weeks of consistent habit changes and exercises.

When to see a professional

Book an appointment if your pain is severe, you have clicking or locking in your jaw, or if self-care doesn't provide relief after 2-3 weeks. A dentist or TMJ specialist may be needed. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Sudden, severe, one-sided headache with vision changes, fever, or jaw pain when chewing (could be temporal arteritis).
  • Headache following a head injury.
  • Inability to close your jaw after it has been opened wide (jaw dislocation).
  • Numbness or weakness in the face.
