Where the tibialis anterior sends pain

Front of the shin, front of the ankle and the big toe. People usually come in describing pain at the front of the ankle or big toe. It is also a common hidden driver behind Shin Splints and Lower-Leg Pain.

What the tibialis anterior does

Lifts the foot and toes up toward the shin (dorsiflexion) and helps turn the foot inward (inversion). It prevents your foot from 'slapping' the ground when you walk.

  • Starts at: Upper, outer surface of the shin bone (tibia).
  • Attaches to: A bone on the inside of the mid-foot (medial cuneiform) and the base of the big toe bone (first metatarsal).
  • Nerve supply: Deep Fibular (Peroneal) Nerve (L4, L5)

Symptoms people describe

  • Aching or sharp pain on the front of the shin, just to the outside of the shin bone.
  • Pain at the front of the ankle, where the foot meets the leg.
  • Pain that sometimes travels down to the top of the big toe.
  • A feeling of weakness when trying to lift your foot or toes.
  • Pain that is worse when walking or running, especially downhill or down stairs.
  • The muscle may feel tight or tender to the touch.

What makes it worse

Running or sports · Stairs, squats or hills. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the tibialis anterior is involved:

  • Is your pain located in the muscle on the front and outer side of your shin bone?
  • Does the pain sometimes refer to the front of your ankle or the top of your big toe?
  • Is the pain worse when you go down stairs or walk/run downhill?
  • Do you notice the pain after driving for a long time or after activities that involve a lot of foot tapping?
  • Do your shoes feel too tight across the top of your foot?

Resisted Foot Lift. While sitting, cross your legs so the affected ankle rests on the other knee. Place your hand on top of your foot and try to lift your foot up against your hand's resistance. Suggests this muscle if: A positive test is pain or noticeable weakness in the muscle on the front of your shin.

Heel Walk. Try to walk for 10-15 steps on your heels only, keeping the front part of your feet off the ground. Suggests this muscle if: A positive test is a sharp increase in your shin pain or an inability to perform the walk.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Shin splints (medial tibial stress syndrome)
  • Tibial stress fracture
  • Chronic exertional compartment syndrome
  • Deep fibular (peroneal) nerve entrapment

Common causes

  • Overuse from activities like running (especially downhill), hiking, or kicking sports.
  • Tight calf muscles, which force the tibialis anterior to work harder to lift the foot.
  • Switching to minimalist shoes or a different running style too quickly.
  • Lacing shoes, boots, or skates too tightly across the front of the ankle.
  • Driving for long periods, keeping the foot constantly lifted.
  • Weakness in the muscle, leading to fatigue and strain.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Tibialis Anterior Release · 2 minutes, once daily
    Sit on a chair or the floor. Use your thumbs or a lacrosse ball to apply firm, sustained pressure to tender spots in the muscle on the front/outside of your shin.
  2. Gentle Ankle Circles · 10 circles each direction, 3 times per day
    Sit or lie down and slowly rotate your ankle in large, gentle circles. This improves mobility and blood flow without straining the muscle.
  3. Calf Stretch · 2 x 30 seconds per side, twice daily
    Stand facing a wall with one foot back. Keep the back leg straight and heel on the floor to stretch the upper calf. Then, bend the back knee to stretch the lower calf.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Kneeling Shin Stretch · 2 x 30 seconds, once daily
    Kneel on a soft surface with the tops of your feet flat on the floor. Gently sit back towards your heels until you feel a stretch on the front of your shins.
  2. Ankle Neuroglide (video) · 2 x 10 reps, once daily
    Follow the video to gently mobilize the nerves that run along the front of the ankle, which can become irritated with this condition.
  3. Isometric Dorsiflexion · 5 reps of 5-second holds, twice daily
    Sit with your heel on the ground. Use your hand or your other foot to provide resistance on top of your foot as you try to lift it, without allowing any movement.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Heel Walk (video) · 3 x 20 steps, every other day
    As shown in the video, walk on your heels while keeping the balls of your feet off the ground. This is the most direct way to strengthen the tibialis anterior.
  2. Banded Dorsiflexion · 3 x 15 reps, every other day
    Anchor a resistance band to a sturdy object in front of you. Sit and loop the other end around the top of your foot. Pull your toes toward your shin against the band's resistance.
  3. Eccentric Calf Drops (video) · 3 x 15 reps, every other day
    Strengthening the opposing calf muscles helps balance the forces around the ankle. Follow the video, focusing on the slow lowering phase.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Heel Walk, Ankle Neuroglide.

Habits that help

  • Ensure your shoes are not laced too tightly, especially over the front of the ankle.
  • When running, focus on taking shorter, quicker steps to avoid 'over-striding' and slapping your foot down.
  • Regularly stretch your calf muscles, as tightness there can overload the shin muscles.
  • Gradually increase the intensity and duration of your activities, especially on hills.

What to expect

Mild strains often feel significantly better within 1-3 weeks. More persistent pain from chronic overuse can take 4-8 weeks of consistent rehab to resolve.

When to see a professional

Book an appointment if you experience 'foot drop' (inability to lift your foot), numbness on top of the foot, or pain that is severe and doesn't improve with rest. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Severe pain, swelling, and redness on the shin after an injury (possible fracture).
  • Numbness on the top of the foot or a 'foot drop' (a new inability to lift your foot at all).
  • Intense, tight, or burning pain in the shin that gets progressively worse during exercise and doesn't resolve with rest.
  • Signs of a blood clot in the calf: localized swelling, redness, and warmth.

Frequently asked questions

Can a tight tibialis anterior cause pain at the front of the ankle or big toe?

Often, yes. Front of the shin, front of the ankle and the big toe. That referred pain is why it gets blamed on the joint or the spot that hurts instead of the muscle.

How long does tibialis anterior pain take to settle?

Mild strains often feel significantly better within 1-3 weeks. More persistent pain from chronic overuse can take 4-8 weeks of consistent rehab to resolve.

When should I see someone about it?

Book an appointment if you experience 'foot drop' (inability to lift your foot), numbness on top of the foot, or pain that is severe and doesn't improve with rest.

What else can feel like tibialis anterior pain?

Shin splints (medial tibial stress syndrome), Tibial stress fracture, Chronic exertional compartment syndrome. An evaluation separates muscle pain from these, and anything that needs a physician is referred on.
