Where the quadratus femoris sends pain

Deep in the buttock low down near the sit bone, and into the back of the hip; hurts to sit on. People usually come in describing pain at the middle of the buttock or buttock crease.

What the quadratus femoris does

Externally rotates the hip and helps hold the head of the femur in the socket. It is the lowest of the deep hip rotator group.

  • Starts at: The lateral border of the ischial tuberosity, the sit bone.
  • Attaches to: The quadrate tubercle on the intertrochanteric crest at the back of the femur.
  • Nerve supply: Nerve to quadratus femoris, from the sacral plexus (L4 to S1).

Symptoms people describe

  • Deep buttock pain low down, near the sit bone.
  • Pain that is worse sitting on a hard surface.
  • Discomfort deep in the back of the hip.
  • Pain on turning the leg outward or crossing it over.
  • A deep ache after driving or long flights.
  • Pain that has been treated as piriformis without lasting change.

What makes it worse

Sitting · Stairs, squats or hills · Running or sports. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the quadratus femoris is involved:

  • Is your deep buttock pain low down, near your sit bone?
  • Is sitting on a hard surface particularly painful?
  • Has piriformis treatment helped only briefly?
  • Does turning your leg outward provoke it?
  • Is it worse after long drives or flights?

Low buttock palpation. Lie on your side with the hip bent. Find the sit bone, then press just outward from it toward the hip bone. Suggests this muscle if: Deep tenderness between the sit bone and the hip reproduces the pain.

Resisted external rotation. Sitting with the knee bent at 90 degrees, try to turn the foot inward, which rotates the thigh outward, against resistance. Suggests this muscle if: Deep pain low in the buttock rather than higher where piriformis sits.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Piriformis syndrome, which sits higher in the buttock.
  • Proximal hamstring tendinopathy, which is directly on the sit bone.
  • Ischial bursitis.
  • Sciatic nerve irritation.
  • Hip joint problems.

Common causes

  • Prolonged sitting, especially on hard surfaces.
  • A sudden increase in running or hill work.
  • Sports with rotation and direction change.
  • A fall onto the buttock.
  • Compensating for weak gluteal muscles.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Change the sitting surface · Ongoing
    A cushion, and standing breaks, take the direct pressure off.
  2. Break up sitting (video: Walk Break Protocol Seated) · Every 30 minutes
    This muscle is loaded by sustained sitting more than by activity.
  3. Gentle hip opening (video: Figure Four Stretch) · Thirty seconds each side
    Mild stretch only; aggressive stretching can irritate the sciatic nerve nearby.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Hip rotation range (video: 90/90 Hip Switch) · Ten each side, daily
    Restores rotation in both directions.
  2. Hip CARs (video: Standing Hip CARs) · Five each way
    Takes the hip through full controlled range.
  3. Nerve glide if needed (video: Hip Neuroglide) · Ten slow repetitions
    Only if there is any leg symptom; gliding rather than stretching.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. External rotation strength (video: Standing Hip ER Liftoff) · Two sets of twelve each side
    Loads the deep rotators directly.
  2. Glute strength (video: Hip Thrust) · Three sets of ten
    Strong glutes mean the deep rotators do not have to stabilise alone.
  3. Single leg control (video: Single Leg Balance) · Three holds of 30 seconds each side
    Builds hip stability through the chain.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Figure Four Stretch, 90/90 Hip Switch, Standing Hip ER Liftoff.

Habits that help

  • Use a cushion if you sit on hard surfaces for long periods.
  • Stand and move every half hour.
  • Build running and hill volume gradually.
  • Strengthen the glutes rather than repeatedly stretching the buttock.

What to expect

Deep hip rotator pain usually eases within three to four weeks once sitting is addressed and the glutes are strengthened. Stretching alone tends to give short-lived relief.

When to see a professional

Book an evaluation if buttock pain persists beyond three weeks. Leg weakness, foot drop, saddle numbness or loss of bladder or bowel control needs emergency care. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Progressive leg weakness or foot drop.
  • Loss of bladder or bowel control, or saddle numbness, which needs emergency care.
  • Buttock pain after a fall, especially with thinning bones.
  • Night pain not relieved by position change.
  • Fever with buttock pain.
