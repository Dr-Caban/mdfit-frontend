Where the semispinalis capitis sends pain

A band of pain wrapping from the base of the skull over the head toward the forehead and temple; often described as a tight hat band. People usually come in describing pain at the back of the head, forehead, base of the skull or back of the neck.

What the semispinalis capitis does

Extends the head and neck and controls the forward nod. It is the main muscle holding the head up when you look down at a screen.

  • Starts at: The transverse processes of C7 through T6 and the articular processes of C4 through C6.
  • Attaches to: The back of the skull, between the superior and inferior nuchal lines of the occipital bone.
  • Nerve supply: Posterior rami of the cervical spinal nerves, including the greater occipital nerve, which pierces the muscle.

Symptoms people describe

  • A band of headache running from the base of the skull forward over the head.
  • Pain felt at the forehead and temple although the source is at the back of the neck.
  • Deep aching at the back of the neck after screen work.
  • Scalp tenderness or a sense of pressure on top of the head.
  • Stiffness looking up, more than looking down.
  • Headache that builds through the working day rather than arriving suddenly.

What makes it worse

Desk, phone or screens · Stress · Turning your head or body · Lying on it at night. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the semispinalis capitis is involved:

  • Does your headache feel like a band or tight hat wrapping from the back of your head to your forehead?
  • Does it build through a day of screen work rather than starting suddenly?
  • Is the back of your neck tender to press at the base of the skull?
  • Is your scalp sore to touch or to brush?
  • Is looking up more uncomfortable than looking down?

Base of skull palpation. Press into the thick muscle either side of the midline just below the base of the skull, with the head supported and relaxed. Suggests this muscle if: Firm pressure reproduces the band of headache toward the forehead.

Sustained flexion test. Let the head drop gently forward for 30 seconds, then return it to neutral. Suggests this muscle if: A familiar ache or headache builds at the back of the neck during the hold.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Occipital neuralgia, where the greater occipital nerve itself is irritated.
  • Cervicogenic headache arising from the upper neck joints.
  • Tension-type headache.
  • Migraine.
  • Upper cervical arthritis.

Common causes

  • Screen and phone use with the head carried forward.
  • Sleeping with too many or too few pillows.
  • Stress and sustained low-level bracing.
  • Whiplash or a fall.
  • Prolonged driving or reading in bed.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Screen height correction (video: Desk Setup Tutorial) · Once, then check weekly
    The top of the screen at eye level removes the sustained load this muscle is carrying.
  2. Supported rest position · Five minutes, twice daily
    Lie on your back with a small rolled towel under the neck and let the head weight rest, rather than holding it.
  3. Gentle neck release (video: Neck Neuroglide Seated) · Ten slow repetitions
    Slow, small movements to reduce guarding before any stretching.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Chin glide (video: Chin Glide Seated) · Two sets of ten, several times daily
    Glide the chin straight back without tipping the head. This is the single most useful movement for this muscle.
  2. Eccentric neck lengthening (video: Towel Neck Stretch Eccentric) · Five slow repetitions, once daily
    Control the lowering rather than pulling into a stretch.
  3. Thoracic extension (video: Thoracic Extension Over Chair) · Ten repetitions daily
    A stiff upper back forces the neck to compensate; freeing it reduces the load here.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Deep neck flexor training (video: Deep Cervical Flexor Nods Seated) · Ten second holds, five repetitions, daily
    Builds the counterbalance at the front so the extensors stop overworking.
  2. Scapular retraction endurance (video: Floor Slide Scapular Retract) · Two sets of ten, daily
    A supported shoulder girdle reduces neck load.
  3. Postural endurance (video: Seated Desk Posture Reset) · Every 30 minutes at a desk
    Frequent short resets beat one long correction.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Chin Glide Seated, Towel Neck Stretch Eccentric, Neck Neuroglide Seated.

Habits that help

  • Set the screen so you look straight ahead, not down.
  • Take a movement break at least every half hour.
  • Use one supportive pillow that keeps the neck in line with the spine.
  • Hold a phone up rather than looking down at it.

What to expect

Headaches driven by this muscle often ease within two to three weeks once screen height and break frequency change. Without those changes, hands-on work tends to give only short-lived relief.

When to see a professional

Book an evaluation if headaches persist beyond three weeks, are becoming more frequent, or are waking you at night. Any red flag above needs a physician first. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • The worst headache of your life, or one that comes on like a thunderclap.
  • Headache with fever and a stiff neck that will not bend forward.
  • A new headache after a head injury.
  • Headache with weakness, slurred speech, or vision loss.
  • A new persistent headache over the age of 50, particularly with scalp tenderness and jaw pain on chewing.
