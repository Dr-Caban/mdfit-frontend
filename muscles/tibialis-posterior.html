Where the tibialis posterior sends pain

Inner ankle and arch, along the inner shin; common with flat feet. People usually come in describing pain at the inner ankle and arch, lower calf or sole of the foot. It is also a common hidden driver behind Achilles Tendon Pain.

What the tibialis posterior does

Points the foot down and turns it inward. It is the primary muscle that holds up the main arch of your foot.

  • Starts at: Posterior surfaces of the tibia and fibula, and the membrane between them.
  • Attaches to: Multiple bones on the underside of the foot, primarily the navicular bone.
  • Nerve supply: Tibial Nerve (L4, L5)

Symptoms people describe

  • Aching pain deep in the calf or along the inner shin bone.
  • Sharp pain on the inside of the ankle or in the arch, especially with activity.
  • A feeling that your arch is collapsing or your foot is becoming flatter.
  • Weakness or instability when trying to stand on your tiptoes.
  • Pain that gets worse after long periods of standing, walking, or running.
  • Morning stiffness in the inner ankle and arch.

What makes it worse

Running or sports · Standing or walking. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the tibialis posterior is involved:

  • Is your pain located along the inside of your ankle bone, running down into your arch?
  • Do you feel like the arch on the inside of your foot is getting lower or 'falling'?
  • Is it very difficult or painful to do a single-leg heel raise (go up on your tiptoes) on the affected side?
  • Does your pain feel deep inside your calf, rather than on the surface?
  • Does wearing shoes with good arch support seem to help?

Single Leg Heel Raise. Stand on the affected foot only and try to raise your heel off the ground, going up onto your tiptoes. You can lightly touch a wall for balance. Suggests this muscle if: A positive test is significant pain on the inside of the ankle, inability to lift the heel, or if your heel rolls outward as you try to lift it.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Plantar fasciitis
  • Tarsal tunnel syndrome (tibial nerve entrapment)
  • Shin splints (medial tibial stress syndrome)
  • Navicular stress fracture
  • Deltoid ligament sprain

Common causes

  • Over-pronation or having 'flat feet'.
  • A sudden increase in impact activities like running or jumping.
  • Wearing unsupportive footwear for long periods.
  • Chronic ankle instability from previous injuries.
  • Being overweight, which increases the load on the arch.
  • Age-related changes to the tendon.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Lacrosse Ball Arch Release · 2 minutes, twice daily
    While seated, roll a lacrosse or tennis ball under the arch of your foot with firm pressure. Focus on any tender spots to release tension in the muscles supporting the tendon.
  2. Ankle Pumps · 3 x 15 reps, several times per day
    While sitting or lying down, gently point your toes away from you, then pull them back towards your shin. This gentle movement helps reduce swelling and stiffness.
  3. 90/90 Breathing (video) · 5 minutes, once daily
    Follow the video to lie in a relaxed position. This helps calm the nervous system and reduce overall muscle guarding and pain perception.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Short Foot Exercise · 5 reps of 10-second holds, twice daily
    With your foot flat on the floor, try to shorten your foot by pulling the ball of your foot towards your heel, creating an arch. Do this without curling your toes.
  2. Ankle Neuroglide (video) · 2 x 10 reps, once daily
    Follow the video to gently mobilize the nerves around the ankle. This can help reduce irritation and improve tissue health in the area.
  3. Towel Curls · 3 x 15 reps, once daily
    Sit with your foot flat on a towel. Use your toes to scrunch the towel towards you, which helps strengthen the small muscles in your foot that support the arch.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Eccentric Calf Drops (video) · 3 x 15 reps, every other day
    Perform on a step as shown, focusing on a very slow (3-4 second) and controlled lowering of your heel. This builds strength and resilience in the tendon.
  2. Single Leg Balance (video) · 3 x 30-60 seconds, daily
    Stand on one leg, focusing on keeping your arch lifted and your ankle stable. This retrains the muscle's crucial role in stabilizing your foot.
  3. Toe Walk (video) · 3 x 20 steps, every other day
    Walk on your tiptoes as shown in the video. This directly strengthens the tibialis posterior and other calf muscles that support the arch.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Single Leg Balance, Toe Walk, Ankle Neuroglide.

Habits that help

  • Wear supportive shoes with good arch support daily. Avoid going barefoot on hard surfaces.
  • Consider an over-the-counter orthotic insert to provide consistent arch support.
  • Gradually progress your activity levels. Avoid sudden jumps in running mileage or intensity.
  • Maintain a healthy weight to reduce the daily stress on your feet and ankles.

What to expect

With consistent daily exercises and supportive footwear, mild symptoms often improve in 2-4 weeks. More significant tendinopathy can take 6-12 weeks or longer to fully resolve.

When to see a professional

Book an appointment if you are unable to perform a single-leg heel raise, if pain is severe and not improving after 2 weeks of self-care, or if you suspect a fracture. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Inability to bear any weight on the foot after an injury.
  • Numbness, tingling, or loss of sensation in the foot.
  • Sudden, severe swelling and bruising around the ankle without a clear injury.
  • Signs of a blood clot in the calf: localized swelling, redness, and warmth.

Frequently asked questions

Can a tight tibialis posterior cause pain at the inner ankle and arch?

Often, yes. Inner ankle and arch, along the inner shin; common with flat feet. That referred pain is why it gets blamed on the joint or the spot that hurts instead of the muscle.

How long does tibialis posterior pain take to settle?

With consistent daily exercises and supportive footwear, mild symptoms often improve in 2-4 weeks. More significant tendinopathy can take 6-12 weeks or longer to fully resolve.

When should I see someone about it?

Book an appointment if you are unable to perform a single-leg heel raise, if pain is severe and not improving after 2 weeks of self-care, or if you suspect a fracture.

What else can feel like tibialis posterior pain?

Plantar fasciitis, Tarsal tunnel syndrome (tibial nerve entrapment), Shin splints (medial tibial stress syndrome). An evaluation separates muscle pain from these, and anything that needs a physician is referred on.
