Where the quadratus lumborum sends pain

Low back and flank, over the SI joint and buttock, and the point of the hip. Hurts rolling in bed, standing up or coughing. People usually come in describing pain at the flank or side of the low back, si joint and back of the pelvis, upper buttock at the belt line or outside of the hip. It is also a common hidden driver behind Low Back Pain and Sciatica-Type Pain.

What the quadratus lumborum does

Hikes the hip up on one side, side-bends the trunk, and stabilizes the pelvis and low back when walking or lifting.

  • Starts at: The top, back border of the hip bone (posterior iliac crest).
  • Attaches to: The lowest rib (12th rib) and the sides of the upper four lumbar vertebrae.
  • Nerve supply: Thoracic (T12) and lumbar (L1-L4) spinal nerves.

Symptoms people describe

  • A deep, nagging ache in the low back, often worse on one side.
  • Sharp, stabbing pain when trying to stand up straight, roll over in bed, or cough.
  • Pain that refers over the top of the buttock (SI joint area) and sometimes to the front of the hip.
  • A feeling of being 'stuck' or unable to stand up straight after sitting for a while.
  • Pain that prevents you from finding a comfortable position in bed.
  • A feeling of intense vulnerability in the low back.

What makes it worse

Standing or walking · Turning your head or body · Deep breaths or coughing · Sitting. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the quadratus lumborum is involved:

  • Do you get a sharp, 'catching' pain in your low back when you try to roll over in bed or get out of a car?
  • Does coughing, sneezing, or taking a deep breath send a sharp pain into your low back or flank?
  • Is it difficult to stand up completely straight after you've been sitting for a while?
  • Does the pain feel very deep, like it's right next to your spine, but also spreads out over your buttock or hip?
  • Do you feel pain when you stand or walk, but find some relief by leaning on a shopping cart or countertop?

Hip Hike Test. Stand in front of a mirror. Try to lift one side of your pelvis straight up toward your ribs without bending your knee or leaning your torso. Suggests this muscle if: Sharp pain in the low back on the side you are hiking, or an inability to perform the movement smoothly.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Lumbar disc herniation
  • Sacroiliac (SI) joint dysfunction
  • Kidney pain or kidney stone (check for fever, nausea, or changes in urination)
  • Lumbar facet syndrome
  • Gluteus medius trigger points

Common causes

  • Sitting for long periods, especially with poor posture or in a slumped position.
  • Lifting a heavy object, especially with a twisting motion.
  • A fall or direct blow to the low back, hip, or buttock.
  • Standing or walking on uneven surfaces for extended periods.
  • A true or functional leg length discrepancy.
  • Weak gluteal and core muscles, forcing the QL to overwork.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. QL Pressure Release (Ball) · 2-3 minutes per side, daily
    Lie on your back with knees bent. Place a lacrosse ball in the soft tissue of your low back, between your ribs and hip bone, just to the side of the spine. Gently rock your hips side to side.
  2. Pillow Squeeze Reset (video: Pillow Belt Reset) · 1 x 5 reps of 5 sec holds, as needed
    Follow the video instructions to gently reset the pelvis. This can provide immediate, temporary relief by relaxing deep muscles around the hips and low back.
  3. Dynamic Child's Pose (video: Dynamic Childs Pose) · 1 x 10 rocks, twice daily
    From hands and knees, sit your hips back towards your heels. Gently rock side to side to feel a stretch in your low back and lats. Breathe deeply into your back.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. QL Side Bend Stretch (video: Sit Turn Lean) · 3 x 30 sec per side, daily
    Sit tall in a chair. Reach one arm overhead and gently lean to the opposite side, feeling a stretch in your flank and low back. Keep both sit bones on the chair.
  2. Core Brace Introduction (video: Core Brace Intro Seated) · 2 x 10 reps of 10 sec holds, daily
    Learning to gently engage your deep core muscles provides stability for the lumbar spine, allowing the overworked QL to relax.
  3. Quadruped T-Spine Rotation (video) · 2 x 10 per side, daily
    This mobilizes the mid-back, which can reduce the strain on the low back and QL. Follow the video cues for smooth, controlled rotation.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Side Plank (video: Dynamic Plank) · 3 x 30-60 sec hold per side, every other day
    Lie on your side, propped on your elbow. Lift your hips off the floor, creating a straight line from head to feet. Start from your knees if a full plank is too difficult.
  2. One-Sided Farmer Carry (video: Farmer Carry) · 3 x 20-30 steps per side, every other day
    Hold a single heavy weight in one hand. Walk with tall posture, fighting the urge to lean away from the weight. This directly challenges the opposite QL to stabilize the pelvis.
  3. Single Leg Glute Bridge (video) · 3 x 10-12 per side, every other day
    Lie on your back with knees bent. Squeeze your glutes to lift your hips. Progressing to the single-leg version improves pelvic stability, reducing strain on the QL.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Sit Turn Lean, Dynamic Childs Pose, Pillow Belt Reset.

Habits that help

  • Avoid sitting for more than 30 minutes at a time. Use the 'Walk Break Protocol Seated'.
  • When standing up from a chair, scoot to the edge, place feet wide, and hinge at your hips, keeping your back straight.
  • Sleep on your side with a pillow between your knees to keep your pelvis aligned.
  • When lifting, face the object, brace your core, and lift with your legs, not your back.

What to expect

Acute QL pain can often be calmed within a few days. Consistent mobility and strengthening can lead to significant improvement in 2-4 weeks, with a focus on preventing recurrence over the next 2-3 months.

When to see a professional

If you have any red flag symptoms, or if the pain is severe and not improving with 1-2 weeks of care, book an appointment. Also see us if you suspect a leg length discrepancy. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Pain accompanied by fever, chills, or unexplained weight loss.
  • Loss of bowel or bladder control (this is a medical emergency).
  • Numbness or weakness in the legs that is severe or getting progressively worse.
  • Back pain that is constant, severe, and not relieved by any change in position.
