Where the extensor digitorum longus sends pain

The top of the foot across the smaller toes, and the front of the ankle and outer shin. People usually come in describing pain at the front of the ankle or big toe.

What the extensor digitorum longus does

Extends the lesser toes and assists dorsiflexion and eversion of the foot, lifting the toes clear during walking.

  • Starts at: The lateral condyle of the tibia, the upper three quarters of the anterior surface of the fibula and the interosseous membrane.
  • Attaches to: The middle and distal phalanges of the second through fifth toes, by four tendons that fan across the top of the foot.
  • Nerve supply: Deep fibular nerve, a branch of the common fibular nerve.

Symptoms people describe

  • Pain across the top of the foot over the smaller toes.
  • Aching at the front of the ankle.
  • Pain along the outer front of the shin.
  • Discomfort under tight shoe laces.
  • Toes that claw, as the extensors overwork against tight flexors.
  • Pain worse with running, particularly downhill.

What makes it worse

Running or sports · Stairs, squats or hills · Standing or walking. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the extensor digitorum longus is involved:

  • Is the pain across the top of your foot over your smaller toes?
  • Do tight laces or shoe pressure make it worse?
  • Is there aching along the outer front of your shin?
  • Do your toes tend to claw upward?
  • Is downhill running or walking particularly painful?

Resisted toe extension. Lift the four smaller toes upward against resistance from a finger. Suggests this muscle if: Pain across the top of the foot or along the outer shin.

Tendon fan palpation. Lift the toes to make the four tendons stand out across the top of the foot, then press along them toward the ankle. Suggests this muscle if: Tenderness along the tendons reproduces the pain.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Extensor tendinopathy.
  • Metatarsal stress fracture.
  • Anterior tarsal tunnel syndrome.
  • Shin splints involving the front of the shin.
  • Peroneal nerve problems causing foot drop.

Common causes

  • Tight lacing or shoe pressure across the top of the foot.
  • A sudden increase in running, especially downhill.
  • Walking on uneven ground.
  • Tight calf muscles, which force the front of the shin to work harder.
  • Direct trauma to the top of the foot.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Lacing and footwear change · Immediately
    Skip eyelets over the sore area and check the shoe is not too tight across the top.
  2. Reduce downhill loading · Two weeks
    Downhill running and walking load the front of the shin heavily.
  3. Gentle ankle movement (video: Ankle Circles) · Ten each way
    Maintains motion without load.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Tendon gliding (video: Ankle Neuroglide) · Ten slow repetitions
    Keeps the tendons moving freely across the top of the foot.
  2. Calf lengthening (video: Eccentric Calf Drops) · Ten slow repetitions
    Reduces the work the front of the shin has to do to clear the foot.
  3. Toe control · Ten repetitions, twice daily
    Practise lifting and lowering the toes evenly rather than clawing.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Front-of-shin loading (video: Heel Walk) · Three sets of 20 metres
    Loads the muscle directly in its working role.
  2. Balance and foot control (video: Single Leg Balance) · Three holds of 30 seconds each side
    Builds the control that spreads the load.
  3. Graded return to running · Build over weeks
    Flat surfaces first, adding downhill only once pain free.

Habits that help

  • Check lacing and shoe fit across the top of the foot.
  • Build downhill running and hiking volume gradually.
  • Keep the calves flexible to reduce front-of-shin workload.
  • Have any foot drop or weakness assessed rather than trained through.

What to expect

Pressure-related irritation often settles in one to two weeks once lacing is changed. Training-load irritation takes four to six weeks with graded return.

When to see a professional

Weakness lifting the foot or toes needs prompt assessment. Book an evaluation for top-of-foot pain persisting beyond three weeks, or focal bony tenderness with night pain. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Weakness lifting the foot or toes, which may indicate nerve compression.
  • Foot drop, which needs prompt assessment.
  • Top-of-foot pain after a fall, which may be a fracture.
  • Focal bony tenderness with night pain, which may indicate a stress fracture.
  • Swelling with redness, warmth and fever.

Frequently asked questions

Can a tight extensor digitorum longus cause pain at the front of the ankle or big toe?

Often, yes. The top of the foot across the smaller toes, and the front of the ankle and outer shin. That referred pain is why it gets blamed on the joint or the spot that hurts instead of the muscle.

How long does extensor digitorum longus pain take to settle?

Pressure-related irritation often settles in one to two weeks once lacing is changed. Training-load irritation takes four to six weeks with graded return.

When should I see someone about it?

Weakness lifting the foot or toes needs prompt assessment. Book an evaluation for top-of-foot pain persisting beyond three weeks, or focal bony tenderness with night pain.

What else can feel like extensor digitorum longus pain?

Extensor tendinopathy., Metatarsal stress fracture., Anterior tarsal tunnel syndrome.. An evaluation separates muscle pain from these, and anything that needs a physician is referred on.
