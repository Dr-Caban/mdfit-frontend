Where the pronator teres sends pain

Deep in the front of the forearm toward the wrist and the base of the thumb; can add tingling in the thumb, index and middle fingers. People usually come in describing pain at the front of the elbow or forearm.

What the pronator teres does

Pronates the forearm, turning the palm downward, and assists elbow flexion.

  • Starts at: Two heads: the larger from the medial epicondyle of the humerus and the common flexor origin, the smaller from the coronoid process of the ulna. The median nerve passes between them.
  • Attaches to: The middle of the lateral surface of the radius.
  • Nerve supply: Median nerve.

Symptoms people describe

  • Deep ache in the front of the forearm, below the inner elbow.
  • Pain running toward the wrist and the base of the thumb.
  • Tingling or numbness in the thumb, index and middle fingers.
  • Symptoms worse with repeated turning of the palm, such as using a screwdriver.
  • Forearm pain worse during the day with activity, rather than waking you at night.
  • Weakness of grip with sustained use.

What makes it worse

Gripping, lifting or typing · Desk, phone or screens · Bending or lifting. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the pronator teres is involved:

  • Do you have a deep ache in the front of your forearm below the inner elbow?
  • Do you get tingling in your thumb, index and middle fingers that is worse with activity rather than at night?
  • Does repeatedly turning your palm down, such as using a screwdriver, provoke it?
  • Is the muscle in your upper forearm tender to press?
  • Has a wrist splint for carpal tunnel not helped?

Resisted pronation. With the elbow bent to 90 degrees and tucked to your side, try to turn your palm downward against resistance. Suggests this muscle if: Pain in the upper forearm, or tingling into the fingers.

Pronator palpation. Press into the muscle belly in the upper third of the forearm, just below and outside the inner elbow bone. Suggests this muscle if: Tenderness reproduces the forearm ache or the finger tingling.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Carpal tunnel syndrome, which usually wakes people at night.
  • Golfer's elbow at the inner elbow.
  • Cervical radiculopathy from the neck.
  • Thoracic outlet symptoms.
  • Elbow joint problems.

Common causes

  • Repetitive turning of the forearm, as with tools, racquets or manual work.
  • Heavy gripping combined with pronation.
  • Mouse and keyboard use with the forearm pronated for hours.
  • A sudden increase in lifting volume.
  • Direct trauma to the upper forearm.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Reduce sustained pronation · Ongoing
    Use a vertical mouse, rotate tool use, and break up long periods with the palm turned down.
  2. Forearm flexor lengthening (video: Forearm Flexor Stretch) · Thirty seconds, three times daily
    Elbow straight, palm up, gently extend the wrist.
  3. Nerve glide (video: Full Neuroglide Flow Seated) · Ten slow repetitions
    If the nerve is involved, gentle gliding is better tolerated than stretching.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Controlled pronation and supination (video: Eccentric Wrist Pronator) · Ten slow repetitions, twice daily
    Control the lowering phase, which is where the muscle builds tolerance.
  2. Wrist circles under light load (video: Weighted Wrist Circles) · Ten each way
    Restores smooth forearm rotation.
  3. Neck and shoulder check (video: Chin Glide Seated) · Two sets of ten
    Nerve symptoms in the hand often have a contribution from the neck.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Progressive rotation load (video: Eccentric Wrist Pronator) · Three sets of ten, twice weekly
    Add light weight once symptom free through range.
  2. Grip strength (video: Farmer Carry) · Three carries of 30 seconds
    Builds forearm endurance in a neutral position.
  3. Neutral-grip pulling (video: Dumbbell Row) · Three sets of ten
    Strengthens the arm without forcing pronation.

Habits that help

  • Switch to a vertical or neutral mouse if you work at a screen.
  • Take breaks from repeated screwdriver or racquet use.
  • Keep the forearm neutral when carrying.
  • Treat the neck alongside the forearm when fingers are involved.

What to expect

Forearm symptoms usually ease in two to four weeks once the repeated pronation is reduced. Tingling can take longer to settle than the ache.

When to see a professional

Book an evaluation if symptoms persist beyond three weeks. Progressive weakness, muscle wasting or constant numbness needs a physician promptly. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Progressive weakness of the thumb or hand muscles, or visible wasting.
  • Constant numbness that does not come and go.
  • Symptoms after a significant fall or elbow injury.
  • Forearm swelling with severe pain, which needs urgent assessment.
  • Symptoms in both hands with neck pain or clumsiness.
