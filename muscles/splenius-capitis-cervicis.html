Where the splenius capitis & cervicis sends pain

Pain on the top of the head, and deep ache inside the head behind the eye. People usually come in describing pain at the top of the head, back of the neck, behind or around the eye, base of the skull or back of the head.

What the splenius capitis & cervicis does

Working together, they tilt your head backward (extension). Working alone, one side turns and tilts your head to that same side.

  • Starts at: The spine at the base of the neck and upper back (C7-T6).
  • Attaches to: The base of the skull (capitis) and the sides of the upper neck vertebrae (cervicis).
  • Nerve supply: Dorsal rami of the middle and lower cervical nerves.

Symptoms people describe

  • A headache that feels like it's on the very top of your head.
  • A strange, deep ache that feels like it's inside your skull, often behind one eye.
  • Neck stiffness, especially when trying to look down or turn your head.
  • Pain can sometimes feel like a 'cap' on your head.
  • Occasional blurry vision in the eye on the same side as the neck pain.

What makes it worse

Desk, phone or screens · Turning your head or body. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the splenius capitis & cervicis is involved:

  • Does your headache feel like it's on the very top of your skull?
  • Do you have a persistent, deep ache that feels like it's behind your eye?
  • Is your neck stiff and painful when you try to tuck your chin to your chest?
  • Does looking up for a long time (like painting a ceiling) trigger your neck pain or headache?
  • Do you spend many hours a day with your head tilted forward looking at a screen?

Splenius Contraction Test. Sit tall. Turn your head about 45 degrees to one side, then gently tilt your head backward to look up at the ceiling. Suggests this muscle if: This movement reproduces the specific headache pain on the top of your head or behind your eye.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Cervicogenic headache (headache originating from the neck joints).
  • Migraine or tension-type headache.
  • Occipital neuralgia.
  • Sinus headache (pain behind the eye).

Common causes

  • Forward head posture from computer or phone use.
  • Whiplash injury, which overstretches these muscles.
  • Sleeping with too many pillows, forcing the neck into flexion.
  • Activities that require looking up for long periods (e.g., rock climbing, painting).
  • Emotional stress, leading to chronic neck tension.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Gentle Chin Tucks (video: Chin Glide Seated) · 2 x 10 reps, several times daily
    Gently glide your chin straight back as if making a 'double chin'. Avoid tilting your head down; the motion is a straight glide.
  2. Splenius Pressure Release · 60-90 seconds per side, daily
    Place a lacrosse ball on the muscle at the back/side of your neck, just below the skull. Lean against a wall and apply gentle, sustained pressure to tender spots.
  3. Gentle Neck Flexion Stretch · 2 x 30 sec, daily
    Sit tall and gently bring your chin toward your chest. Use your hand to apply very light overpressure on the back of your head. You should feel a stretch, not pain.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Eccentric Neck Extension (video: Towel Neck Stretch Eccentric) · 2 x 8 reps, every other day
    Use a towel to gently pull your head forward into flexion, then use your neck muscles to slowly resist the towel as you return to the start position over 3-5 seconds.
  2. Neck & Arm Nerve Glide (video: Neck Neuroglide Seated) · 1 x 10 reps per side, daily
    This helps ensure the nerves in the area are moving freely. Follow the video cues and move gently without forcing into pain.
  3. Thoracic Spine Mobility (video: Quadruped T-Spine Rotation) · 2 x 10 reps per side, daily
    Improving mobility in your upper back reduces the strain on your neck. Focus on rotating through your rib cage.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Deep Neck Flexor Endurance (video: Deep Cervical Flexor Nods Seated) · 3 x 30 sec holds, daily
    Perform a gentle chin tuck and hold it, maintaining the contraction in the deep front-neck muscles. Breathe normally.
  2. Upper Back Strengthening (video: Dumbbell Row) · 3 x 10-12 reps, twice weekly
    Hinge at your hips with a flat back. Pull the weights up towards your chest, squeezing your shoulder blades together at the top.
  3. Scapular Retraction (video: Face Pull) · 3 x 15 reps, twice weekly
    Using a band or cable, pull the rope towards your face, aiming your hands towards your ears. Squeeze your shoulder blades together and back.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Chin Glide Seated, Neck Neuroglide Seated, Towel Neck Stretch Eccentric.

Habits that help

  • Use a single, supportive pillow that keeps your neck in a neutral line with your spine.
  • Position your computer monitor so your eyes are level with the top third of the screen.
  • When reading on a phone, try to bring the phone up to your face rather than dropping your head down to the phone.
  • Take frequent micro-breaks from desk work to perform chin glides and look around.

What to expect

Headaches often decrease in frequency and intensity within the first 1-2 weeks. Neck stiffness may take 3-6 weeks to fully resolve with consistent exercises.

When to see a professional

See a professional if your headache is severe, doesn't respond to self-care, or is accompanied by any of the red flag symptoms. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • A 'thunderclap' headache that comes on suddenly and is the 'worst headache of your life' (call 911).
  • Headache accompanied by fever, stiff neck, confusion, or rash.
  • Headache that starts after a head injury.
  • New or worsening headache pattern, especially if over age 50.
