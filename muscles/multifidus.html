Where the multifidus sends pain

Deep, pinpoint pain right next to the spine, the sacrum and sometimes the tailbone. People usually come in describing pain at the right beside the spine (low back), tailbone or si joint and back of the pelvis.

What the multifidus does

These small, deep muscles provide fine-tuned stability to each individual joint of the spine, preventing small, painful movements.

  • Starts at: Back of the sacrum and the transverse processes of vertebrae from the low back to the neck.
  • Attaches to: The spinous processes (the bony bumps in the middle of your back) of the vertebrae 2-4 levels above the origin.
  • Nerve supply: Dorsal rami of the spinal nerves at each level.

Symptoms people describe

  • A deep, sharp, or burning pain felt right next to the spine.
  • Pain is often pinpoint and you can put a finger directly on it.
  • Can refer pain down to the sacrum, tailbone, or occasionally into the abdomen.
  • A feeling of instability or 'giving way' in the low back.
  • Pain is often worse when bending forward, twisting, or returning to upright from a bent position.
  • Often persists as a chronic low-grade ache after an acute back injury has healed.

What makes it worse

Sitting · Standing or walking · Bending or lifting. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the multifidus is involved:

  • Can you point with one finger to a specific, deep spot of pain right next to your spine?
  • Do you feel a deep ache near your tailbone or sacrum that doesn't seem to be in the buttock muscles?
  • Does your back feel weak or like it might 'give out' with certain movements?
  • Is the pain worse when you bend forward to put on your socks and shoes?
  • Is the pain worse when sitting on a soft couch compared to a firm chair?

Prone Leg Lift Test. Lie face down. While keeping your pelvis flat on the floor, try to lift one leg straight up just an inch or two. Have a partner place their fingers on the muscles right next to your low back spine. Suggests this muscle if: You feel a sharp, localized pain, or your partner feels the muscle on the lifting side contract late or not at all compared to the opposite side.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Lumbar facet joint syndrome (very closely related and often co-existing)
  • Sacroiliac (SI) joint dysfunction
  • Coccydynia (tailbone pain)
  • Lumbar disc herniation

Common causes

  • A sudden twist or awkward lift that injures the deep spinal structures.
  • Prolonged sitting, which allows the multifidus muscles to weaken and atrophy.
  • Chronic poor posture, especially slouching.
  • Following a lumbar disc injury, as pain can inhibit these muscles from firing correctly.
  • Repetitive flexion activities like sit-ups or gardening.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Positional Release · 5-10 minutes, as needed
    Lie on your back with several pillows under your knees to put your low back in a comfortable, neutral position. The goal is to find a position of maximum ease to let the deep muscles relax.
  2. Pillow Squeeze Pelvic Reset (video: Pillow Belt Reset) · 1 x 5 reps of 5 sec holds, 1-2 times daily
    This gentle isometric exercise can help reset the relationship between the pelvis and sacrum, calming the deep muscles that attach there, including the multifidus.
  3. Cat-Cow Gentle Mobilization (video: Cat Cow Desk Variation) · 2 x 10 cycles, daily
    Perform this movement very slowly and within a small, pain-free range. The goal is gentle movement to reduce stiffness, not a deep stretch.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Dead Bug (video) · 3 x 10 reps (5 per side), daily
    This is a key exercise for multifidus retraining. Focus on keeping your low back absolutely still as you move your limbs. Pressing your back gently into the floor helps activate the deep stabilizers.
  2. Quadruped Glute Kick (video) · 3 x 15 per side, every other day
    From hands and knees, extend one leg back by squeezing your glute. The challenge is to keep your spine perfectly still, which requires the multifidus to work hard as a stabilizer.
  3. Core Brace Introduction (video: Core Brace Intro Seated) · 2 x 10 reps of 10 sec holds, daily
    Practice co-contracting your deep abdominal and back muscles. Imagine gently tightening a corset around your midsection. This is the foundation of spinal stability.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Bird-Dog · 3 x 10 reps per side, every other day
    From hands and knees, extend the opposite arm and leg while keeping your torso completely level. This directly challenges the multifidus to prevent rotation.
  2. Single Leg Glute Bridge (video) · 3 x 10-12 per side, every other day
    Lifting your hips requires the multifidus to stabilize the spine. The single-leg version increases the stability demand significantly.
  3. Rack Pull (video) · 3 x 8-10, every other day
    This is a limited range-of-motion deadlift. Set the bar on blocks so you only move it from just below the knees to standing. This strengthens the back in a safe, upright posture.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Dead Bug, Pillow Belt Reset, Cat Cow Desk Variation.

Habits that help

  • Always sit with your feet flat on the floor and a small support in the curve of your low back.
  • When you bend, hinge at your hips, not your waist. Practice with the 'Dowel Hip Hinge'.
  • Engage your core brace ('Core Brace Intro Seated') before lifting anything, even if it's light.
  • Avoid sitting on couches or chairs that are too soft or deep, as this encourages slouching.

What to expect

Because these are deep stabilizing muscles, retraining them takes time and focus. You may feel less 'instability' in 2-4 weeks, but building true endurance and automatic activation can take 3-6 months of consistent practice.

When to see a professional

Book an evaluation if you have any red flag symptoms, if the pain is the result of a specific injury, or if you are not making steady progress after 4 weeks. This type of pain often benefits from hands-on assessment. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Saddle anesthesia (numbness in the groin/inner thigh area).
  • Loss of bowel or bladder control.
  • Unexplained, progressive weakness in the legs.
  • Pain that is severe, constant, and worse at night.

Frequently asked questions

Can a tight multifidus cause pain at the right beside the spine (low back)?

Often, yes. Deep, pinpoint pain right next to the spine, the sacrum and sometimes the tailbone. That referred pain is why it gets blamed on the joint or the spot that hurts instead of the muscle.

How long does multifidus pain take to settle?

Because these are deep stabilizing muscles, retraining them takes time and focus. You may feel less 'instability' in 2-4 weeks, but building true endurance and automatic activation can take 3-6 months of consistent practice.

What else can feel like multifidus pain?
