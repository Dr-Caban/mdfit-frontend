Where the medial pterygoid sends pain

Back of the mouth and throat, under the jaw, in front of the ear and deep inside the ear. Can make swallowing uncomfortable. People usually come in describing pain at the ear area. It is also a common hidden driver behind Jaw Pain and TMJ Dysfunction.

What the medial pterygoid does

Closes the jaw and helps grind food side to side. Working with the masseter it forms a sling around the angle of the jaw.

  • Starts at: The inner surface of the lateral pterygoid plate of the sphenoid bone, with a smaller slip from the tuberosity of the maxilla (upper jaw).
  • Attaches to: The inner (medial) surface of the angle and ramus of the mandible, mirroring the masseter on the outside.
  • Nerve supply: Trigeminal nerve (cranial nerve V, mandibular division).

Symptoms people describe

  • Deep, hard-to-locate ache behind and below the angle of the jaw.
  • Soreness at the back of the mouth and the throat, sometimes described as a sore throat with no infection.
  • Discomfort or a blocked feeling deep inside the ear, without hearing loss.
  • Pain on swallowing, or a sense that swallowing takes effort.
  • Difficulty opening the mouth fully, often more limiting than masseter tightness.
  • Pain that is hard to reach from the outside, because most of the muscle sits inside the jawbone.

What makes it worse

Clenching or chewing · Stress. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the medial pterygoid is involved:

  • Do you have a deep ache behind the angle of your jaw that you cannot press on from the outside?
  • Does your throat feel sore or tight when nothing else suggests an infection?
  • Is swallowing uncomfortable, or does it feel like it takes effort?
  • Is opening your mouth wide limited, more than it is painful?
  • Do you clench or grind your teeth, especially at night?

Jaw opening measure. Stack your index, middle and ring fingers vertically and try to place them between your front teeth. Most people can fit three finger widths comfortably. Suggests this muscle if: You can fit fewer than three fingers, and the restriction feels deep and inside rather than at the surface.

Angle of jaw palpation. Press just inside the lower edge of the jawbone at its back angle, aiming your fingertip gently upward and inward, with the jaw relaxed. Suggests this muscle if: Deep tenderness here reproduces your throat, ear or jaw ache.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Temporomandibular joint dysfunction.
  • Throat infection or tonsillitis.
  • Middle ear problems.
  • Dental infection in the lower molars.
  • Salivary gland problems.

Common causes

  • Clenching or grinding, particularly at night.
  • Chewing predominantly on one side.
  • Prolonged dental work holding the mouth open.
  • Forward head posture, which changes the resting position of the jaw.
  • Habitual gum chewing or chewing tough foods.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Jaw rest position · Throughout the day
    Lips together, teeth apart, tongue resting on the roof of the mouth. This is the position in which the jaw closers, including this one, are genuinely off duty.
  2. Calming breath work (video: Box Breathing) · 5 minutes, once or twice daily
    Clenching is largely a stress habit. Inhale four seconds, hold four, exhale four, hold four.
  3. Warmth before gentle movement · 10 minutes before mobility work
    A warm compress over the angle of the jaw makes the deeper muscle more willing to let go.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Controlled jaw opening · 10 slow repetitions, several times daily
    Rest the tongue on the roof of the mouth and open only as far as the tongue stays in contact. This trains a hinge opening rather than a forced one.
  2. Side-to-side jaw glide · 10 repetitions each way, daily
    Watching in a mirror, glide the lower jaw sideways without pain, keeping the movement small and even on both sides.
  3. Head position reset (video: Chin Glide Seated) · Two sets of ten, several times daily
    Glide the chin back to stack the head over the shoulders. A head carried forward keeps the jaw muscles working at rest.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Deep neck flexor work (video: Deep Cervical Flexor Nods Seated) · Ten second holds, five repetitions, daily
    Strengthening the front of the neck reduces how much the jaw muscles are recruited to hold the head.
  2. Postural endurance at the desk (video: Seated Desk Posture Reset) · Every 30 minutes at a desk
    Breaks the forward head position that keeps jaw tension going through the working day.
  3. Trunk stability (video: Dead Bug) · Three sets of ten each side, every other day
    A stable trunk gives the head and neck a base, so less tension is borrowed from the jaw.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Box Breathing, Chin Glide Seated, Seated Desk Posture Reset.

Habits that help

  • Keep lips together and teeth apart whenever you are not eating.
  • Chew on both sides, and avoid gum while the jaw is sore.
  • Ask for breaks during long dental appointments.
  • Speak to your dentist about a night guard if you grind.

What to expect

Deep jaw muscles usually settle more slowly than the masseter because they are harder to reach. Many people notice easier opening within two to three weeks of consistent habit change, with the throat and ear symptoms easing last.

When to see a professional

Book an evaluation if the jaw locks, if opening stays limited beyond three weeks, or if ear and throat symptoms persist after a physician or dentist has ruled out infection. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • A jaw that locks and will not open or close.
  • Sudden swelling in the throat, floor of the mouth or neck.
  • Difficulty breathing or swallowing liquids.
  • Fever with jaw or throat pain, which suggests infection.
  • A lump in the neck or under the jaw that does not settle.
