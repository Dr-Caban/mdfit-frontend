Where the thenar muscles sends pain

The thumb pad and the palm side of the thumb, and sometimes into the wrist; weakness here affects every grip. People usually come in describing pain at the palm side of the wrist and hand.

What the thenar muscles does

A group of three short muscles that abduct, flex and oppose the thumb, bringing it across to meet the fingers. Opposition is what makes a human grip possible.

  • Starts at: The flexor retinaculum and the scaphoid and trapezium bones of the wrist.
  • Attaches to: The base of the proximal phalanx of the thumb and the shaft of the first metacarpal.
  • Nerve supply: Median nerve, recurrent branch, for most of the group; part of flexor pollicis brevis is supplied by the ulnar nerve.

Symptoms people describe

  • Aching in the fleshy pad at the base of the thumb.
  • Pain on the palm side of the thumb.
  • Difficulty bringing the thumb across to touch the little finger.
  • Weakness of grip, particularly for opening jars or turning keys.
  • Cramping in the thumb pad with sustained use.
  • Flattening of the thumb pad over time, which suggests nerve involvement.

What makes it worse

Gripping, lifting or typing · Desk, phone or screens. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the thenar muscles is involved:

  • Does the fleshy pad at the base of your thumb ache?
  • Is it hard to bring your thumb across to touch your little finger?
  • Has your grip become weaker for jars, keys or bottle tops?
  • Does the thumb pad look flatter than on the other hand?
  • Do you get numbness in the thumb, index and middle fingers at night?

Thumb pad comparison. Hold both hands palm up side by side and compare the bulk of the muscle pad at the base of each thumb. Suggests this muscle if: A visibly flatter pad on the symptomatic side suggests median nerve involvement and needs assessment rather than self-treatment.

Opposition test. Touch the tip of your thumb to the tip of your little finger, making a round O shape. Suggests this muscle if: You cannot complete the circle, or it is noticeably weaker or more painful than the other side.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Carpal tunnel syndrome.
  • Thumb base arthritis.
  • Cervical radiculopathy affecting the C6 nerve root.
  • De Quervain's tenosynovitis.
  • Trigger thumb.

Common causes

  • Carpal tunnel syndrome, which affects the nerve supplying this group.
  • Repetitive gripping and pinching.
  • Thumb base arthritis, which changes how the thumb is used.
  • Prolonged phone and device use.
  • Manual work with vibrating tools.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Reduce sustained gripping · Ongoing
    Thicker handles, lighter tools and frequent breaks reduce the load on these small muscles.
  2. Night wrist position · Overnight
    If numbness wakes you, keeping the wrist straight at night often helps; a neutral splint can be used short term.
  3. Gentle nerve gliding (video: Full Neuroglide Flow Seated) · Ten slow repetitions
    Where nerve symptoms are present, gliding is better tolerated than stretching.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Thumb opposition practice · Ten repetitions, twice daily
    Touch thumb to each fingertip in turn, slowly and accurately rather than quickly.
  2. Forearm flexor lengthening (video: Forearm Flexor Stretch) · Thirty seconds, three times daily
    Reduces the pull through the carpal tunnel.
  3. Wrist mobility (video: Weighted Wrist Circles) · Ten each way
    Keeps the wrist moving without loading the thumb.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Graded pinch and grip · Ten repetitions, twice daily
    Soft putty or ball, building gradually and stopping short of cramping.
  2. Grip endurance (video: Farmer Carry) · Three carries of 30 seconds
    Whole-hand loading rather than isolated thumb work.
  3. Upper limb strength (video: Dumbbell Row) · Three sets of ten
    Reduces the demand placed on the hand.

Habits that help

  • Use thicker handles on tools and pens.
  • Keep the wrist straight when sleeping if numbness wakes you.
  • Break up long periods of device use.
  • Have any visible thinning of the thumb pad assessed promptly.

What to expect

Muscular thumb pad pain usually eases within two to three weeks. If weakness or visible wasting is present, the nerve needs assessing and recovery depends on addressing that.

When to see a professional

Any flattening of the thumb pad, progressive weakness, or constant numbness needs prompt assessment. Book an evaluation for thumb pad pain persisting beyond three weeks. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Visible flattening or wasting of the thumb pad, which needs prompt assessment for nerve compression.
  • Progressive weakness of grip or opposition.
  • Constant rather than intermittent numbness.
  • Dropping objects without noticing.
  • Symptoms in both hands with neck pain or clumsiness.
