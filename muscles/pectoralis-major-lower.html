Where the pectoralis major lower head sends pain

Front of the chest, down the inner arm to the inner elbow and the ring and little fingers. Chest pain must be checked by a doctor first. People usually come in describing pain at the front of the chest or ring and little finger. It is also a common hidden driver behind Golfer's Elbow (Medial Epicondylitis).

What the pectoralis major lower head does

Pulls the arm down and across the chest and turns it inward; the main chest in pressing, hugging and pushing.

  • Starts at: Front of the breastbone and the cartilages of the upper six ribs.
  • Attaches to: Front of the upper arm bone (lateral lip of the bicipital groove).
  • Nerve supply: Medial and lateral pectoral nerves (C6–T1).

Symptoms people describe

  • Ache across the front of the chest that can feel alarming.
  • Pain running down the inner arm to the inner elbow and sometimes the ring and little fingers.
  • Chest tightness that changes when you move the arm or press on the muscle.
  • Pain with bench pressing, push-ups or hugging motions.
  • Rounded shoulders and shallow breathing.

What makes it worse

Desk, phone or screens · Deep breaths or coughing · Gripping, lifting or typing. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the pectoralis major lower head is involved:

  • Does pressing on the chest muscle reproduce the chest pain?
  • Does the pain change when you move your arm or press, rather than with walking or effort?
  • Does pain travel down the inner arm toward the ring and little fingers?
  • Did it start after bench pressing, push-ups or heavy carrying in front of you?
  • Do you sit with rounded shoulders for long periods?

Low doorway stretch. Place the forearm on a door frame with the elbow below shoulder height and step gently through. Suggests this muscle if: Pulling or your familiar ache across the chest and inner arm.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Heart pain (angina): must be ruled out first
  • Costochondritis
  • Thoracic outlet syndrome
  • Cervical radiculopathy (C7–C8)
  • Ulnar nerve irritation

Common causes

  • Heavy or high-volume pressing.
  • Rounded posture and long desk hours.
  • Shallow chest breathing and stress.
  • Carrying children, groceries or boxes in front of the chest.
  • Sleeping curled with the arms crossed.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Chest ball release · 60–90 sec, twice daily
    Place a ball on the chest muscle below the collarbone against a wall or doorway; hold gentle pressure and breathe slowly.
  2. 90/90 Breathing (video) · 5 min, daily
    Slow belly and rib breathing to switch off over-active chest muscles.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Baseball Cable Stretch (video) · 2 x 30 sec per side, daily
    Arm at or just below shoulder height to target the sternal fibers.
  2. Lying Open Book (video) · 2 x 8 per side, daily
    Open the chest by rotating the upper back.
  3. Thoracic Extension Over Chair (video) · 2 x 8, daily
    Extend the upper back so the chest is not held shortened.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Floor Slide Scapular Retract (video) · 3 x 10, daily
    Build the shoulder blade muscles that hold the chest open.
  2. Eccentric Push Up (video) · 3 x 6, twice weekly
    Slow lowering to rebuild pressing tolerance.
  3. Dumbbell Row (video) · 3 x 10, twice weekly
    Balance chest work with rowing.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Baseball Cable Stretch, Lying Open Book, Thoracic Extension Over Chair.

Habits that help

  • Pause every 30 minutes at the desk to roll the shoulders back and breathe.
  • Keep pressing and pulling in balance.
  • Carry loads close to the body and switch arms.

What to expect

Muscle-driven chest pain usually eases within 1 to 2 weeks once the muscle is released and posture improves.

When to see a professional

Any chest pain should be checked by a doctor first. Book with Dr. Jared once heart causes are ruled out, or if arm and hand symptoms persist. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Chest pain with exertion, shortness of breath, sweating, nausea, or pain into the jaw or left arm: call 911.
  • Chest pain that does not change with arm movement or pressure.
  • Sudden pop and bruising in the chest or armpit after a heavy press.
