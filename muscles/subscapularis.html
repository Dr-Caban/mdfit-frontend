Where the subscapularis sends pain

Back of the shoulder, down the back of the arm, sometimes a band around the wrist. Can feel like a frozen shoulder. People usually come in describing pain at the deep inside the shoulder joint, back of the shoulder, back of the hand or wrist, ring and little finger or back of the upper arm.

What the subscapularis does

The primary internal rotator of the arm (rotates it inward). It's a powerful stabilizer that holds the head of the humerus in the socket.

  • Starts at: Subscapular fossa (the entire front surface of your shoulder blade that faces your ribs).
  • Attaches to: Lesser tubercle of the humerus (a small bump on the front of your upper arm bone).
  • Nerve supply: Upper and Lower Subscapular nerves (C5, C6).

Symptoms people describe

  • Aching pain felt at the back of the shoulder and down the back of the upper arm.
  • A strange, band-like sensation or pain around the wrist, like a tight watchband.
  • Profound stiffness and inability to lift the arm, mimicking a 'frozen shoulder'.
  • Pain when reaching across the body or behind the back.
  • A 'catching' sensation in the shoulder with certain movements.

What makes it worse

Reaching overhead · Reaching behind your back. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the subscapularis is involved:

  • Do you feel a strange band of pain or tightness around your wrist, even though the main problem is your shoulder?
  • Does your shoulder feel incredibly stiff, as if it's 'frozen', making it hard to lift your arm to the side?
  • Is the pain primarily at the back of your shoulder and down the back of your arm?
  • Is it painful to reach across your chest to touch your opposite shoulder?
  • Do you have difficulty reaching into your back pocket?

Belly Press Test. Place the palm of your hand on your stomach. Try to press into your belly while keeping your wrist straight. Compare the strength to your other side. Suggests this muscle if: Pain in the shoulder, or you have to bend your wrist or let your elbow drift backward to generate pressure, indicating weakness.

Lift-Off Test. Place the back of your hand on your lower back. Try to lift your hand away from your back. If you can't, this is a positive test. Suggests this muscle if: Inability to lift the hand off the back, or severe pain when attempting to do so.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Adhesive capsulitis (Frozen Shoulder)
  • Cervical radiculopathy (C7/C8 nerve root)
  • Long head of biceps tendon pathology
  • AC joint sprain
  • Carpal tunnel syndrome (due to the wrist referral)

Common causes

  • Repetitive forceful internal rotation (e.g., swimming, throwing).
  • Falling on an outstretched arm.
  • Immobilization of the shoulder, such as after a fracture or surgery.
  • Chronic poor posture, which shortens the muscle.
  • Over-training 'push' muscles (chest, front of shoulder) without balancing with 'pull' exercises.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Subscapularis Release · 30-60 seconds, 2-3 times daily
    Lie on your side with your arm overhead. Use your opposite thumb to press gently into your armpit, searching for the tender muscle on the front of your shoulder blade. Hold gentle pressure.
  2. Pendulum Swings · 2 x 10 circles each way, twice daily
    Lean over a table, letting the arm hang loose. Use your body to create gentle momentum and swing the arm in small circles to decompress the joint.
  3. Shoulder Drop Hold (video) · 3 x 30-second holds, twice daily
    Lie on your back and let your arm fall open to the side, supported by the floor. This provides a gentle, passive stretch to the front of the shoulder.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Doorway Stretch (Internal Rotators) · 2 x 30 seconds, twice daily
    Stand in a doorway and place the back of your hand and wrist on the frame, with your elbow bent at 90 degrees. Gently turn your body away from the arm to feel a stretch.
  2. Isometric Internal Rotation · 3 x 10-second holds, twice daily
    Stand facing the outside edge of a door frame. With your elbow at 90 degrees, press your palm into the frame as if trying to rotate your arm inward.
  3. Rotator Cuff Internal Rotation (video) · 2 x 15 reps, every other day
    Anchor a light band. With your elbow at your side, pull the band across your body, rotating your arm inward. Control the movement back to the start.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Abducted Internal Rotation (video) · 3 x 10 reps, every other day
    Lie on your back with your arm abducted to 90 degrees, elbow bent. Slowly lower a very light weight toward the floor, then rotate it back up. This strengthens the muscle in a functional position.
  2. Push Up (video) · 3 x as many as possible with good form, every other day
    Perform push-ups (on knees or toes). This integrates the subscapularis with other pressing muscles like the chest and serratus anterior.
  3. Lat Pulldown (video) · 3 x 10-12 reps, every other day
    This exercise strengthens the lats, which are partners in internal rotation, helping to balance the shoulder complex.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Rotator Cuff Internal Rotation, Abducted Internal Rotation, Shoulder Drop Hold.

Habits that help

  • Balance your workout routine. For every 'push' exercise (like bench press), do a 'pull' exercise (like rows).
  • Stretch your chest and lats regularly to prevent them from pulling your shoulder forward and shortening the subscapularis.
  • Avoid carrying heavy bags with a straight arm; keep your elbow slightly bent.
  • Practice proper posture, ensuring your shoulder blades are gently pulled back and down.

What to expect

Due to its tendency to cause significant stiffness, subscapularis issues can be stubborn. Expect gradual improvement in mobility over 3-4 weeks, with strength returning over 6-12 weeks. Consistency is key.

When to see a professional

If you have symptoms of a 'frozen shoulder' (severe, progressive loss of motion) or if you fail the 'Lift-Off Test', it's crucial to get a professional diagnosis to rule out a full tear or adhesive capsulitis. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Complete inability to lift your arm away from your side (abduction).
  • History of shoulder dislocation, as subscapularis tears are common with this injury.
  • Rapid onset of stiffness and pain without any known cause.
  • Pain accompanied by swelling, redness, or warmth over the shoulder joint.
