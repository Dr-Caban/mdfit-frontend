Where the peroneals sends pain

Outer ankle and outside of the foot; common after ankle sprains. People usually come in describing pain at the outer ankle or outer calf.

What the peroneals does

Turn the sole of the foot outward (eversion) and help point the foot down. They are critical for stabilizing the ankle and preventing it from 'rolling' inward.

  • Starts at: The outer surface of the lower leg bone (fibula).
  • Attaches to: One tendon attaches to the outside of the mid-foot (5th metatarsal), and the other runs under the foot to attach to the inside.
  • Nerve supply: Superficial Fibular (Peroneal) Nerve (L5, S1)

Symptoms people describe

  • Aching or sharp pain located behind or below the outer ankle bone.
  • Pain that runs along the outside edge of the foot toward the little toe.
  • A feeling of ankle instability, weakness, or that it might 'give way'.
  • Swelling and tenderness on the outside of the ankle.
  • A snapping or popping sensation on the outside of the ankle with movement.
  • Pain that is worse with cutting, pivoting, or walking on uneven ground.

What makes it worse

Running or sports · Standing or walking. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the peroneals is involved:

  • Is your pain mainly located behind or just below your outer ankle bone?
  • Do you have a history of 'rolling' your ankle (ankle sprains) on this side?
  • Does your ankle feel unstable, like it could easily give out from under you?
  • Is the pain worse when you walk on uneven surfaces like a grassy field or a trail?
  • Do you ever feel a 'snapping' sensation on the outside of your ankle when you walk or run?

Resisted Eversion. Sit in a chair with your foot off the ground. Use your hand to provide resistance on the outside edge of your foot as you try to turn the sole of your foot outward. Suggests this muscle if: A positive test is pain or significant weakness felt behind the outer ankle bone.

Single Leg Balance. Stand on the affected leg and try to balance for 30 seconds without holding on to anything. Suggests this muscle if: A positive test is significant difficulty balancing, a strong feeling of instability, or pain on the outside of the ankle.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Lateral ankle ligament sprain
  • Fracture of the 5th metatarsal (base of the little toe)
  • Sural nerve irritation
  • Ankle impingement syndrome
  • Cuboid syndrome

Common causes

  • Acute ankle sprains, which stretch or damage the peroneal tendons.
  • Chronic ankle instability from repeated sprains.
  • Overuse from running on banked surfaces (like the edge of a road) or uneven trails.
  • Having high-arched feet, which places more stress on the outside of the foot.
  • Wearing worn-out or improper footwear that lacks side-to-side support.
  • Weakness in the hip and gluteal muscles, leading to poor leg control.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Peroneal Muscle Release · 2 minutes, once daily
    Sit on the floor with your leg crossed. Use your thumbs or a lacrosse ball to apply pressure to tender spots in the muscle on the outside of your lower leg, below your knee.
  2. Ankle Alphabet · 2 rounds, twice daily
    Sit with your foot elevated and gently 'draw' the letters of the alphabet in the air with your big toe. This encourages gentle movement in all directions.
  3. 90/90 Breathing (video) · 5 minutes, once daily
    Follow the video to lie in a relaxed position. This helps calm the nervous system, which can be overactive after an injury like an ankle sprain.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Banded Eversion · 3 x 15 reps, every other day
    Anchor a resistance band to a stable object. Sit and loop it around the outside of your foot. Keeping your heel down, rotate your foot outward against the band's resistance.
  2. Ankle Neuroglide (video) · 2 x 10 reps, once daily
    Follow the video to gently mobilize the nerves around the ankle, which are often irritated after ankle sprains and can contribute to pain.
  3. Calf Stretch with Inversion · 2 x 30 seconds, once daily
    Perform a standard calf stretch against a wall, but turn your back foot slightly inward (pigeon-toed). This will direct the stretch more to the outside of the calf.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Single Leg Balance (video) · 3 x 30-60 seconds, daily
    This is the most important exercise to retrain the stabilizing function of the peroneals. As you get better, try it with your eyes closed or on an unstable surface like a pillow.
  2. March Balance (video) · 3 x 10 reps per side, every other day
    This adds a dynamic challenge to your balance, forcing the peroneals to react and stabilize the ankle as you move.
  3. Side Crab Walk (video) · 3 x 10 steps each way, every other day
    Place a band around your knees or ankles. Take side steps as shown to strengthen the hip muscles, which are crucial for controlling leg and ankle alignment.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Single Leg Balance, March Balance, Ankle Neuroglide.

Habits that help

  • Wear supportive shoes, especially for sports. High-top shoes can provide extra stability if you have a history of sprains.
  • Pay close attention when walking on uneven ground, trails, or cobblestones.
  • Avoid sitting for long periods with your ankle 'tucked' under your body.
  • Incorporate balance exercises into your daily routine, such as standing on one leg while brushing your teeth.

What to expect

Following an acute injury, it can take 4-8 weeks to regain stability and strength. For chronic tendon pain, consistent rehab for 6-12 weeks is often needed for significant improvement.

When to see a professional

Book an evaluation if you have persistent ankle instability, a painful 'snapping' tendon, or if you are unable to return to your activities without pain after 4-6 weeks of self-care. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Inability to walk more than a few steps immediately after an ankle injury.
  • A loud 'pop' at the time of injury followed by rapid swelling and bruising.
  • Tenderness directly on the bone at the tip of your outer ankle or on the bony bump on the outside of your mid-foot.
  • Numbness or tingling on the top or outside of your foot.

Frequently asked questions

Can a tight peroneals cause pain at the outer ankle?

Often, yes. Outer ankle and outside of the foot; common after ankle sprains. That referred pain is why it gets blamed on the joint or the spot that hurts instead of the muscle.

How long does peroneals pain take to settle?

Following an acute injury, it can take 4-8 weeks to regain stability and strength. For chronic tendon pain, consistent rehab for 6-12 weeks is often needed for significant improvement.

When should I see someone about it?

Book an evaluation if you have persistent ankle instability, a painful 'snapping' tendon, or if you are unable to return to your activities without pain after 4-6 weeks of self-care.

What else can feel like peroneals pain?

Lateral ankle ligament sprain, Fracture of the 5th metatarsal (base of the little toe), Sural nerve irritation. An evaluation separates muscle pain from these, and anything that needs a physician is referred on.
