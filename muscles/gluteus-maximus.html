Where the gluteus maximus sends pain

Buttock and the tailbone; aches with long sitting and walking uphill. People usually come in describing pain at the tailbone or buttock crease.

What the gluteus maximus does

The main muscle for extending your hip. It powers movements like standing up from a chair, climbing stairs, running, and lifting.

  • Starts at: Ilium (back of the hip bone), sacrum, and coccyx (tailbone).
  • Attaches to: Gluteal tuberosity of the femur (thigh bone) and the iliotibial (IT) band.
  • Nerve supply: Inferior gluteal nerve (L5, S1, S2).

Symptoms people describe

  • A deep, nagging ache in the buttock.
  • Pain concentrated around the tailbone (coccyx) and the bony part you sit on (ischial tuberosity).
  • Stiffness and pain after sitting for a long time, especially on hard surfaces.
  • A feeling of weakness when climbing stairs or getting up from a squat.
  • Pain that makes it hard to lie on your back or the affected side.
  • A 'bruised' feeling deep in the buttock.

What makes it worse

Sitting · Stairs, squats or hills. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the gluteus maximus is involved:

  • Is your pain mainly in the fleshy part of your buttock, possibly near your tailbone?
  • Does the ache get worse after sitting for more than 30 minutes, especially on a hard chair?
  • Do you feel pain or weakness when pushing up stairs, walking up a hill, or rising from a deep chair?
  • Is the pain a deep ache rather than a sharp, shooting, or tingling pain down your leg?
  • Does pressing deeply into the main part of your buttock reproduce your specific ache?

Active Hip Extension Test. Lie on your stomach with your legs straight. Squeeze the buttock muscles on the painful side and lift that entire leg a few inches off the floor, keeping the knee straight. Suggests this muscle if: You feel your specific buttock pain, or the leg feels significantly weaker or heavier than the other side.

Glute Bridge Test. Lie on your back with knees bent and feet flat on the floor, hip-width apart. Squeeze your buttocks and lift your hips toward the ceiling. Suggests this muscle if: This movement reproduces your buttock or tailbone pain, or you feel your hamstrings cramping because the glutes aren't working well.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Sciatica or lumbar radiculopathy (nerve root irritation).
  • Sacroiliac (SI) joint dysfunction.
  • Piriformis syndrome.
  • Ischial bursitis ('sits bone' bursitis).
  • Coccygodynia (tailbone pain).

Common causes

  • Prolonged sitting, which compresses and deactivates the muscle.
  • Sudden increase in activities like running, hiking, or weightlifting (squats, deadlifts).
  • Direct trauma, such as a fall onto the buttocks.
  • Sleeping on a hard mattress or on one side without support.
  • An inactive lifestyle leading to weak, inhibited gluteal muscles.
  • Poor form during exercise, leading to overuse or strain.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Glute Pressure Release · 2 minutes, twice daily
    Sit on a lacrosse ball or tennis ball, placing it on the most tender spot in your buttock. Gently roll to apply pressure, but avoid sharp, shooting pain.
  2. 90/90 Breathing (video) · 2 x 5 minutes, daily
    Lie on your back with feet on a wall or chair, knees and hips at 90 degrees. Focus on deep belly breaths to relax the pelvic floor and hips.
  3. Dynamic Childs Pose (video) · 2 x 10 reps, daily
    Gently rock your hips side to side in a child's pose to create space and mobility in the low back and hips.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Quadruped Glute Kick (video) · 3 x 15 per side, every other day
    On all fours, keep your core braced and extend one leg back, squeezing the glute at the top. Avoid arching your lower back.
  2. Single Leg Glute Bridge (video) · 3 x 10 per side, every other day
    Lie on your back with one knee bent and the other leg straight. Drive through the heel to lift your hips, focusing the work in your glute.
  3. Hip Neuroglide (video) · 2 x 10 reps, daily
    Perform this gentle nerve mobilization to ensure the nerves around the hip are moving freely.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Hip Thrust (video) · 3 x 10-12, twice weekly
    With your upper back on a bench and a weight across your hips, drive your hips up by squeezing your glutes. Keep your chin tucked.
  2. Romanian Deadlift (video) · 3 x 8-10, twice weekly
    Holding weights, hinge at your hips with a flat back, feeling a stretch in your hamstrings. Squeeze your glutes to return to standing.
  3. Constant Glute Engagement (video) · Incorporate into daily walking
    Practice this cue to gently engage your glutes with each step, improving your mind-muscle connection throughout the day.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Hip Thrust, Quadruped Glute Kick, Constant Glute Engagement.

Habits that help

  • Take frequent breaks from sitting. Use our 'Walk Break Protocol Seated' every 30 minutes.
  • Sit on a cushion or donut pillow to reduce direct pressure on the muscle.
  • When standing, maintain a neutral pelvis instead of letting your hips sway forward.
  • Focus on pushing through your heels when climbing stairs or standing up to engage the glutes.

What to expect

With consistent application of this protocol, most people feel a significant reduction in pain within 2-3 weeks. Building full strength and resilience can take 6-8 weeks.

When to see a professional

Book an appointment if your pain is severe, doesn't improve after 2 weeks of self-care, or if you experience any of the 'Red Flags'. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Pain accompanied by numbness or tingling in the 'saddle' area (groin, inner thighs).
  • Sudden, unexplained loss of bowel or bladder control.
  • Progressive weakness in the leg, causing you to trip or your foot to 'drop'.
  • Pain that is constant, severe, and doesn't change with position or movement.
