Where the iliacus sends pain

Groin, front of the thigh, and the back of the pelvis high in the glute. Often overlooked. People usually come in describing pain at the groin, front of the hip bone, upper buttock at the belt line, front of the thigh or si joint and back of the pelvis.

What the iliacus does

Works with the psoas as a primary hip flexor, lifting the thigh toward the chest. Also helps stabilize the pelvis.

  • Starts at: Iliac fossa (the inner surface of the pelvic bone).
  • Attaches to: Lesser trochanter of the femur (shares a tendon with the psoas).
  • Nerve supply: Femoral nerve (L2, L3).

Symptoms people describe

  • A persistent ache deep in the groin or front pocket area.
  • Pain that refers to the front of the thigh.
  • A tender spot on the front of the hip, just inside the bony point (ASIS).
  • Pain that can also refer to the low back or upper buttock area.
  • Stiffness and pain when getting up from a chair, especially after a long car ride.

What makes it worse

Sitting · Running or sports. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the iliacus is involved:

  • Is your primary pain located deep in your groin, like in your front pocket?
  • Do you feel a very tender spot if you press your fingers just inside the bony point at the front of your hip?
  • Does the pain get worse when you are sitting for a long time, especially while driving?
  • Does your pain sometimes feel like it's in your low back or high in your glute, but the main spot is in the front?
  • Do you feel a 'catching' sensation in the front of your hip when you stand up?

Iliacus Palpation. Lie on your back with your knees bent. Find the bony point at the front of your hip (ASIS). Place your fingertips just inside this bone and gently curl them into your abdomen as you exhale. Slowly straighten your leg. Suggests this muscle if: A sharp, localized tenderness or reproduction of your familiar groin pain indicates iliacus involvement.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Hip joint impingement (FAI) or labral tear
  • Inguinal hernia
  • Sports hernia (athletic pubalgia)
  • Femoral nerve entrapment
  • Hip flexor strain (tendonitis)

Common causes

  • Prolonged sitting, which keeps the muscle in a shortened position.
  • Activities involving repetitive hip flexion, like running, cycling, or kicking.
  • Driving for long periods, especially with the gas pedal foot.
  • Wearing a heavy tool belt that presses on the front of the hips.
  • A history of falls or direct impact to the front of the pelvis.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Iliacus Release (Ball) · 2-3 minutes, once daily
    Lie on your back with knees bent. Place a small, soft ball just inside your hip bone. Let the ball sink in as you breathe deeply. Avoid any pulsing or sharp pain.
  2. Dynamic Childs Pose (video) · 2 x 10 rocks, twice daily
    From a child's pose position, gently rock your hips side to side to create a mild stretch and movement in the deep hip flexors and low back.
  3. 90/90 Breathing (video) · 5 minutes, once daily
    Lie on your back with your lower legs on a chair or box, so your hips and knees are at 90 degrees. This position helps the iliacus and psoas to relax.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Couch Stretch (video) · 2 x 30-60 sec per side, daily
    This stretch targets the entire iliopsoas complex. Focus on keeping your torso upright and squeezing the glute of the back leg to deepen the stretch.
  2. Hip Neuroglide (video) · 2 x 10 reps per side, daily
    Gently mobilize the femoral nerve, which passes near the iliacus, to ensure it can move freely and reduce irritation.
  3. Glute Bridge · 2 x 15 reps, every other day
    Lie on your back with knees bent. Squeeze your glutes and lift your hips toward the ceiling. This activates the glutes, which helps the tight hip flexors to relax (reciprocal inhibition).

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Dead Bug (video) · 3 x 10 reps (alternating), every other day
    This exercise strengthens the core muscles that stabilize the pelvis, reducing the strain on the iliacus.
  2. Bodyweight Squat (video) · 3 x 12 reps, every other day
    Practice squatting with good form, focusing on using your glutes to power the movement up. This retrains proper hip mechanics.
  3. Lunges (video) · 3 x 10 reps per side, every other day
    Lunges build single-leg stability and strength in the glutes and quads, which supports healthy hip function.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Couch Stretch, Hip Neuroglide, Dynamic Childs Pose.

Habits that help

  • Take frequent breaks from sitting. Use our 'Walk Break Protocol Seated' video.
  • Adjust your car seat to be more upright, reducing the sharp angle at your hips.
  • When standing, avoid locking your knees and letting your pelvis tilt forward.
  • Use a lumbar support roll when sitting to maintain a neutral spinal curve.

What to expect

Most people experience noticeable relief within 1-2 weeks of consistent release work and stretching. Building strength to prevent recurrence typically takes 4-6 weeks.

When to see a professional

Schedule a consultation if the pain is sharp, doesn't improve with 2 weeks of this protocol, or if you suspect a hernia. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • A palpable lump or bulge in the groin area.
  • Numbness, tingling, or weakness in the front of the thigh.
  • Pain accompanied by fever, night sweats, or unexplained weight loss.
  • Inability to bear weight on the leg.

Frequently asked questions

Can a tight iliacus cause pain at the groin?

Often, yes. Groin, front of the thigh, and the back of the pelvis high in the glute. Often overlooked. That referred pain is why it gets blamed on the joint or the spot that hurts instead of the muscle.

How long does iliacus pain take to settle?

Most people experience noticeable relief within 1-2 weeks of consistent release work and stretching. Building strength to prevent recurrence typically takes 4-6 weeks.

When should I see someone about it?

Schedule a consultation if the pain is sharp, doesn't improve with 2 weeks of this protocol, or if you suspect a hernia.

What else can feel like iliacus pain?

Hip joint impingement (FAI) or labral tear, Inguinal hernia, Sports hernia (athletic pubalgia). An evaluation separates muscle pain from these, and anything that needs a physician is referred on.
