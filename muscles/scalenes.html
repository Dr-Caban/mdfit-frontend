What the scalenes does

Lifts the top two ribs to help you take a deep breath (accessory breathing). Also bends your neck to the side and helps with turning.

  • Starts at: Sides of your neck vertebrae (C2-C7).
  • Attaches to: The top surfaces of your first and second ribs.
  • Nerve supply: Cervical spinal nerves (C3-C8).

Symptoms people describe

  • Aching pain in the chest, sometimes mistaken for a heart issue.
  • Pain, numbness, or tingling that travels from the neck down the arm to the thumb and index finger.
  • A feeling of a 'dead arm' or waking up with a numb hand.
  • Aching pain between the shoulder blades.
  • Pain is often worse when taking a deep breath, coughing, or carrying heavy items.
  • Difficulty finding a comfortable sleeping position for your head and arm.

What makes it worse

Deep breaths or coughing · Desk, phone or screens · Stress · Lying on it at night. If two or more of those match your day, this muscle moves up the list.

Quick self-check

Education, not a diagnosis. The more of these that ring true, the more likely the scalenes is involved:

  • Do you feel pain or tingling that runs from your neck down your arm to your thumb and index finger?
  • Does your pain get worse when you carry something heavy, like a bag of groceries?
  • Do you ever feel a strange, non-cardiac pain in your chest, especially after a lot of coughing?
  • Do you often wake up with a 'dead arm' or tingling in your hand?
  • Is your pain aggravated by looking at a phone or computer screen for long periods?

Scalene Relief Test. While sitting, gently lift the arm on the painful side and rest your forearm and hand on top of your head. Hold for 30 seconds. Suggests this muscle if: A significant reduction or complete relief of your arm and hand symptoms while your arm is raised.

What it gets mistaken for

  • Cervical disc herniation (pinched nerve in the neck).
  • Thoracic Outlet Syndrome (TOS).
  • Rotator cuff injury.
  • Angina or cardiac pain (always rule this out first with a physician).
  • Carpal Tunnel Syndrome.

Common causes

  • Shallow, chest-breathing patterns, especially during stress or intense exercise.
  • Forward head posture from prolonged desk work or phone use ('tech neck').
  • Whiplash injury from a car accident or fall.
  • Chronic coughing from asthma, bronchitis, or allergies.
  • Carrying heavy backpacks or purses with a strap over one shoulder.
  • Sleeping with your arm overhead or under your head.

Dr. Jared's 3-phase relief plan

Work through the phases in order and move on when the current one feels easy and pain-free. Every video is in the free MD Fit app.

Phase 1 · Calm it down

  1. Diaphragmatic Breathing (video: 90/90 Breathing) · 5 minutes, twice daily
    Lie on your back with feet up on a chair. Focus on letting your belly rise as you inhale and fall as you exhale, keeping your chest and neck relaxed.
  2. Scalene Pressure Release · 60-90 seconds per side, daily
    Using the fingers of your opposite hand, gently press into the soft, ropy muscles in the groove on the side of your neck, above your collarbone. Hold gentle pressure on tender spots.
  3. Gentle Scalene Stretch (video: Standing Scalene Stretch) · 2 x 30 sec per side, daily
    Gently tilt your ear away from the shoulder you're stretching. To deepen, you can gently hold onto a chair or place your other hand over your head.

Phase 2 · Restore movement

  1. Eccentric Scalene Control (video: Eccentric Scalene Drop) · 2 x 8 reps per side, every other day
    Use your hand to help lift your head from a side-tilted position, then slowly lower it back down over 3-5 seconds, controlling the movement.
  2. Neck & Arm Nerve Glide (video: Neck Neuroglide Seated) · 1 x 10 reps per side, daily
    Gently move through the prescribed range of motion to mobilize the nerves that pass through the neck and arm. Move slowly and without pain.
  3. Postural Reset (video: Seated Desk Posture Reset) · Every 30 minutes at your desk
    Follow the video cues to reset your head, neck, and shoulder position to a more neutral alignment.

Phase 3 · Strengthen

  1. Deep Neck Flexor Activation (video: Deep Cervical Flexor Nods Seated) · 2 x 10 sec holds, 5 reps, daily
    Gently tuck your chin to create a 'double chin' and feel the deep muscles at the front of your neck engage. Don't use your large, outer neck muscles.
  2. Core Bracing (video: Core Brace Intro Seated) · 3 x 30 sec holds, daily
    Engage your abdominal muscles as if you're about to be punched in the stomach. Breathe normally while holding this tension.
  3. Loaded Carries (video: Farmer Carry) · 3 x 40 yards, twice weekly
    Hold a moderate weight in each hand, stand tall with good posture, and walk. This builds postural endurance for the neck and shoulders.

Suggested library videos for this muscle: Standing Scalene Stretch, Eccentric Scalene Drop, 90/90 Breathing.

Habits that help

  • Set a timer to take a 'Walk Break' every 30-60 minutes to break up prolonged sitting.
  • Adjust your monitor height so the top of the screen is at or slightly below eye level.
  • Practice belly breathing throughout the day, especially when you feel stressed.
  • Avoid sleeping on your stomach or with your arm tucked under your head.

What to expect

With consistent application of these steps, most people feel a noticeable improvement in 2-4 weeks. Full resolution may take 6-8 weeks.

When to see a professional

Book an evaluation if your symptoms include significant weakness, numbness that doesn't go away, or if the pain is not improving after 2 weeks of consistent self-care. Get urgent care for any of these:

  • Chest pain accompanied by shortness of breath, sweating, or nausea (call 911).
  • Sudden, severe weakness or loss of coordination in your arm or hand.
  • Pain that is constant, unrelenting, and not changed by position.
  • A cold, pale, or blue hand.
