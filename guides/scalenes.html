The Scalenes & Intercostals

ANATOMY • ATTACHMENTS • PAIN PATTERNS • CLINICAL PEARLS

“The scalene muscles are a bridge between the neck, the rib cage, and the arm. When they become tight, they don’t just affect your breathing – they can send pain far beyond the neck.” - MD FIT

Referred pain patterns

  • Scalenes (Lateral View - Arm & Shoulder)

    Pain can radiate from the scalenes to the acromion and down the lateral arm, forearm, and into the thumb and fingers.

  • Scalenes (Anterior View)

    Can refer pain to the anterior shoulder, chest, and down the front of the arm into the hand (often thumb, index and middle fingers).

  • Scalenes (Posterior View)

    Can refer pain to the scapular region (especially medial border), posterior shoulder, and down the back of the arm into the hand.

  • Scalenes (Scapular View - Posterior)

    Commonly refers pain to the upper, medial, and inferior scapular region. Can mimic rhomboid or levator scapulae pain.

Anatomy & attachments

Anterior Scalene

Origin
Anterior tubercles of transverse processes C3–C6
Insertion
Superior surface of 1st rib (scalene tubercle, anterior to subclavian groove)
Notes
Passes anterior to the Brachial Plexus.

Middle Scalene

Origin
Posterior tubercles of transverse processes C2–C7
Insertion
Superior surface of 1st rib (posterior to subclavian groove)
Notes
The Brachial Plexus passes between the anterior and middle scalene.

Posterior Scalene

Origin
Posterior tubercles of transverse processes C4–C7
Insertion
Superior surface of 2nd rib

External Intercostals

Action
elevate ribs – inspiration
Notes
Intercostal muscles run between the ribs and work with the diaphragm and scalenes to facilitate breathing. Dysfunction can contribute to rib pain, chest tightness, and referred shoulder or back pain.

Internal Intercostals

Action
depress ribs – forced expiration

Innermost Intercostals

Action
help with forced expiration

Key Functions

  • Assist with breathing (inspiration)
  • Stabilize the cervical spine
  • Influence neck, shoulder and upper back pain
  • Can refer pain into the arm, hand, and scapular region
  • Commonly involved in headaches, thoracic outlet syndrome, and shoulder dysfunction

Scalenes & Neurovascular Relationships

  • Scalenes can contribute to neurovascular compression (Thoracic Outlet Syndrome)

Common Symptoms

  • Neck pain and stiffness
  • Shoulder pain (anterior, lateral, or posterior)
  • Pain radiating down the arm (may reach hand)
  • Scapular pain or burning between shoulder blades
  • Chest tightness or rib pain
  • Headaches (especially at the base of the skull)
  • Tingling or numbness (possible thoracic outlet involvement)
  • Pain with deep breathing

Clinical Pearls

  • Assess breathing pattern – look for accessory muscle use.
  • Palpate scalenes gently – they can be tender and refer pain.
  • Evaluate rib mobility and intercostal tightness.
  • Consider scalene involvement in persistent shoulder or arm pain.
  • Differentiate from cervical radiculopathy and rotator cuff pathology.
  • Treatment: soft tissue release, breathing retraining, postural correction, and rib mobility.

The takeaway

The scalenes are more than breathing muscles.

They can refer pain to the shoulder, scapula, chest, and entire arm.

Intercostal muscles often work with the scalenes and can contribute to similar symptoms.

Accurate assessment helps identify the true source of pain and leads to more effective treatment.

Restore mobility, improve breathing, and reduce unnecessary pain.
