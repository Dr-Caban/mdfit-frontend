Referred pain patterns

  • Gluteus minimus

    Pain often refers to the lateral hip and flank (iliac crest region), then travels in a straight line down the lateral side of the leg into the lateral calf.

  • Piriformis

    Pain starts in the middle of the glute, travels inferior-laterally across the buttock, down the posterior-lateral thigh, continues down the lateral calf, and can reach the top of the lateral foot.

  • Tensor Fasciae Latae (TFL)

    Pain radiates down the anterior-lateral thigh, overlapping the vastus lateralis (quadriceps), and typically does not travel past the knee.

Anatomy & attachments

Glute Minimus

Origin
Outer surface of ilium (between anterior and inferior gluteal lines)
Insertion
Anterior facet of the greater trochanter (of femur)
Action
Hip abduction; Pelvic stabilization (especially during single-leg stance); Medial rotation; Keeps pelvis level during gait
Nerve
Superior gluteal nerve (L4–S1)
Blood supply
Superior gluteal artery
Notes
HIP STABILIZER (DEEP LATERAL)

Piriformis

Origin
Anterior surface of sacrum (S2–S4)
Insertion
Greater trochanter (superior medial facet)
Action
External rotation of the hip; Abduction (especially at 90° hip flexion); Stabilizes the hip joint
Nerve
Nerve to piriformis (S1–S2)
Blood supply
Inferior gluteal artery
Notes
DEEP EXTERNAL ROTATOR

Tensor Fasciae Latae (tfl)

Origin
Anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS) and adjacent iliac crest
Insertion
Iliotibial band (ITB) (to Gerdy's tubercle on lateral tibia)
Action
Hip flexion; Hip abduction; Medial rotation; Tenses the iliotibial band (stabilizes the knee)
Nerve
Superior gluteal nerve (L4–S1)
Blood supply
Lateral circumflex femoral artery (ascending branch)
Notes
HIP FLEXOR & ABDUCTOR ASSIST

Key Comparisons

  • Glute minimus: lateral line (flank → lateral leg/calf)
  • Piriformis: posterior-lateral line (glute → calf → lateral foot)
  • TFL: anterior-lateral thigh (over vastus lateralis, stops at knee)
  • Different origins, insertions, innervations, and pain patterns.
  • Evaluate and treat each muscle specifically.

Clinical Pearls

  • Consider nerve involvement (superior gluteal nerve, sciatic nerve, lateral femoral cutaneous nerve).
  • Assess for vascular compression or irritation.
  • Different pain patterns help identify the true source.
  • Address contributing factors (pelvic position, gait, core control).
  • Restore mobility, reduce tension, and retrain proper function.
