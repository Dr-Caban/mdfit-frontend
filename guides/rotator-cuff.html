The Rotator Cuff

ANATOMY • ATTACHMENTS • PAIN PATTERNS • CLINICAL INSIGHTS

The rotator cuff consists of four muscles that stabilize the glenohumeral joint, center the humeral head, and control shoulder movement. Each muscle has a unique attachment and a distinct referred pain pattern.

"A strong rotator cuff keeps you in the game." - MD FIT

Referred pain patterns

  • Supraspinatus

    Pain in the top of the shoulder (acromion area). Can radiate down the lateral arm (deltoid region). May refer to the lateral forearm and thumb side of the hand. Often aggravated by overhead activity.

  • Infraspinatus

    Pain in the back of the shoulder (below the spine of scapula). Radiates down the posterior lateral arm. Can travel to the lateral forearm and hand (outside of arm). Often associated with external rotation weakness.

  • Teres Minor

    Pain in the back of the shoulder (inferior to infraspinatus). Radiates down the posterior lateral arm. Can extend to the lateral forearm and hand (outside of arm). Often feels deep and achy.

  • Subscapularis

    Pain in the front of the shoulder (anterior, deep). Can radiate down the medial (inner) arm. May travel to the medial forearm and ring/pinky fingers. Often associated with internal rotation weakness.

  • Scapular Pain (All RC)

    Rotator cuff trigger points can refer pain to the shoulder blade (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor). Often felt along the medial border or under the scapula. Can mimic 'nerve pain' or rhomboid pain.

Anatomy & attachments

Supraspinatus

Origin
Supraspinous fossa
Insertion
Greater tubercle (superior facet)
Action
Initiates abduction (0-15°) and stabilizes the joint
Notes
Common Issues: Tendinopathy, impingement, overload

Infraspinatus

Origin
Infraspinous fossa
Insertion
Greater tubercle (middle facet)
Action
External rotation and stabilization
Notes
Common Issues: Tightness, trigger points, posterior shoulder pain

Teres Minor

Origin
Lateral border of scapula
Insertion
Greater tubercle (inferior facet)
Action
External rotation and stabilization
Notes
Common Issues: Trigger points, posterior shoulder pain, referred pain down lateral arm

Subscapularis

Origin
Subscapular fossa
Insertion
Lesser tubercle
Action
Internal rotation and stabilization
Notes
Common Issues: Tightness, strain, anterior shoulder pain

Rotator Cuff Overview

  • Stabilizes the shoulder
  • Centers the humeral head
  • Controls rotation and elevation
  • Works with the deltoid and scapula
  • Common source of shoulder and arm pain

How to Differentiate

  • Muscle: Supraspinatus, Primary Pain Location: Top of shoulder (acromion), Arm Radiation: Lateral arm -> thumb side, Key Movement Pain: Abduction (0-15°)
  • Muscle: Infraspinatus, Primary Pain Location: Back of shoulder (below spine), Arm Radiation: Posterior lateral arm -> outside of hand, Key Movement Pain: External rotation
  • Muscle: Teres Minor, Primary Pain Location: Lower posterior shoulder, Arm Radiation: Posterior lateral arm -> outside of hand, Key Movement Pain: External rotation
  • Muscle: Subscapularis, Primary Pain Location: Front of shoulder (anterior), Arm Radiation: Medial arm -> ring/pinky fingers, Key Movement Pain: Internal rotation

Clinical Pearls

  • Palpate each muscle's insertion and reproduce symptoms.
  • Use resisted testing (abduction, external rotation, internal rotation) to identify the source.
  • Look for scapular dysfunction and thoracic mobility deficits.
  • Rotator cuff pain can refer far down the arm — don't assume it's 'nerve pain.'
  • Treat the source: release tight tissue, restore mobility, strengthen, and correct movement patterns.
  • Address posture (shoulders down, rib cage stacked).

The takeaway

Each rotator cuff muscle has a unique insertion and pain pattern.

Pain can radiate down the arm — either lateral (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor) or medial (subscapularis).

Accurate assessment leads to targeted treatment and faster results.

A healthy rotator cuff keeps you strong, mobile, and pain-free.
