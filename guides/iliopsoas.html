The Iliopsoas Complex

ANATOMY • FUNCTION • PAIN PATTERNS • CLINICAL INSIGHTS

A STRONG CORE STARTS AT THE HIPS.

“Loosen what holds you back. Strengthen what moves you forward.”

Referred pain patterns

  • Psoas

    Can refer pain to the lower back, up the lumbar spine, and even to the area below the shoulder blades (thoracolumbar junction).

  • Iliacus

    Tight iliacus can refer pain to the back of the iliac crest and high in the gluteal region.

  • Iliacus

    Common referral to the groin and inner thigh.

  • Psoas & Iliacus

    Can refer pain to the anterior thigh and quadriceps, often feeling like a deep, achy or tight pain.

  • Iliopsoas Complex

    May also contribute to sacral pain and can occasionally refer pain into the inner knee region.

Anatomy & attachments

Psoas Major

Origin
T12-L5 (vertebral bodies, transverse processes, intervertebral discs)
Insertion
Lesser trochanter of femur

Iliacus

Origin
Iliac fossa (inner surface of ilium)
Insertion
Lesser trochanter of femur

Iliopsoas Tendon

Insertion
Lesser trochanter of femur
Notes
This is the common insertion tendon for the Psoas Major and Iliacus muscles.

How the Psoas Pulls on Your Spine

  • The psoas attaches to the lumbar vertebrae (T12-L5) and runs downward to the femur.
  • When the psoas is chronically tight (from prolonged sitting, stress, or hip flexion), it pulls the lumbar spine into: Increased lumbar lordosis (anterior pelvic tilt), Compression of the lower lumbar segments (especially L4-L5), Altered disc mechanics, Increased load on facet joints, Inhibition of glute activation and abdominal function.
  • A tight psoas doesn't just affect the hip... It changes the entire lumbar-pelvic system.

Functions

  • Primary hip flexor (flexes the thigh)
  • Stabilizes the lumbar spine
  • Assists in trunk flexion
  • Helps with postural control
  • Works with the diaphragm in breathing mechanics
  • Important for gait, running, and functional movement

Iliacus - the Hidden Driver

  • The iliacus lies deep in the pelvis and blends with the psoas major to form the iliopsoas tendon. When tight, the iliacus is a common but often overlooked source of:
  • Pain around the back of the iliac crest (high glute/upper hip)
  • Groin pain
  • Pain into the anterior thigh (quadriceps)
  • Feeling of hip 'tightness' or restricted mobility
  • Altered pelvic mechanics

Common Causes of Tightness

  • Prolonged sitting (hip flexion)
  • Repetitive hip flexion (running, cycling)
  • Stress and sympathetic nervous system overactivity
  • Core weakness / poor pelvic control
  • Previous low back or hip injury
  • Breathing dysfunction (overuse of accessory muscles)
  • Poor posture (anterior pelvic tilt)

Key Clinical Pearls

  • Assess both psoas and iliacus — they have different referral patterns.
  • Iliacus is the key source for high glute, iliac crest, groin, and quad pain.
  • A tight psoas can increase lumbar lordosis and contribute to low back pain.
  • Look for associated breathing dysfunction and core inhibition.
  • Release and lengthen the iliopsoas, then retrain the glutes and deep core.

Effective Interventions

  • Soft tissue release (psoas and iliacus)
  • Diaphragmatic breathing and rib mobility
  • Hip extension drills and glute activation
  • Core stability training (dead bug, bird dog, Pallof press)
  • Postural correction (reduce anterior pelvic tilt)
  • Avoid prolonged sitting and incorporate regular movement

How this pattern shows up day to day

  • Standing up after twenty minutes in a chair or a long drive and needing a few steps before you can straighten fully.
  • A vertical band of ache beside the lumbar spine that eases with knees to chest and returns when you lie flat.

Self-check (education, not diagnosis)

  • Modified Thomas test: sit on the edge of a bed, lie back hugging both knees, then let one leg hang while the low back stays flat. A thigh that floats, or a knee that drifts straight, suggests a short iliopsoas.
  • Iliacus palpation: with knees bent, curl your fingertips just inside the front hip bone as you exhale, then slowly straighten the leg. Familiar groin tenderness points at the iliacus rather than the psoas.

When to get it checked

  • Groin or back pain with fever, nausea, night sweats or unexplained weight loss.
  • Any change in bowel or bladder control, or numbness in the groin or saddle area.
  • A lump or bulge in the groin, which needs a hernia check rather than a stretch.

Go deeper

  • Muscle pages: psoas and iliacus and rectus femoris.
  • Where it hurts: front of the hip, groin, beside the spine, upper buttock and front of the thigh.
  • Related conditions: low back pain and hip labral tear.

The takeaway

The iliopsoas complex is more than a hip flexor — it connects your spine, pelvis, and legs.

Tightness can create pain far beyond the hip, from the back to the quad.

Find the true source. Treat the root. Restore movement.
