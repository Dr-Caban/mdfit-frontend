The Calf Complex

GASTROCNEMIUS • SOLEUS • POSTERIOR TIBIALIS

Strong Foundations Keep You Moving Forward.

Referred pain patterns

  • Gastrocnemius

    Pain in the upper calf ("tennis leg"). Can radiate to the back of the knee. May refer to the lower calf and Achilles. Can contribute to heel pain. Often feels like a deep, aching tightness or cramp.

  • Soleus

    Deeper, lower calf pain (often felt with standing and walking). Can refer to the Achilles tendon and heel. May cause a diffuse, achy pain behind the lower leg. Often associated with postural fatigue. Common in runners, hikers, and those who stand for long periods.

  • Tibialis Posterior

    Pain along the inner lower leg (posterior medial aspect). Can radiate to the inside of the ankle. May refer to the arch or medial foot. Often associated with flat foot, overpronation, and medial ankle pain. Can cause a deep, aching pain with activity or after prolonged standing.

  • Combined Patterns

    Gastrocnemius (upper calf)
    Soleus (lower calf)
    Tibialis posterior (inner lower leg)
    Overlapping trigger points can create complex pain patterns in the calf, Achilles, ankle, and foot.

Anatomy & attachments

Gastrocnemius

Origin
Medial head: posterior femoral condyle
Lateral head: lateral femoral condyle
Insertion
Posterior calcaneus (via Achilles tendon)
Action
Plantarflexion (strong), knee flexion
Nerve
Tibial nerve (S1–S2)
Blood supply
Sural branches (popliteal artery), posterior tibial artery

Soleus

Origin
Posterior head of fibula, soleal line and medial border of tibia, tendinous arch
Insertion
Posterior calcaneus (via Achilles tendon)
Action
Plantarflexion (postural/ endurance)
Nerve
Tibial nerve (S1–S2)
Blood supply
Posterior tibial artery (primary), peroneal artery (contribution)

Tibialis Posterior

Origin
Posterior surface of tibia (below soleal line), interosseous membrane, posterior fibula
Insertion
Navicular (tuberosity), cuneiforms, cuboid, bases of metatarsals 2–4
Action
Inversion, arch support, plantarflexion, helps stabilize the medial foot
Nerve
Tibial nerve (L4–L5)
Blood supply
Posterior tibial artery

Clinical Pearls

  • Calf tightness can contribute to plantar fasciitis, Achilles tendinopathy, and knee pain.
  • The tibial nerve innervates both gastrocnemius and soleus — neural tension can mimic muscular pain.
  • The posterior tibial artery and tibial nerve run deep between the soleus and tibialis posterior.
  • Posterior tibialis dysfunction can lead to flat foot, medial ankle pain, and poor gait mechanics.
  • Assess strength, flexibility, and trigger points in all three muscles.
  • Treatment: soft tissue release, eccentric loading, ankle mobility, and foot intrinsic strengthening.

How this pattern shows up day to day

  • Gastrocnemius: a runner or hiker who added mileage or hills too fast, now waking at night with an upper-calf cramp and an arch that aches in the morning.
  • Soleus: someone who stands all day on a hard floor or drives for hours, ending the day with a low-grade ache in the lower calf and heel that mimics plantar fasciitis but sits further back.
  • Tibialis posterior: an inner ankle that hurts on the first steps of the morning, an arch that seems to be flattening, and a heel raise that is suddenly hard on one side.

Self-check: which calf muscle is talking?

  • Straight-knee heel raise on one leg: pain or weakness high in the calf points toward the gastrocnemius.
  • Bent-knee heel raise (knee at about 45 degrees): pain or weakness low in the calf or at the heel isolates the soleus.
  • Watch the heel as you rise. If it rolls outward, or the inner ankle lights up, the tibialis posterior is likely involved.

What tends to make it worse

  • Running, hills and stairs, especially after a jump in volume or intensity.
  • Long stretches of standing on hard surfaces, or driving with the foot pinned on the pedal.
  • High heels, which keep the calf shortened, or a rushed switch to minimalist shoes, which overloads it.

What Dr. Jared does about it

  • Assess. Heel raises with the knee straight and bent, ankle and arch mobility, gait, and a tibial nerve check so a nerve or vascular cause is not mistaken for muscle.
  • Release. Hands-on work and medical massage to the gastrocnemius, soleus and the deep compartment where the tibialis posterior lives.
  • Correct. Restore ankle dorsiflexion and big-toe motion, then clean up push-off so the calf stops doing the hips' job.
  • Homework. A short daily routine in the free MD Fit app: calf and soleus stretches, slow eccentric heel drops and foot intrinsic drills.
  • Strengthen. Loaded heel raises through full range, single-leg balance and hopping progressions for runners.

When to get it checked

  • Sudden calf pain with swelling, redness and warmth in one leg. A blood clot (DVT) must be ruled out urgently; go to the emergency department rather than stretching it.
  • A pop or a feeling of being kicked in the back of the leg, then trouble walking or rising onto the toes. This can be a torn gastrocnemius or a ruptured Achilles.
  • Numbness or loss of sensation in the foot, or pain that is unchanged by rest and severe at night.
