The basic trade

A training session temporarily makes you worse: tired, sore, slightly weaker. The improvement comes afterwards, during recovery, and only if recovery is allowed to happen.

Stack sessions too closely and you accumulate fatigue faster than you adapt. For a while this looks like discipline. Then performance flattens, then it declines, and the instinctive response — train harder — makes it worse.

This is the single most common pattern we see in motivated people who have stopped progressing. It is rarely a programming subtlety. It is usually too much, too often, with no easy days.

What it looks like when it is tipping

  • Sessions that feel disproportionately hard at weights or paces that were comfortable a fortnight ago.
  • Sleep getting worse rather than better, despite being tired.
  • Resting heart rate drifting upward over a week or two.
  • Irritability, low motivation, a flat mood around training specifically.
  • Niggles multiplying — several small complaints rather than one clear injury.
  • Getting ill more often than usual.

One bad session means nothing. Several of these together across two or three weeks means something.

How much rest

At least one full day off a week for most people, and often two. Beyond that, the useful question is not how many days off but how many genuinely hard days are in the week.

Two to three hard sessions a week is plenty for most people who are not athletes. The rest can be easy aerobic work, mobility, or light technical practice. A week with six hard sessions is not six times better than a week with two; it is usually worse.

Every eight to twelve weeks, a lighter week — reducing volume by around half while keeping movement quality — lets accumulated fatigue clear. See deload weeks.

Age changes the arithmetic

Recovery between hard sessions tends to take longer with age. That is not a reason to train less hard; it is a reason to train hard less often.

Many people in their fifties and sixties get better results from two quality sessions a week with real recovery between them than from four mediocre ones. The stimulus still needs to be meaningful — easy training does not maintain muscle or bone — but the spacing matters more than it did at twenty-five.

Overreaching and overtraining are not the same thing

Deliberately pushing a hard block and feeling flat for a week or two is normal, and performance usually rebounds above where it started once the load eases. That is functional overreaching and it is a tool rather than a mistake.

Genuine overtraining syndrome is a different and much rarer thing: months of reduced performance, persistent fatigue and mood changes that do not resolve with a week off. It mostly affects athletes training at high volume, not people doing four sessions a week.

The practical point is that a short dip after a hard block does not mean you are overtrained, and a lighter week is usually all that is needed. If a fortnight of genuine rest changes nothing, that is when to look further.

What to do when you have overdone it

  1. Take two to three easy days. Walking, mobility, nothing taxing.
  2. Return at a reduced load — roughly two thirds of what you were doing — and build back over a fortnight.
  3. Cut the number of hard sessions rather than the length of each one.
  4. Look at sleep honestly before changing anything else in the programme.

If fatigue persists after a genuinely light fortnight, that is worth a medical conversation rather than a programming one. Thyroid problems, anaemia, sleep apnoea and a number of other conditions present exactly like overtraining, and training harder will not fix any of them.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Gluteus maximus: Buttock and the tailbone; aches with long sitting and walking uphill.
  • Rectus femoris: A deep ache around and behind the kneecap, and the front of the hip bone.
  • Latissimus dorsi: Bottom tip of the shoulder blade and down the back of the arm to the ring and little fingers.
  • Upper trapezius: Up the side of the neck, behind the ear and into the temple (a question-mark shape).

When to get medical care first

  • Fatigue that persists after two weeks of genuinely light training and adequate sleep.
  • Resting heart rate that stays elevated, or unexplained breathlessness.
  • Unintentional weight loss, night sweats or fever.
  • Loss of periods in women who train, which needs medical assessment.
  • Low mood or loss of interest that extends beyond training into the rest of life.

Frequently asked questions

How many rest days do I need?

At least one complete day a week suits most people, often two. What matters more is limiting genuinely hard sessions to two or three a week.

Is it bad to train every day?

Not if most days are easy. Daily movement is fine; daily hard training is where people run into trouble.

How do I know the difference between laziness and needing rest?

Fatigue that lifts once you start moving is usually inertia. Fatigue that persists or deepens through a session, especially alongside poor sleep and flat mood, is a signal worth heeding.

Will I lose fitness taking a week easy?

No. Fitness is not lost over a week, and most people return from a lighter week performing better than they did going into it.
