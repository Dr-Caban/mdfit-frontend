A Daily Mobility Routine

What this is for

Three areas account for most of the stiffness people complain about: the hips, the upper back, and the ankles. They stiffen because of how we spend the day — seated hips, rounded upper backs, shoes and flat ground limiting ankle motion — and they are the areas whose stiffness shows up elsewhere as pain.

A stiff upper back makes the neck and shoulders work harder. Stiff hips push movement into the low back. Stiff ankles change how the knee tracks. Working on them directly is usually more productive than chasing the place that hurts.

The routine

  1. Hip flexor lunge stretch, 45 seconds each side. Half-kneeling, back knee down, squeeze the back glute and tuck the pelvis before leaning forward. The glute squeeze is the part people skip and the part that makes it work on the psoas.
  2. Open book rotation, 8 each side. On your side, knees bent, arms together in front; open the top arm across and let the chest follow while the knees stay down. For the mid-back.
  3. Cat-cow, 10 slow rounds. On hands and knees, alternating arch and round. Moves the whole spine gently through range.
  4. 90/90 hip switches, 8 each way. Seated with both knees bent at right angles, rotating from one side to the other. Hip rotation, which almost nobody trains and almost everyone loses.
  5. Knee-to-wall ankle rock, 10 each side. Foot a few inches from a wall, drive the knee forward over the toes without the heel lifting. For ankle range, which affects squatting and walking.
  6. Wall slides, 10. Back to a wall, arms up and down keeping contact. Shoulder and upper-back mobility together.

How it should feel

A stretch or a working sensation, not pain. Sharp, shooting or electrical sensations mean stop, particularly if they travel down a limb.

Move slowly. The point is control, not range, and bouncing to get further defeats the purpose. If a position cannot be held without holding your breath, make it easier.

When to do it

Whenever you will actually do it. In practice that means attaching it to something existing: after your morning coffee, before a shower, at the end of a work block.

Before training, use it as part of a warm-up rather than as long static holds — see stretching before or after exercise for why that distinction matters. Mornings can feel stiffer, especially with arthritic joints, so give yourself a few minutes of general movement first.

Why six movements and not twenty

Long routines get abandoned. The six above cover hip flexion and extension, spinal rotation and flexion-extension, hip rotation, ankle dorsiflexion and shoulder elevation — which between them account for most of the range people actually lose.

If you have time for only three on a given day, do the hip flexor stretch, the 90/90 switches and the wall slides. Those address the hip front, hip rotation and the upper back, which is where sitting does most of its damage.

Adding more movements is usually less valuable than adding strength. A hip that can reach a position but cannot hold it under load will stiffen again, which is the argument for pairing this with resistance work rather than expanding it — see mobility vs flexibility.

What it will and will not do

It will keep accessible range available and usually makes stiff mornings less stiff. Done consistently, it makes the positions needed for training and daily life more comfortable, and it gives you a daily read on what is sensitive before it becomes a problem.

It will not fix a structural restriction, resolve a disc problem, or substitute for strength work. If a specific area is persistently restricted or painful, that deserves assessment rather than more repetitions — see functional mobility for how we approach it.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Psoas: Vertical band along the low back, plus the front of the thigh and groin.
  • Iliacus: Groin, front of the thigh, and the back of the pelvis high in the glute.
  • Gluteus medius: Along the belt line, over the SI joint, the buttock and the top of the hip.
  • Upper trapezius: Up the side of the neck, behind the ear and into the temple (a question-mark shape).
  • Pectoralis minor: Front of the shoulder and chest, down the inner arm to the last three fingers.

When to get medical care first

  • Sharp, shooting or electrical pain during any movement, especially travelling into an arm or leg.
  • Numbness, tingling or weakness that appears during or after the routine.
  • Dizziness with neck movements, which should be assessed before continuing.
  • A joint that catches, locks or feels like it will give way.

Frequently asked questions

How long before I notice a difference?

Most people notice easier mornings within two to three weeks. Lasting change in range usually takes longer and is helped considerably by strength work alongside.

Is it better in the morning or evening?

Whichever you will keep doing. Evening sessions often feel better because you are already warm; morning sessions address the stiffness when it is worst.

Should I do this if something hurts?

Gentle movement is usually helpful for ordinary stiffness and soreness. Anything that produces sharp pain or symptoms down a limb should be assessed first.

Do I need to hold stretches longer than 45 seconds?

Longer holds are not clearly better for most purposes. Consistency across the week matters more than the length of any single hold.
