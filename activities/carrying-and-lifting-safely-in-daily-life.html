Lifting and Carrying in Daily Life

Why daily lifting catches people out

In a gym the load is known, the position is deliberate, and you are warmed up and paying attention. None of that applies when you reach into a car boot holding a phone.

Real-world lifts are also geometrically worse. The load is further from your body, often off to one side, sometimes at head height, frequently involving a twist. Distance from the spine is what determines how hard the back has to work, and a light object at arm's length can demand more than a heavy one held close.

So the useful skills are not about lifting heavy things. They are about managing awkward ones.

The things that actually matter

  1. Get close. The single biggest factor. Step toward the object, move obstacles, open the boot fully. Halving the distance from your body roughly halves the demand on your back.
  2. Decide before you lift. Where it is going, whether you can grip it, whether it is heavier than it looks. Most awkward lifts go wrong mid-way when the plan changes.
  3. Hinge at the hips and use your legs. Not because a rounded back is forbidden, but because the hips and legs are the stronger system and are better suited to the job.
  4. Brace before you take the weight, not after. A gentle stiffening of the trunk as you take hold.
  5. Turn with your feet. Lifting and twisting simultaneously under load is the combination most associated with trouble. Pick it up, then step round.

The back-is-fragile myth

Standard lifting advice has sometimes drifted into suggesting that bending the spine at all is dangerous. That is not well supported, and the belief itself appears to be unhelpful — people who are frightened of bending tend to move stiffly, avoid activity, and do worse.

Backs bend. They are built to. The problem is not the shape of your spine during one lift; it is doing something heavy, awkward or repetitive that you are not conditioned for.

The practical version: hinge and use your legs for heavy or awkward loads because it is more efficient, and do not panic about rounding your back to pick a sock off the floor.

Carrying well

Carrying is where a lot of daily load actually sits, and it is trainable. A one-sided carry — a suitcase, a bag of compost, a toddler — demands that the trunk resist tipping, which is exactly what the quadratus lumborum and obliques are for.

  • Walk tall rather than leaning away from the load.
  • Swap sides regularly on long carries.
  • Two lighter loads, one in each hand, are usually easier on the back than one heavy one.
  • Shoulder bags belong on alternating shoulders, not always the same one.

Training carries deliberately — see core stability for real life — transfers to this more directly than almost anything else in a gym.

Overhead, and the forgotten direction

Putting a case in an overhead locker or a box on a high shelf combines a load at arm's length with an extended back and often a rotation. For anyone with a stiff upper back or limited shoulder range, the lumbar spine ends up making up the difference.

Use a step. Bring the load close before pressing it up. If overhead reach is genuinely restricted, that is worth addressing rather than working around indefinitely — see shoulder shape and overhead lifting.

Building the capacity rather than avoiding the task

The most reliable protection is being strong enough for what life asks. Someone who deadlifts and carries regularly is at far less risk from a suitcase than someone who avoids lifting entirely and then has to.

Avoidance shrinks capacity, and shrinking capacity makes the next awkward lift riskier than the last. This is most of what we work on with people who have had a back episode: restoring confidence and capacity rather than teaching them to be careful forever.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Quadratus lumborum: Low back and flank, over the SI joint and buttock, and the point of the hip.
  • External oblique: Abdominal wall, the side of the belly and down into the groin; can feel like heartburn or a side stitch.
  • Multifidus: Deep, pinpoint pain right next to the spine, the sacrum and sometimes the tailbone.
  • Gluteus maximus: Buttock and the tailbone; aches with long sitting and walking uphill.
  • Latissimus dorsi: Bottom tip of the shoulder blade and down the back of the arm to the ring and little fingers.

When to get medical care first

  • Back pain travelling down a leg, or numbness, tingling or weakness in a leg.
  • Loss of bladder or bowel control, or numbness around the groin and inner thighs: seek emergency care.
  • A sudden sharp pain during a lift with immediate loss of strength or an inability to straighten up.
  • Back pain after a fall or significant impact, particularly if you have reduced bone density.
  • Back pain that is constant, worse at night, or comes with fever or unexplained weight loss.

Frequently asked questions

Is it bad to round my back when lifting?

For heavy or awkward loads, hinging and using your legs is more efficient and easier on the back. For light everyday objects, a rounded back is not dangerous and fearing it tends to do more harm than good.

Should I wear a back belt for household lifting?

Generally no. Belts have a role in heavy barbell lifting; routine use for daily tasks does not appear to prevent injury and can encourage reliance.

Why does my back hurt from a light box?

Usually distance and angle rather than weight. A light object held away from the body, or lifted while twisting, can demand more from the back than something heavier held close.

I've hurt my back lifting before. Should I avoid it?

Avoidance tends to reduce capacity and makes the next lift riskier. Rebuilding strength gradually is usually the better route, ideally with guidance if episodes keep recurring.
