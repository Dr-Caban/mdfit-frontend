Leg Training: Quads, Hamstrings and Glutes

The quadriceps and the knee

The quadriceps extend the knee, and the four heads sit in slightly different positions. The vastus lateralis on the outside is usually the most developed; the vastus medialis on the inside is the one that tends to be underdone and is frequently implicated in kneecap pain. The rectus femoris crosses both the hip and the knee, which is why it responds differently depending on hip position.

Squats, leg presses and split squats all train the quads. Adding a movement where the hip is extended, such as a leg extension or a reverse Nordic, loads rectus femoris differently from a squat. See patellofemoral pain for what the common knee complaint actually involves.

The hamstrings have two jobs

The hamstrings both bend the knee and extend the hip, and the two are trained by different movements. A leg curl trains knee flexion. A Romanian deadlift or a good morning trains hip extension with the hamstrings lengthened. A programme with only one of these leaves the hamstrings half-trained.

The hip extension half matters more for most people, both for performance and because loaded lengthening is what builds tolerance in a tissue that gets strained in sprinting and fast changes of direction. Biceps femoris is the one most often strained.

The glutes are three muscles

Gluteus maximus extends the hip and is trained by squats, hinges and thrusts. Gluteus medius and gluteus minimus control the hip from the side, keeping the pelvis level when you stand on one leg, and they are barely touched by bilateral barbell work.

That gap shows up as hip pain on the outside, knees that drift inward under load, and a pelvis that drops when running. Single-leg work and deliberate abduction work fill it. See outer hip pain and knee tracking.

What almost every leg programme misses

  • Single-leg work. Split squats, step-ups and single-leg hinges expose and correct side-to-side differences that a barbell hides.
  • Calves. The soleus in particular takes enormous load in walking and running and is almost never trained deliberately.
  • Adductors. Adductor magnus is a powerful hip extensor as well as an adductor and is commonly strained in sport.
  • Loaded lengthening. Training muscles in a stretched position builds tolerance that short-range work does not.

Training legs when a knee complains

Knee pain rarely requires stopping leg training. It usually requires finding the range and the loads that do not provoke symptoms, training those properly, and addressing what is underneath. Often that means partial-range squatting, more single-leg work, and deliberate attention to the muscles controlling the hip from the side.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Vastus lateralis: Down the outer thigh to the outside of the knee; can make lying on that side painful.
  • Vastus medialis: Inner knee and inside of the kneecap; can make the knee feel like it buckles.
  • Biceps femoris: Back of the knee on the outside and up the back of the thigh.
  • Gluteus maximus: Buttock and the tailbone; aches with long sitting and walking uphill.
  • Gluteus medius: Along the belt line, over the SI joint, the buttock and the top of the hip.
  • Adductor magnus: Deep inside the pelvis and groin, and the inner thigh from the groin toward the knee.

When to get medical care first

  • A knee that locks, catches or gives way, or that swells within a day of training.
  • A sudden sharp pain in the back of the thigh during a fast or heavy movement, sometimes with bruising.
  • Pain travelling down the leg below the knee, or numbness, tingling or weakness in the foot.
  • Calf pain with swelling, warmth or redness, particularly in one leg only: see a physician promptly rather than training on.
  • Hip or knee pain that is present at rest or wakes you at night.

Frequently asked questions

Are squats enough for legs?

They train the quads and glutes well but leave the hamstrings' hip extension role, the side glutes, the calves and the adductors largely untouched. Most people need more than one movement.

Why do my knees hurt after leg day?

Most often a jump in volume or load rather than a technique flaw. Kneecap pain is also commonly associated with the muscles controlling the hip from the side not contributing well.

Do I need leg curls if I do Romanian deadlifts?

They train different jobs. A Romanian deadlift trains hip extension with the hamstrings lengthened; a leg curl trains knee flexion. Including both covers the muscle properly.

Should I train legs if one side is weaker?

Yes, and single-leg work is how the difference gets addressed. Bilateral barbell work tends to hide a side-to-side difference rather than correct it.
