What tennis actually does to the body

A tennis point is a series of short sprints, hard stops, rotations and overhead reaches. Unlike running or cycling, almost nothing about it is symmetrical. Your racquet arm absorbs every ball strike, your dominant hip loads on the serve and forehand, and your calves fire on every split step.

Sarasota adds its own factor: you can play twelve months a year. Players who move here from the north often double or triple their court time without a build-up period, and the forearm and shoulder tendons are usually the first to object.

The complaints we see most in tennis players

  • Outside-of-elbow pain (tennis elbow) from gripping, late backhands and heavy or badly strung racquets.
  • Inside-of-elbow pain (golfer's elbow) in players who hit a lot of topspin or serve with a hard wrist snap.
  • Shoulder pain on the serve, often an impingement-type picture or an irritated rotator cuff.
  • Low back pain on the serve or after long matches, usually tied to a stiff hip and a trunk that rotates from the wrong place.
  • Calf and Achilles trouble, including the sudden calf strain some players call "tennis leg", and Achilles tendon pain that builds over weeks.
  • Ankle sprains and knee pain from hard courts and lateral stops.

Muscles that usually drive the pain

Tendons hurt where muscles pull. In tennis players the pulling muscles are predictable:

  • Wrist extensors: they steady the wrist in every grip and refer pain to the outside of the elbow and the back of the hand on the thumb side. The supinator often joins in when elbow pain will not settle.
  • Infraspinatus and supraspinatus: rotator cuff muscles that decelerate the serve. They refer deep into the front and side of the shoulder and make sleeping on that side hard.
  • Quadratus lumborum: the deep low-back muscle that hikes the hip; it gets overworked when the trunk side-bends on the serve instead of rotating through the hips.
  • Gastrocnemius and soleus: the calf pair behind split steps and push-offs, and the usual source of calf cramping and Achilles load.

What you can do this week

  1. Cut total court time by about a third for a week rather than stopping completely, unless pain is sharp or you cannot grip.
  2. Check your equipment: a grip that is too small, strings that are too tight or a racquet that is too head-heavy all raise forearm load.
  3. Warm up the calves and shoulders for five minutes before the first ball: calf raises, arm circles and light shadow swings.
  4. After play, do slow eccentric wrist extension with a light weight (lower slowly over three to four seconds) if your elbow is the issue, staying under a mild discomfort level.

If something makes the pain sharper or lingers more than a day afterward, back off.

When to get checked

Most tennis aches respond to a few weeks of smart load management. Book an assessment if elbow or shoulder pain has lasted more than three weeks despite easing off, if you have lost grip strength or cannot lift the arm overhead, if calf pain came on suddenly with a pop, or if pain is now showing up off the court in everyday tasks like lifting a kettle or reaching behind you.

When to get medical care first

  • A sudden pop in the calf or Achilles followed by inability to push off the foot.
  • Inability to lift the arm at all after a fall or an awkward serve.
  • Numbness, tingling or weakness in the hand that does not change with position.
  • A visible deformity, rapid swelling or a joint that locks or gives way.
  • Calf pain with swelling, warmth and redness, which needs urgent medical evaluation to rule out a blood clot.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to stop playing tennis while my elbow heals?

Usually not completely. Reducing volume, fixing grip size and string tension, and loading the tendon with slow eccentric work often lets players keep hitting at a lower level while the tissue recovers. Sharp pain or loss of grip strength is different and should be assessed.

Is tennis elbow really an elbow problem?

The pain is at the elbow, but the load comes from the wrist extensors and often from a shoulder or trunk that is not rotating well, so the forearm overworks. That is why we assess the whole chain.

What causes shoulder pain on the serve?

Commonly an irritated rotator cuff or an impingement pattern from a shoulder blade that does not rotate upward well. Tight chest muscles and a stiff upper back are frequent contributors, and both respond well to release and corrective work.
