Why Squat Depth Differs From Person to Person

Two different limits, often confused

Depth is limited by two separate things and it helps to keep them apart.

The first is leverage. Femur length decides how far your hips travel back and how much you lean, which changes what depth looks like from the side. That is covered in femur length and the squat.

The second is joint range: how much the ball of your femur can actually rotate and tuck into the socket before bone meets bone. That is what this page is about, and it is the one people most often try to fix with stretching when the limit is structural.

When the hip reaches the end of its available flexion, the pelvis has to tuck underneath, which is what lifters call a butt wink. Pushed hard and repeatedly under load, that is where lower back complaints tend to come from.

What actually varies between hips

  • Socket depth. A deeper socket holds the joint more securely and gives less range. A shallower one gives more. Neither is a fault.
  • Socket orientation. Sockets face slightly differently from person to person, and even between a person's own two sides. That is why many lifters are more comfortable with one foot turned out further than the other.
  • The angle of the femoral neck. The short section of bone between the shaft and the ball sits at an angle that varies, and it changes which stance lets the hip reach its deepest position.
  • Bony shape at the rim. Extra bone at the socket rim or at the head-neck junction can limit flexion earlier and is the usual background to pinching at depth.

None of that is visible from outside, and none of it is something you can measure at home. What you can do is find your range by testing it.

Finding your own end range

  1. Test unloaded first. On hands and knees, rock your hips back towards your heels with a flat back. The point where your low back starts to round is roughly where your hip range runs out in that position.
  2. Change the stance and test again. Repeat with the knees wider and the feet turned out. Most people find noticeably more range in one configuration. That configuration is a clue to your squat stance.
  3. Film a bodyweight squat from the side. Watch for the moment your pelvis tucks under. That is your depth with that stance, with no bar involved.
  4. Retest with heels elevated. If depth improves markedly, ankle range was part of the limit rather than the hip. See ankle mobility and the squat.

Whatever that process gives you is your working depth. It may be below parallel, at parallel, or above it, and all three are legitimate starting points.

Why chasing someone else's depth backfires

Forcing depth past where the hip runs out does not lengthen the joint. It borrows the extra range from the lumbar spine, which tucks under at the exact moment the load is heaviest and the position weakest. Done occasionally, most backs cope. Done every session for years, it is a reasonable explanation for the low back complaints that follow.

The other cost is the one people notice less. Grinding into a pinching bottom position irritates the front of the hip, and that nagging pinch at depth is a common reason lifters quietly stop squatting. The tissue involved is often the psoas and iliacus, which complicates matters because muscular tightness and bony limitation feel similar from the inside.

Telling them apart is a job for an assessment rather than a guess, because the treatment is completely different. One responds to hands-on work and mobility. The other needs the position changed.

What mobility work can and cannot change

It can change a surprising amount, and it has limits that are worth being honest about.

  • It can help when the limit is muscular guarding, when the adductors or hip flexors are short and tight, when ankle range is restricting how far the knee can travel, or when you have range you are not used to using.
  • It cannot help when the limit is the shape of the socket, the angle of the femoral neck, or extra bone at the joint rim.

A fair test is a few weeks of consistent work. If depth improves, the limit was soft tissue. If it does not move at all, the limit is probably structural and the useful response is to build the squat around the range you have rather than keep pushing at it.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Psoas: Vertical band along the low back, plus the front of the thigh and groin.
  • Iliacus: Groin, front of the thigh, and the back of the pelvis high in the glute.
  • Adductor magnus: Deep inside the pelvis and groin, and the inner thigh from the groin toward the knee.
  • Gluteus maximus: Buttock and the tailbone; aches with long sitting and walking uphill.
  • Gluteus medius: Along the belt line, over the SI joint, the buttock and the top of the hip.
  • Soleus: Heel and Achilles tendon, lower calf; can contribute to plantar fasciitis.

When to get medical care first

  • Sharp pinching in the front of the hip or groin at depth that does not settle when you reduce the range.
  • A hip that clicks painfully, catches or gives way under load.
  • Low back pain during squatting, especially with pain or numbness travelling down a leg.
  • Numbness in the groin or inner thighs, or any loss of bowel or bladder control, which needs emergency care.
  • Groin pain that is worsening week on week despite reducing depth and load.

Frequently asked questions

Does everyone need to squat below parallel?

No. Depth that your hip can reach without the pelvis tucking is the useful target, and that differs between people. Partial-range squatting with good control builds strength perfectly well.

Is butt wink always a problem?

A small amount at the very bottom is common and often harmless. It becomes worth addressing when it happens early in the descent, when it is large, or when loaded squatting leaves your back sore.

Can I improve my hip range?

Often yes, if the limit is muscular or you simply have not trained that range. If the limit is the shape of the joint, mobility work will not change it, and a few weeks of consistent effort will usually tell you which you have.

Why can I squat deeper with my toes turned out?

Turning the feet out rotates the femur in the socket, which for many hip shapes moves the bone away from the rim and gives more flexion before anything bumps. It is a normal and useful adjustment.
