Back Training: Lats, Traps and Rhomboids

Four jobs, not one

The latissimus dorsi pulls the arm down and back towards the body. It is the muscle behind pull-ups and pulldowns, and it also has a role in stabilising the trunk under load, which is why strong lats help in a deadlift.

The middle trapezius and the rhomboids pull the shoulder blades together. The lower trapezius pulls them down and rotates them upward, which is what lets the arm travel overhead cleanly. And the upper trapezius, which almost nobody needs to train harder, is usually already doing too much.

Vertical and horizontal pulling are different

Pulldowns and pull-ups load the lats through a vertical path. Rows load the lats differently and recruit the mid-back far more. Programmes that contain only one leave a gap, and the gap is almost always horizontal pulling, which is also the direction that balances benching.

If you have to choose, rowing does more for the typical lifter's problems than pulldowns do. See the barbell row. Both is better.

Why your upper traps take over

A very common pattern is a lifter whose top of shoulder and neck are permanently tight, who has strong-looking upper traps and a weak mid and lower back. The upper trapezius is compensating for muscles that are not contributing, and it refers pain up the side of the neck and into the head, which is where some tension headaches come from.

Training the lower trapezius and rhomboids directly, with light weights and deliberate control rather than heavy shrugging, is usually more effective than stretching the upper traps repeatedly. See upper back and rhomboid pain and tension headache.

Technique points that matter

  • Lead with the elbow, not the hand. Thinking about driving the elbow back recruits the back rather than the arms.
  • Let the shoulder blade move. Allowing it to travel at both ends of the movement trains the muscles that control it, which locking it rigid does not.
  • Control the lowering. The lengthened portion of a pull is where a lot of the stimulus sits, and dropping the weight wastes it.
  • Do not shrug every row. If your traps are doing the work, lighten it and slow down.

Grip, straps and what the forearms limit

On heavy pulling the grip often fails before the back does, which quietly caps how much back training you actually get. The finger flexors and wrist flexors are working hard on every row and pull-up, and in a long session they are the tissue that accumulates the most fatigue.

Straps are a reasonable tool for the heaviest sets, because the point of a row is to train the back. Using them on everything means the grip never develops, so a common compromise is to strap the top sets and leave the rest unstrapped. If your forearms ache between sessions, or you are getting pain at the forearm or on the outside of the elbow, that is worth attention rather than working through: see tennis elbow, which is common in people doing high volumes of pulling.

Grip width and hand position also change what gets worked. A wider overhand grip emphasises the upper back and rear shoulder; a narrower or underhand grip brings the lats and biceps in more. Varying it across a programme is generally more useful than finding one grip and never changing it.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Latissimus dorsi: Bottom tip of the shoulder blade and down the back of the arm to the ring and little fingers.
  • Middle trapezius: Burning ache between the shoulder blade and the spine, and the top of the shoulder.
  • Lower trapezius: Up the back of the neck to the base of the skull, the top of the shoulder, and along the inner shoulder blade.
  • Rhomboid major: Between the shoulder blade and the spine.
  • Upper trapezius: Up the side of the neck, behind the ear and into the temple (a question-mark shape).
  • Teres major: Back of the shoulder and the back of the upper arm, sometimes the back of the forearm.

When to get medical care first

  • Pain, numbness or tingling travelling down an arm into the hand.
  • Neck pain with headache, visual change or dizziness.
  • A sudden sharp pain in the back or armpit during a heavy pull with loss of strength.
  • Shoulder or neck pain that is present at rest or wakes you at night.
  • Back pain that travels down a leg, or weakness in a leg or foot.

Frequently asked questions

Are pull-ups enough for back training?

They train the lats well but do little for the mid-back muscles that pull the shoulder blades together. Most people need horizontal pulling as well, and it is usually the more useful of the two.

Why are my upper traps always tight?

Often because they are compensating for a weak lower trapezius and rhomboids. Strengthening those directly tends to help more than repeatedly stretching the upper traps.

Should I train traps with shrugs?

Most recreational lifters already have overactive upper traps and would benefit more from lower trapezius and rhomboid work than from heavy shrugging.

How much back work compared to chest?

Matching pulling sets to pressing sets is a reasonable target. Lifters who press far more than they pull are the ones who tend to develop shoulder complaints over time.
