Training Around an Injury Instead of Stopping

Why stopping entirely usually backfires

Detraining starts quickly. Strength, muscle and tolerance to load all decline during a layoff, so a lifter who stops for six weeks returns weaker, and the return itself becomes the next risk. Meanwhile the original problem has often not improved, because most tissue problems respond better to controlled loading than to avoidance.

There are genuine exceptions. A fresh tear, a suspected fracture, anything with neurological symptoms, or anything a physician has told you to rest, is not something to train around. The judgement of which category you are in is the part worth getting help with.

The modifications that usually work

Four levers cover most situations, and they are worth trying in this order:

  1. Reduce the range. Pin squats, board presses, rack pulls. Often the painful part of a lift is a specific portion of the range, not all of it.
  2. Change the implement. Dumbbells, a trap bar, a safety squat bar, a landmine. Each changes the joint angles enough to be a different experience.
  3. Change the load and the tempo. Lighter weight moved deliberately often loads the tissue usefully without provoking symptoms.
  4. Train the other side and the rest of the body. Training one limb produces some benefit in the untrained one, and the rest of your body does not need to detrain because a shoulder is sore.

The rule for deciding what is allowed

A widely used approach is to allow mild discomfort that stays mild during the set, does not build across the session, and has settled by the next day. Pain that climbs rep by rep, lingers for days, or changes how you move is a signal to back off further.

Write it down. People are poor at remembering whether last Tuesday was better or worse, and a one-line record of load and symptoms turns a vague impression into something you can act on. It also makes the first clinical appointment far more productive.

Working around it is not treating it

This is the part lifters get wrong. Successfully modifying your training means you can keep going, which removes the urgency, which is why people arrive at a clinic having worked around something for eighteen months. The modification buys time to address the cause; it is not itself the fix.

In parallel with the modified training, something should be changing: the restricted tissue being released, the position being rebuilt, the weak link being strengthened. If nothing is changing, you are not training around an injury, you are accommodating it indefinitely.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Gluteus maximus: Buttock and the tailbone; aches with long sitting and walking uphill.
  • Quadratus lumborum: Low back and flank, over the SI joint and buttock, and the point of the hip.
  • Supraspinatus: Deep ache at the top and side of the shoulder that can run down the outside of the arm to the elbow.
  • Vastus lateralis: Down the outer thigh to the outside of the knee; can make lying on that side painful.
  • Latissimus dorsi: Bottom tip of the shoulder blade and down the back of the arm to the ring and little fingers.
  • Multifidus: Deep, pinpoint pain right next to the spine, the sacrum and sometimes the tailbone.

When to get medical care first

  • Pain that travels down an arm or leg, or any numbness, tingling or weakness.
  • A joint that locks, gives way, or swells rapidly.
  • A sudden tearing sensation during a lift, particularly with bruising afterwards.
  • Pain that is present at rest, at night, or that is getting worse week by week despite reducing load.
  • Fever, unexplained weight loss, or pain that is unrelated to activity: see a physician rather than adjusting your programme.

Frequently asked questions

Should I train through pain?

Mild discomfort that stays mild, does not build during the session and has settled by the next day is usually acceptable. Pain that climbs, lingers for days or changes how you move means backing off further.

How long before I can go back to normal training?

That depends entirely on what the problem is, which is why an assessment is worth more than a timeline from a page. Returning when symptoms allow, rather than on a fixed date, is the general principle.

Is it bad to only train one side?

No. Training one limb produces some carryover to the untrained side and preserves far more than stopping altogether. It is a standard approach while one side is unavailable.

Can I keep training if I have been told to rest?

Follow the advice you have been given by the clinician who examined you. This page is about the common situation where nobody has examined you yet, not about overriding someone who has.
