The Barbell Back Squat: Form, Faults and Pain

A cue that fixes one lifter's knee pain creates another lifter's back pain, because the two are built differently. What is commonly taught, and why it varies

Most coaching for the back squat agrees on a short list: a braced trunk, a bar held tight against the back, a controlled descent, knees tracking over the feet, and hips and shoulders rising together out of the bottom. Those are reasonable starting points. What they do not settle is how wide your feet should be, how far forward your torso should lean, or how deep you should go.

Those three are decided largely by your skeleton. The length of your thigh bone relative to your shin and torso changes how far your hips must travel backwards to keep the bar over mid-foot, and the shape and orientation of your hip sockets changes where your legs run out of room. Two people can both be squatting well and look nothing alike from the side. If you have been told repeatedly to stay more upright and it has never worked, read long femurs and the squat and why squat depth differs before changing anything else.

High bar and low bar are different lifts

The bar position changes the lift more than most people expect. A high bar squat, with the bar resting on the upper traps, keeps the torso more upright and asks more of the quadriceps. A low bar squat, with the bar sitting across the rear deltoids and the spine of the shoulder blade, pushes the hips further back, increases forward lean, and shifts work towards the hips and gluteus maximus.

Neither is correct in the abstract. Low bar tends to let people move more weight and demands more shoulder and wrist range to hold the bar in place. High bar is usually kinder to an irritable shoulder and harder on the knees. If your shoulders or wrists complain every session, the bar position is worth examining before you blame the shoulder itself.

The faults that actually matter

Plenty of things look untidy and cause no trouble. A few are worth correcting:

  • The hips shooting up first. The hips rise, the torso folds, and the bar path drifts forward. The load transfers from the legs to the low back, often because the lifter is out of position at the bottom or the weight is simply too heavy.
  • Losing the brace at the bottom. If the trunk softens as you change direction, the spine takes a load it was holding safely a moment earlier.
  • Knees caving under heavy loads in a way that is not there when light. This is usually a strength and control issue rather than a structural one. The usual knees out cue is right some of the time and unhelpful the rest.
  • Heels lifting. Often ankle range, not weakness. Ankle mobility and the squat covers what actually changes this and what does not.

Where squatting shows up as pain

Low back pain after squatting is the most common complaint, usually at the muscle right beside the spine, where quadratus lumborum, multifidus and the long back muscles work hard to keep the trunk from folding. Soreness that settles in a day or two is ordinary. Pain that arrives the moment you unrack the bar, or that travels down a leg, is not.

Knee pain is usually felt below the kneecap or around its edges. It frequently follows a jump in volume or a change in depth rather than anything wrong with the technique. Hip pinching at the front of the hip in the bottom position is often a range issue; forcing through it repeatedly is what turns it into a problem.

What to change this week

Change one thing at a time, and give it two or three sessions before judging it. Useful experiments: widen or narrow your stance by a few centimetres and see what the bottom position feels like; try the other bar position for a training block; drop the weight enough that you can hold your brace all the way through and rebuild from there.

If a position hurts, the answer is rarely to push through it and rarely to abandon squatting altogether. Most people need a version of the squat they can load without symptoms while the underlying restriction is worked on. A front squat, a box squat, or a slightly elevated heel are all ways of keeping training while something is addressed.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Gluteus maximus: Buttock and the tailbone; aches with long sitting and walking uphill.
  • Vastus lateralis: Down the outer thigh to the outside of the knee; can make lying on that side painful.
  • Vastus medialis: Inner knee and inside of the kneecap; can make the knee feel like it buckles.
  • Quadratus lumborum: Low back and flank, over the SI joint and buttock, and the point of the hip.
  • Multifidus: Deep, pinpoint pain right next to the spine, the sacrum and sometimes the tailbone.
  • Adductor magnus: Deep inside the pelvis and groin, and the inner thigh from the groin toward the knee.

When to get medical care first

  • Back pain that travels down a leg, or numbness, tingling or weakness in the foot after squatting.
  • A sharp, specific pain at the moment of a lift that stops you mid-rep, rather than soreness that builds afterwards.
  • A knee that locks, gives way, or swells within a day of training.
  • Pain that is present at rest and at night, not only under load.
  • Any loss of bladder or bowel control, or numbness around the groin: this needs emergency care the same day.

Frequently asked questions

How deep should I squat?

Deep enough that your hips and spine stay in a position you control, which varies by person. Hip shape and thigh bone length set a genuine limit for some people, and chasing someone else's depth is a common cause of back pain.

Should I use a belt?

A belt gives your trunk something to brace against and can let you lift more. It does not replace the bracing itself, and it is not a fix for a position you cannot hold without it.

My knees hurt after squatting. Should I stop?

Usually not, but something should change. Knee pain after squatting more often follows a jump in load or volume than a flaw in technique, and it is worth having looked at before it becomes the reason you stop training.

Is the back squat bad for your back?

Squatting loads the back, which is not the same as harming it. Backs tolerate load well when the position is one you can hold; the trouble comes from loading a position you cannot.
