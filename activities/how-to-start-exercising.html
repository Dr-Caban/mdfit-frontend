Why restarts fail

The pattern is consistent. An enthusiastic first week, soreness that makes week two unpleasant, a missed session, a missed week, and a quiet conclusion that exercise is not for you.

Nobody fails because the first week was too easy. The useful question in week one is not how much can I do, but how little can I do and still call it a session — because the thing being built is not fitness yet. It is the habit that fitness attaches to.

Week one

Two sessions. Twenty minutes each. That is the whole plan.

  1. Session A: ten minutes of easy walking, then one set each of a sit-to-stand from a chair, a wall push-up, and a standing row with a band or a towel against a door. Eight to ten repetitions each.
  2. Session B: the same, with a second set of each if the first felt manageable.

If that feels insultingly easy, good. The soreness arrives two days later and will be more than you expect from work that felt trivial. That is normal — see muscle soreness and DOMS.

Weeks two to six

Add one thing at a time, roughly one change a week:

  • A third session.
  • A set to each exercise, up to three.
  • Five to ten minutes to the walking.
  • Progress each movement: sit-to-stand becomes a bodyweight squat, wall push-up moves to a counter, band row gets heavier.

Never add volume and difficulty in the same week. It is the fastest route to the shin, knee or shoulder complaint that ends the attempt.

What to include, and why strength is not optional

Cardio alone does not maintain muscle or bone, and both decline with age without deliberate loading. If you have three sessions a week, two with strength work and one of cardio serves most people better than three walks.

Cover the basic patterns: a squat or sit-to-stand, a hinge, a push, a pull, and something single-leg. See single-leg strength and balance, which matters more than most beginners realise.

For cardio, start easy and conversational — cardio for beginners covers the build-up.

What normal feels like

Muscle soreness a day or two after a new session, spread through the muscle, easing as you move, settling in two to four days. Breathlessness that improves noticeably within a few weeks. Days where it feels harder for no obvious reason.

What is not normal: sharp pain during a movement, pain that is pinpoint over bone, joint swelling, or pain that worsens session on session. Those are reasons to get something looked at rather than to push on.

If something already hurts

Plenty of people start exercising precisely because something hurts, and existing pain is not a reason to wait. It is a reason to start differently — around the problem rather than into it, with the painful area loaded in a way it tolerates.

That is most of what we do here: finding the version of the work that does not provoke the problem, so fitness improves while the cause is addressed. See clinical exercise physiology, and if you are not sure where to start, the pain by location pages are a reasonable place to look first.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Gluteus maximus: Buttock and the tailbone; aches with long sitting and walking uphill.
  • Rectus femoris: A deep ache around and behind the kneecap, and the front of the hip bone.
  • Latissimus dorsi: Bottom tip of the shoulder blade and down the back of the arm to the ring and little fingers.
  • Pectoralis major lower head: Front of the chest, down the inner arm to the inner elbow and the ring and little fingers.
  • Gluteus medius: Along the belt line, over the SI joint, the buttock and the top of the hip.

When to get medical care first

  • Chest pressure or pain during exertion, or pain spreading to the jaw, neck or arm: stop and seek emergency care.
  • Fainting, near-fainting, or a racing or irregular heartbeat.
  • Breathlessness far out of proportion to the effort, or new breathlessness lying flat at night.
  • Sharp joint pain during a movement, or a joint that swells afterwards.
  • Pain that is pinpoint over bone and worsens with each session.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to see a doctor before starting?

For gentle walking and light strength work, most people do not. If you have a heart condition, chest symptoms on exertion, or have been inactive for a very long time, a conversation first is sensible.

How long before I see results?

Strength improves within a few weeks, largely through better coordination at first. Visible changes take longer and depend on a great deal besides training.

What if I can only manage ten minutes?

Then ten minutes is the plan. Consistency at a small dose beats an ambitious plan you abandon, and ten minutes grows naturally once it is established.

Should I join a gym?

Not necessarily. A band, a chair and somewhere to walk covers the first couple of months perfectly well.
