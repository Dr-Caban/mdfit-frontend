The Sumo Deadlift: Setup, Form and Faults

Sumo is not the easy deadlift, despite the argument that follows it around. It shortens the distance the bar travels and moves work towards the hips and quadriceps, and in exchange it demands hip range that not everybody has. Which style suits you is decided largely by how you are built.

What actually changes

Taking a wide stance and gripping inside the legs does two things. It lowers the hips and raises the starting position of the bar relative to your body, shortening the pull. And it turns the lift into something closer to a squat pattern, with more demand on the quadriceps, the gluteus medius and the adductors, and less on the long muscles of the back.

The shorter range is why sumo is sometimes called easier. The counterweight is that the start position is far harder to reach. You have to get your hips close to the bar with your shins near vertical, and that requires hip range in a direction many people do not train.

Stance width is a structural question

There is no correct sumo stance. The depth and angle of your hip sockets decide how far your legs can travel out and turn before the bones run out of room, and that varies a lot between people. A stance that feels powerful for one lifter will feel like a pinch at the front of the hip for another.

The practical approach is to test it without load. Take a wide stance, turn the feet out, and sink down as if setting up. If you find a position where your hips feel open and you can keep your chest up, that is your starting point. If every wide position produces a pinch at the front of the hip, sumo may not be your lift, and that is structural rather than a failure of mobility work. The bar that will not leave the floor

The characteristic sumo failure is the bar not breaking the floor at all, which is different from the conventional lifter's mid-shin grind. It usually comes from one of three things:

  • Hips too high at the start. The lift becomes a stiff-legged pull from a wide stance, and the leg drive never happens.
  • Not pulling the slack out. Sumo punishes a loose start more than conventional does, because there is less room to recover.
  • Knees drifting in or the feet rolling. If the knees do not stay over the feet, the drive has nowhere to go.

Pulling against the bar to create tension before the weight moves, and spreading the floor with the feet, fix a surprising amount of this.

Where sumo shows up as pain

Inner thigh and groin complaints are the most distinctive, since the wide stance loads adductor magnus and the shorter adductors hard. Pain felt at the inner thigh or the groin after a heavy sumo session is common; pain that persists between sessions or sharpens when you bring the legs together is worth assessing.

Front-of-hip pinching at the bottom is the other frequent complaint, and it is usually a range limit rather than an injury. Repeatedly forcing past it is what turns it into something that needs treating. Low back pain is less common than in conventional pulling but not absent, particularly when the hips rise early.

Choosing between the two

Most people are modestly better at one style, and the difference is smaller than the internet suggests. Longer arms and a longer torso tend to favour conventional; shorter arms, longer legs and open hips tend to favour sumo. The honest way to decide is to train both for a block and compare, rather than reasoning from body type alone.

If you are not competing, there is no obligation to pick. Many lifters run one as the main lift and the other as an accessory, which also spreads the load across different tissues. Muscles behind this pain

  • Adductor magnus: Deep inside the pelvis and groin, and the inner thigh from the groin toward the knee.
  • Adductor longus & brevis: Deep groin, the inner thigh and down to the inner knee and top of the shin.
  • Gluteus medius: Along the belt line, over the SI joint, the buttock and the top of the hip.
  • Gluteus maximus: Buttock and the tailbone; aches with long sitting and walking uphill.
  • Vastus lateralis: Down the outer thigh to the outside of the knee; can make lying on that side painful.
  • Quadratus lumborum: Low back and flank, over the SI joint and buttock, and the point of the hip.

When to get medical care first

  • Groin or inner thigh pain that is sharp rather than sore, especially if it arrived suddenly during a lift.
  • Front of hip pain that clicks, catches or gives way, rather than simply feeling tight.
  • Pain travelling down the back of the leg below the knee, or numbness or weakness in the foot.
  • Hip pain that is present at rest or at night and not only when training.
  • Any loss of bladder or bowel control, or numbness around the groin: this is an emergency, call 911.

Frequently asked questions

Is sumo deadlifting cheating?

No. It is a recognised competition style with its own demands. It shortens the range of motion and makes the hip position much harder to reach, so it trades one difficulty for another.

How wide should my stance be?

Wide enough that your shins are close to vertical and your hips feel open, which depends on the shape of your hip sockets. There is no universal width, and copying someone else's stance is a common source of hip pinching.

My groin is sore after sumo. Is that normal?

Some inner thigh soreness is expected because the adductors work hard in a wide stance. Sharp pain, pain that arrived suddenly mid-lift, or soreness that does not settle between sessions is worth having looked at.

Can I switch to sumo because my back hurts?

Sometimes it helps, because it reduces the demand on the long back muscles. It is not automatic, and swapping lifts without finding out why the back is irritable tends to postpone the problem rather than solve it.
