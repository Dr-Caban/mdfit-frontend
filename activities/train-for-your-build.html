Train for Your Build, Not the Textbook

Most lifting advice is written for an average body that very few people have. Why the same cue helps one lifter and hurts another

A barbell lift is a balance problem. The bar has to stay over roughly the middle of the foot, and your skeleton solves that with the levers it has. Change the lever lengths and the solution changes with them.

That is why a lifter is told to keep their chest up for five years without it working, while the person next to them does it effortlessly. It is why one lifter feels squats in their low back and another in their knees, doing what looks like the same lift. And it is why "knees behind your toes" is sensible for some people and almost impossible for others.

None of this means technique does not matter. The bar drifting forward is still a fault. A back changing shape under load is still worth fixing. But the shape of a correct lift is individual, and the first job is working out which shape is yours.

The principles underneath all of it

  1. Balance is not negotiable, position is. The bar over the midfoot is physics. Stance, bar height, grip and footwear are all adjustable to serve it.
  2. Change one thing at a time. Widen the stance or elevate the heels, not both, then judge over two or three sessions.
  3. Film from the side. You cannot feel your own torso angle accurately. A phone at hip height settles arguments that cues never will.
  4. Bone does not change, range often does. Ankle and hip range respond to work. Femur length does not. Spend your effort accordingly.
  5. Pain is a signal to assess, not a tax on your build. Long femurs do not mean your back has to hurt. Short femurs do not mean your knees have to ache.

When it stops being a technique question

Setup changes solve a lot. They do not solve everything, and there is a point where the limit is tissue rather than geometry.

If the same spot hurts in every session for more than a couple of weeks, if you have quietly dropped a lift or changed your technique to avoid a feeling, if pain travels into a limb or comes with pins and needles, or if a single rep produced a pop, swelling or sudden weakness, that is worth an assessment rather than another setup experiment. Muscles behind this pain

  • Gluteus maximus: Buttock and the tailbone; aches with long sitting and walking uphill.
  • Vastus lateralis: Down the outer thigh to the outside of the knee; can make lying on that side painful.
  • Latissimus dorsi: Bottom tip of the shoulder blade and down the back of the arm to the ring and little fingers.
  • Psoas: Vertical band along the low back, plus the front of the thigh and groin.
  • Soleus: Heel and Achilles tendon, lower calf; can contribute to plantar fasciitis.
  • Lower trapezius: Up the back of the neck to the base of the skull, the top of the shoulder, and along the inner shoulder blade.

When to get medical care first

  • A pop or tearing sensation during a lift, followed by weakness, swelling or bruising.
  • Pain, numbness or tingling travelling down an arm or leg rather than staying at the joint.
  • Numbness in the groin or inner thighs, or any loss of bowel or bladder control, which needs emergency care.
  • A joint that locks, catches, gives way or swells within hours of training.
  • The same spot hurting in every session for more than two weeks despite reducing load.

Frequently asked questions

Is this an excuse for bad technique?

No. Some faults are real whatever your build, including the bar drifting forward of the midfoot and a back that changes shape under load. Proportion explains the shape of a good lift, not the absence of standards.

Where should I start?

With the squat, because proportion shows up there most clearly. Measure your femur length, film a set from the side, and read the page that matches what you see.

Do I need a coach to work this out?

A phone and a tape measure get most people a long way. An assessment is worth it when something hurts, when progress has stalled for months, or when you want the joint range and the training plan looked at together.

Does MD Fit only work with lifters?

No. The same reasoning applies to golf, pickleball, running and everyday movement. Lifting is simply where the levers are easiest to see.
