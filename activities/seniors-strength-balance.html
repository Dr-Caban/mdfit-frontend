Why strength and balance come first

Muscle and bone both respond to load at any age, and both quietly shrink when load disappears. The spiral is familiar: a sore knee or hip leads to doing less, doing less leads to weaker legs and a less reliable sense of balance, and that leads to the fear of falling, which leads to doing even less. At MD Fit we treat the pain, but we also break the spiral by rebuilding the capacity underneath it. Our balance and fall prevention page covers the balance side in detail; this page is about how it fits together with strength and bone health.

Common complaints in older adults

  • Knee pain on stairs and getting out of a chair, often labeled knee osteoarthritis, and often with weak quads and glutes doing less than their share.
  • Hip and groin stiffness, including hip osteoarthritis, that shortens stride and makes putting on socks a project.
  • Low back ache after standing or walking, frequently from tight hip flexors and sleepy glutes rather than the spine alone. See low back pain.
  • Shoulder pain reaching overhead or behind the back, often a rotator cuff issue or a frozen shoulder.
  • Unsteadiness on uneven ground, in the dark or when turning quickly.
  • Feeling stiff all over in the morning, which is usually the sum of many small losses of range rather than one condition.

Three pillars: strength, balance, bone

Strength. The legs and hips matter most: sitting to standing, stepping up, carrying groceries. Progressive resistance, meaning the load goes up as you adapt, is what maintains muscle.

Balance. Balance is a skill, not a fixed trait, and it is trained by practicing it safely: standing on one leg at the kitchen counter, walking heel to toe, turning the head while walking. Hip strength is a big part of it, which is why the two pillars overlap.

Bone. Bone responds to impact and to muscle pulling on it. Weight-bearing exercise, resistance work and, where appropriate, carefully introduced impact like brisk walking or step-ups all contribute. Medication and nutrition decisions belong with your physician; our role is the loading side.

The muscles that matter most

  • Gluteus medius: holds the pelvis level on one leg. Weakness here shows up as a hip that drops or a waddle, and it refers pain along the belt line and over the SI joint. Our lateral hip guide covers it.
  • Gluteus maximus: the engine of standing up, climbing stairs and walking uphill. It goes quiet with long sitting.
  • Vastus medialis: the inner quad that guides the kneecap and keeps the knee from feeling like it buckles. See the quads guide.
  • Soleus: the deep calf muscle that holds you upright and absorbs each step. Calf strength is strongly tied to steady walking.
  • Tibialis anterior: lifts the foot and toes so you clear the rug, the curb and the dock plank.

Safe things to start this week

  1. Sit to stand from a firm chair, five to ten times, two or three times a day, using hands only as needed.
  2. Stand on one foot at the kitchen counter while the coffee brews, holding on lightly, and switch feet.
  3. Heel raises holding the counter, slow up and slow down.
  4. Walk daily, even ten minutes, on a surface you trust, and add a little distance each week.

These are general suggestions for healthy older adults. If you have a heart condition, recent surgery or a history of falls, check with your physician first or let us assess you before starting.

When to get checked

Any fall, even one without injury, is a reason to be assessed. So is new unsteadiness, pain that has changed how you walk, pain that keeps you from getting up from a chair without help, or a joint that has steadily lost range over months. Catching these patterns early is far easier than catching them after a fracture.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Gluteus medius: Along the belt line, over the SI joint, the buttock and the top of the hip.
  • Gluteus maximus: Buttock and the tailbone; aches with long sitting and walking uphill.
  • Vastus medialis: Inner knee and inside of the kneecap; can make the knee feel like it buckles.
  • Soleus: Heel and Achilles tendon, lower calf; can contribute to plantar fasciitis.
  • Tibialis anterior: Front of the shin, front of the ankle and the big toe.

When to get medical care first

  • A fall with a blow to the head, especially if you take blood thinners.
  • Sudden inability to bear weight on a leg, or a leg that looks shortened or turned out after a fall.
  • New dizziness, confusion, slurred speech or weakness on one side of the body: call 911.
  • Back pain with numbness in the groin or loss of bowel or bladder control.
  • Unexplained weight loss, fever or night pain alongside new joint or back pain.

Frequently asked questions

Am I too old to build muscle?

No. Muscle responds to progressive resistance at every age. The gains may come more slowly and the starting load is lower, but the process is the same.

Is it safe to exercise with arthritis in my knees or hips?

In most cases appropriate, pain-respecting strengthening is one of the most helpful things for an arthritic joint. The assessment determines what is appropriate for you.

I have osteoporosis. What should I avoid?

Generally, loaded forward bending and twisting of the spine are approached with care, and impact is introduced gradually if at all. Dr. Jared will coordinate with your physician's guidance and build the program around it.
