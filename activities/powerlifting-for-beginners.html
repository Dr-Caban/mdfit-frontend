Powerlifting for Beginners: Starting Without Breaking

★★★★★ 5.0 · 89 Google reviews

Powerlifting is three lifts and a simple idea: add weight over time. The difficulty is that strength improves faster than tendons, joints and connective tissue adapt, so the first year is where most avoidable injuries happen. Starting well is mostly about restraint.

What the three lifts ask of you

The squat, the bench press and the deadlift each demand a position before they demand strength. The squat needs hips and ankles that let you get down without the spine folding. The bench needs shoulders that tolerate the bottom position. The deadlift needs a start position your back can hold.

If any of those positions is unavailable, the honest answer is to train a version you can hold while the range is worked on, rather than to force the standard version with willpower. Start with the back squat, the bench press and the conventional deadlift pages for what each one actually requires.

Strength outruns tissue

This is the single most useful thing a new lifter can understand. Your nervous system gets better at producing force within weeks. Tendons, ligaments and the structures around joints change far more slowly. For a few months you will be able to lift more than your connective tissue has adapted to carry, and this is the window in which things go wrong.

The practical consequence is that progress should be slower than it feels possible. Adding a small amount regularly and holding weights for longer than you want to is not timidity, it is the thing that lets you still be lifting in five years.

The mistakes that fill clinic appointments

  • Testing a maximum too often. Maximum attempts are a skill and a stressor. Doing them monthly in the first year adds risk without adding much.
  • Adding weight every single session regardless. Linear progression works until it does not, and continuing past that point is where positions break down.
  • No pulling volume. A programme of squat, bench and deadlift with nothing else builds a pressing imbalance quickly. See the barbell row.
  • Training through sharp pain. Soreness and pain are different. Sharp pain during a rep is information, not weakness.
  • Copying a cue from a lifter built differently. Much coaching advice is specific to the body it was developed on, which is what training for your build covers.

Soreness, pain, and telling them apart

Ordinary training soreness is diffuse, appears a day or two later, affects the muscle belly, eases as you warm up and settles within a few days. It is a normal part of training.

Pain worth paying attention to is sharp, specific, present during the lift rather than after it, located at a joint or tendon rather than in the middle of a muscle, and gets worse with repeated exposure rather than better. Pain that is there at rest or wakes you at night belongs in a different category again, and nothing on this page should be read as a reason to delay getting that looked at.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Gluteus maximus: Buttock and the tailbone; aches with long sitting and walking uphill.
  • Quadratus lumborum: Low back and flank, over the SI joint and buttock, and the point of the hip.
  • Multifidus: Deep, pinpoint pain right next to the spine, the sacrum and sometimes the tailbone.
  • Pectoralis major lower head: Front of the chest, down the inner arm to the inner elbow and the ring and little fingers.
  • Latissimus dorsi: Bottom tip of the shoulder blade and down the back of the arm to the ring and little fingers.
  • Vastus lateralis: Down the outer thigh to the outside of the knee; can make lying on that side painful.

When to get medical care first

  • Sharp pain during a lift that stops you mid-rep, as distinct from soreness afterwards.
  • Pain that is present at rest or wakes you at night.
  • Numbness, tingling or weakness anywhere in an arm or leg after training.
  • A joint that swells, locks or gives way within a day of a session.
  • Chest pain, pressure, shortness of breath or light-headedness during lifting: stop and call 911.

Frequently asked questions

How often should I test a one-rep max?

Rarely in the first year. Maximum attempts are a skill of their own and a significant stressor, and most progress comes from consistent work at submaximal weights.

Do I need a coach to start powerlifting?

Not strictly, but having someone watch your lifts early saves a lot of time. Video of your own sets from the side is the next best thing and shows most of the common faults.

Should I wear a belt as a beginner?

A belt gives your trunk something to brace against. Learning to brace without one first means the belt adds to a skill you have rather than replacing one you do not.

How fast should I add weight?

Slower than feels possible. Strength improves faster than tendons and joints adapt, so the first year is where adding too quickly causes most of the avoidable problems.
