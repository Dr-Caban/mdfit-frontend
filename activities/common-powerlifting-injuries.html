Common Powerlifting Injuries and What They Mean

The low back, and why it leads the list

Low back complaints are the most common thing in this sport, mostly because all three lifts load the trunk and most lifters train all three. The usual presentation is soreness beside the spine or across the SI joint that builds across a training block.

Most of it is muscular and responds well to load management and addressing whichever position is breaking down. What needs separating out is pain that travels below the knee, or that comes with numbness, tingling or weakness, which points to the nerve rather than the muscle. See low back pain, herniated disc and sciatica, and read lifting with back pain.

Shoulders, and the bench press

The front of the shoulder is the second most common complaint, and benching is usually involved. The typical pattern is an ache at the front of the shoulder that appears during or after pressing, often in someone who presses far more than they pull.

The structures that get blamed are the rotator cuff and the long head of the biceps, and they are often genuinely irritated, but the cause is usually upstream: shoulder blades that do not stay set, a shortened pectoralis minor, and grip width or touch point that puts the shoulder at the edge of its range every rep. See shoulder impingement and biceps tendinopathy.

Hips, knees and the squat

Front-of-hip pinching at the bottom of a squat is common and is frequently a range issue rather than an injury, though repeatedly forcing past it is what turns it into one. Knee pain tends to appear below the kneecap and more often follows a jump in volume than a flaw in technique. See patellar tendinopathy and hip pain.

Adductor and groin complaints belong mostly to sumo pullers and wide-stance squatters, and hamstring complaints near the sitting bone mostly to conventional deadlifters. Both tend to build gradually, which is why they are ignored until they are not.

The dramatic ones, and how rare they are

Pec tears during heavy benching and biceps tears during mixed-grip deadlifting are the injuries everyone has seen a video of. They do happen, they are serious, and they share a signature: a sudden tearing sensation at a specific moment, immediate loss of strength, and bruising over the following days.

If that happens, it is not something to train around or wait out. It needs assessing promptly, because the timing of what is done next can matter. Nothing else on this page is in the same category.

Telling soreness from a problem

Ordinary soreness is spread across a muscle, appears a day or two after training, eases as you warm up, and settles within a few days. A problem worth attention is specific, located at a joint or tendon, present during the lift rather than after, and gets worse with repeated exposure.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Quadratus lumborum: Low back and flank, over the SI joint and buttock, and the point of the hip.
  • Multifidus: Deep, pinpoint pain right next to the spine, the sacrum and sometimes the tailbone.
  • Pectoralis major lower head: Front of the chest, down the inner arm to the inner elbow and the ring and little fingers.
  • Supraspinatus: Deep ache at the top and side of the shoulder that can run down the outside of the arm to the elbow.
  • Semitendinosus & semimembranosus: The buttock crease, back of the thigh and the inner back of the knee, sometimes the inner calf.
  • Adductor longus & brevis: Deep groin, the inner thigh and down to the inner knee and top of the shin.

When to get medical care first

  • A sudden tearing sensation during a lift with immediate loss of strength, often followed by bruising: get this assessed promptly.
  • Pain travelling below the knee or down an arm, or numbness, tingling or weakness.
  • Any loss of bladder or bowel control, or numbness around the groin: this is an emergency, call 911.
  • A joint that locks, gives way or swells rapidly after a session.
  • Pain present at rest or at night, or that is worsening week by week despite reduced load.

Frequently asked questions

Is powerlifting dangerous?

Injury rates in powerlifting are generally comparable with other recreational sports rather than exceptional. Most problems are gradual overload rather than dramatic failures, which also means most are avoidable.

What is the most common powerlifting injury?

Low back complaints, largely because all three lifts load the trunk. Shoulder complaints related to benching are the next most common.

How do I know if it is just soreness?

Soreness is diffuse, appears a day or two later, eases with warming up and settles within days. Pain that is sharp, specific to a joint or tendon, present during the lift, or worsening with repetition is different.

Can I keep lifting with an injury?

Often yes, in a modified form, but that is a judgement made after assessment rather than from a page. Sudden tearing injuries and anything with nerve symptoms are exceptions that need looking at first.
