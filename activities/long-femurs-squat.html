Long Femurs and the Squat

★★★★★ 5.0 · 89 Google reviews

If your squat looks like a good morning no matter how often you are told to keep your chest up, the problem is usually not discipline or core strength. It is proportion. A long thigh bone relative to your shin and torso changes where your hips have to travel, and that changes what a correct squat looks like on you.

What is actually happening when you tip forward

A barbell squat has one rule that cannot be negotiated: the bar has to stay roughly over the middle of your foot. Drift forward or back and you either fall over or dump the bar. Everything else about your technique is your body solving that problem with the levers it has.

Your femur is the long bone from hip to knee. When you bend your knees, your hips travel backwards, and the longer the femur, the further back they go for the same amount of knee bend. Now the bar is behind your midfoot and you would fall over. Your body fixes it the only way it can, by leaning the torso forward until the bar is back over the middle of the foot.

That forward lean is not a fault. It is the correct answer to the physics of your skeleton. A lifter with long femurs and a short torso will always show more forward angle than a lifter with short femurs, both of them squatting well. Side-by-side, one looks textbook and one looks like a hinge, and the one that looks like a hinge is often the one doing it right for their build.

How to tell this is you

Some reliable signs, none of which need a tape measure:

  • Your torso angle in a squat is noticeably more forward than most people in the room, even with a light bar.
  • You have been cued to keep your chest up for years and it has never stuck.
  • Sitting in a normal chair, your knees sit noticeably higher than your hips.
  • Your hips shoot up out of the bottom before the bar moves, so the lift finishes as a hinge.
  • Front squats and goblet squats feel dramatically harder than back squats relative to what you can handle.

If you want to put numbers on it, measuring your femur length at home takes about two minutes and tells you how your thigh compares to your shin and torso.

The sticking point long-femur lifters share

Watch a long-femur lifter miss a squat and the shape of the miss is nearly always the same. Out of the bottom, the hips rise first and fast, the torso angles further forward, and the bar stalls somewhere around or just above parallel. The lift turns into a stiff-legged good morning with a loaded bar.

What has happened is a handoff that did not work. Coming out of the hole the quads should drive the knees back while the hips and back hold the torso angle steady. If the quads cannot produce enough at that depth, or the hips are simply much stronger than the knees, the body uses what it has: the hips extend early and leave the torso to carry the rest.

That is why long-femur lifters so often feel squats in their low back the next day rather than their legs. The lift is being completed by the posterior chain and the spinal erectors because the position demands it. It is also why the fix is rarely bracing harder.

What to change on your next session

  1. Widen your stance and turn your toes out. This is the single biggest lever. A wider stance with the feet angled out lets the hips open to the side instead of travelling straight back, which reduces how far back they go and lets you stay more upright. Start a hand-width wider than you currently stand and add toe-out until the bottom feels like it has room.
  2. Move the bar down. A low-bar position, sitting on the rear deltoids rather than the upper traps, shifts the bar back over the midfoot and lets your torso angle forward without the bar drifting. For long femurs this often feels instantly more stable.
  3. Elevate your heels. Lifting shoes or a small wedge lets the knees travel further forward, which brings the hips back under you and the torso back up. It is the fastest change to test and the effect is usually obvious on the first rep.
  4. Use a box to find your depth. Squatting to a box at a consistent height takes the guesswork out and stops you chasing a depth that your hips do not have. Set it where your back stays neutral, not where someone else's parallel is.
  5. Pick a bar that suits the lever. A safety squat bar or a front squat changes the demand so the torso stays more upright. Many long-femur lifters find a safety squat bar is the one they progress on fastest.

The cues that work against you

Most general coaching cues were written for average proportions, and some of them actively fight a long-femur build.

  • "Chest up, stay upright." With long femurs and a narrow stance this is physically unavailable. Forcing it either stops you reaching depth or pulls the bar forward of the midfoot.
  • "Knees behind your toes." This pushes the hips even further back and increases the forward lean, which is the opposite of what you need.
  • "Everyone should squat to depth." Depth is set by your hip anatomy, not by a standard. Why squat depth differs covers what actually limits it.
  • "Narrow stance is the real squat." There is no real squat. There is the stance that lets your skeleton put the bar over the midfoot.

Where it shows up as pain

A long-femur lifter grinding against the wrong setup usually feels it in one of three places. The low back, because the erectors and quadratus lumborum are holding a long forward lever rep after rep, which can present as low back pain or stiffness beside the spine. The front of the hip, where the psoas and iliacus get pinched at a depth the joint does not have room for. Or the adductors and groin from a wide stance taken on suddenly without building up to it.

None of that means stop squatting. It usually means the setup is asking for a position your body cannot hold yet, and both the setup and the tissue need attention. Soreness that settles in two or three days is normal adaptation. The same spot hurting every session for a fortnight is worth getting assessed.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Gluteus maximus: Buttock and the tailbone; aches with long sitting and walking uphill.
  • Vastus lateralis: Down the outer thigh to the outside of the knee; can make lying on that side painful.
  • Psoas: Vertical band along the low back, plus the front of the thigh and groin.
  • Quadratus lumborum: Low back and flank, over the SI joint and buttock, and the point of the hip.
  • Adductor magnus: Deep inside the pelvis and groin, and the inner thigh from the groin toward the knee.
  • Rectus femoris: A deep ache around and behind the kneecap, and the front of the hip bone.

When to get medical care first

  • Sharp low back pain during a rep, especially with pain, numbness or tingling travelling down a leg.
  • Numbness in the groin or inner thighs, or any loss of bowel or bladder control, which needs emergency care.
  • A pop or tearing sensation in the hip, groin or hamstring followed by weakness or bruising.
  • A hip or knee that locks, catches or gives way under load.
  • Front-of-hip pain that is sharp and pinching at depth and does not settle when you reduce the range.

Frequently asked questions

Do long femurs mean I should not squat?

No. It means the squat that suits you looks different from the one in the coaching video. Stance width, bar position, heel elevation and bar choice are all adjustable, and most long-femur lifters squat well once the setup matches their build.

Is more forward lean dangerous?

Forward lean itself is not the problem. A back that changes shape under load, or a bar that drifts forward of the midfoot, is what tends to cause trouble. A well-braced forward-angled squat is a normal lift for many builds.

Should I just switch to front squats?

Front squats suit many long-femur lifters because the front rack forces a more upright torso. They are a good tool rather than a replacement, and a safety squat bar often gives similar benefit with heavier loads.

Will stretching make me squat more upright?

Mobility work can help if ankles or hips are genuinely restricted, and it will not change bone length. The usual gain comes from the setup changes, with mobility work supporting them.

How do I know if my femurs are actually long?

Compare your thigh to your shin and torso rather than guessing from height. Our guide to measuring femur length at home walks through it with a tape measure and a chair.
