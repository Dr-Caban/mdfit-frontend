The Overhead Press: Form and Shoulder Pain

Overhead range is earned, not assumed

To press a bar directly overhead, the arm needs to reach near-vertical, and the shoulder blade has to rotate upward to let it. If either is missing, the body finds the range somewhere else, almost always by extending the low back. That is why people finish an overhead press with the ribs flared and the bar still slightly forward.

A quick check: stand with your back against a wall, feet a little way out, low back flat against the wall. Raise your arms overhead keeping the back flat. If your arms will not reach the wall without your back lifting off it, you do not currently have the range to press straight overhead, and pressing anyway will load the low back every rep.

Bar path and the face problem

The bar has to travel around the head, which is the awkward geometry at the heart of this lift. The usual sequence is to move the head back slightly as the bar passes the face, then push the head forward through the window as the bar clears, finishing with the bar over the middle of the foot and the biceps near the ears.

Common faults: pressing the bar forward in an arc to avoid the face, which keeps the load out in front and loads the shoulders and low back; and failing to push the head through at the top, which leaves the bar in front of the body where it is far harder to hold.

Why it hurts, and who it hurts

Overhead pressing commonly irritates the top of the shoulder and the outside of the shoulder or upper arm. The tissues involved are often the supraspinatus and the infraspinatus, with the upper trapezius taking over when the shoulder blade does not rotate well.

The shape of the shoulder itself varies between people, and so does the room available as the arm travels overhead. That is part of why two lifters doing the same programme have different experiences. Shoulder shape and overhead lifting covers it, and shoulder impingement covers what the pattern can become.

Good alternatives while you build the range

Abandoning overhead work entirely is usually the wrong call, because overhead strength is worth having and tends to deteriorate without it. Better options while range is being built:

  • Landmine press. The angled path asks for less overhead range while still loading the pattern.
  • Incline press. A middle ground, adjustable to whatever angle is currently comfortable.
  • Half-kneeling single-arm press. Removes the ability to arch the low back, which exposes how much range is genuinely there.
  • Dumbbells over a barbell. Lets each shoulder find its own path rather than being locked to a fixed bar.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Supraspinatus: Deep ache at the top and side of the shoulder that can run down the outside of the arm to the elbow.
  • Infraspinatus: Deep in the front of the shoulder joint, down the front and outside of the arm.
  • Anterior deltoid: Front of the shoulder and down the front of the upper arm, close to the muscle itself.
  • Upper trapezius: Up the side of the neck, behind the ear and into the temple (a question-mark shape).
  • Lower trapezius: Up the back of the neck to the base of the skull, the top of the shoulder, and along the inner shoulder blade.
  • Serratus anterior: Side of the rib cage and the bottom tip of the shoulder blade; a side "stitch" that hurts with deep breaths.

When to get medical care first

  • Shoulder pain that continues at rest or wakes you at night.
  • Weakness lifting the arm to the side, or an arm that cannot be held out against gentle resistance.
  • A sudden sharp pain with a loss of strength during a press, rather than gradual soreness.
  • Numbness or tingling travelling down the arm or into the hand.
  • Any chest pain, pressure, shortness of breath or light-headedness during lifting: stop and call 911.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my low back arch when I press overhead?

Usually because the arms cannot reach vertical, so the body borrows the range from the spine. Testing your overhead range against a wall with the low back flat shows whether that is what is happening.

Is the overhead press bad for shoulders?

Pressing overhead is not inherently harmful, but it requires range that many adults have lost. Pressing into a range you do not have is what tends to cause trouble, not the lift itself.

Should I use dumbbells instead of a barbell?

Dumbbells let each shoulder find its own path, which many people find more comfortable. A barbell is not better or worse, it is simply less forgiving of asymmetry.

Can I press overhead with a sore shoulder?

Often a modified version is fine, such as a landmine or incline press at an angle that does not provoke symptoms. Pushing through overhead pain in the hope it settles is the approach that tends to prolong it.
