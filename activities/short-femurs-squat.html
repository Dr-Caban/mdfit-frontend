Short Femurs and the Squat

★★★★★ 5.0 · 89 Google reviews

Short femurs are usually described as the lucky build for squatting, and in one sense they are: depth comes easily and you look upright without trying. The trade-off is that the work moves to your knees, and most of the coaching advice you will hear was written for the opposite problem.

Why your squat looks upright without effort

A squat is your skeleton keeping the bar over the middle of your foot. The femur, the bone from hip to knee, decides how far your hips have to travel back to let your knees bend. A short femur means less backward hip travel, which means less forward lean is needed to keep the bar balanced.

So you stand up, squat down, and the torso stays close to vertical. People tell you your form is excellent. What is really happening is that your proportions are doing the work that a long-femur lifter has to solve with stance width and heel wedges.

The flip side is where the load goes. Less hip travel and more knee travel means the knees move further forward over the feet, and the quadriceps and the patellar tendon take a larger share of the lift. Your back feels fine. Your knees are the ones having the conversation.

How to tell this is you

  • You reach full depth comfortably without heel elevation and without thinking about it.
  • Your torso stays close to upright even in a narrow stance.
  • Sitting in a normal chair, your knees sit level with or below your hips.
  • Front squats and goblet squats feel natural rather than punishing.
  • Your knees travel well forward of your toes at the bottom, and always have.
  • Squat soreness shows up in the front of your thighs and around the kneecap, not your low back.

If you would like the numbers rather than the impression, measuring your femur length at home compares your thigh with your shin and torso in a couple of minutes.

Your sticking point is a different one

Long-femur lifters miss squats by folding forward and turning the lift into a good morning. Short-femur lifters rarely do that. The miss looks different: the torso stays upright, the depth is fine, and the bar simply stops somewhere in the first third of the ascent while the legs run out of drive.

That is a quad-strength and knee-extension problem rather than a position problem. The lift is already efficient; it is asking a lot of the knee extensors through a long range. When short-femur lifters stall, more mobility work and more stance fiddling usually change nothing, because the position was never the limit.

The other common complaint is accumulated knee load. Deep, upright, knees-forward squatting repeated for years is a lot of work for the patellar tendon and the quadriceps attachment, and it is a common background to patellar tendinopathy and patellofemoral pain.

What to change on your next session

  1. Stop forcing a wide stance. Wide stance with toes out is a long-femur fix. If you have short femurs it can crowd your hips at the bottom and give you groin or front-of-hip pinching for no benefit. Use the stance where depth feels open.
  2. A high bar position is fine. The low-bar squat exists largely to help lifters who need to angle forward. If your torso is already upright, high bar usually feels better and loads the quads the way your build is set up for.
  3. Stop adding heel lift by default. Lifting shoes push the knees further forward. If your knees already travel a long way forward, a flatter shoe can be the more comfortable choice. Test both and judge by how your knees feel the next day, not by what the rack looks like.
  4. Train the knee extensors directly. If your miss is a drive problem rather than a position problem, that is what needs loading. Tempo squats, pause squats at the sticking depth, split squats and leg extensions all target it more precisely than another mobility circuit.
  5. Manage total knee volume. Deep squats, lunges, step-ups and box jumps all tax the same tissue. Spreading them across the week matters more for you than for most lifters.

The cues that work against you

Much of the standard squat coaching exists to stop long-femur lifters tipping over. Applied to short femurs it creates problems that were not there.

  • "Sit back, push your hips back." This pulls you out of the balanced position your proportions give you for free and can shift load to the back unnecessarily.
  • "Keep your knees behind your toes." For a short-femur lifter this usually means refusing to squat at all. Knees travelling forward of the toes is normal and not inherently harmful.
  • "Everyone needs heel elevation." Many short-femur lifters are more comfortable flat.
  • "Go wide, toes out." A long-femur solution. Worth testing, but not the default.

Where it shows up as pain

The pattern is front-of-knee rather than low-back. Pain or ache around and under the kneecap during or after squatting, stairs and sitting for long periods points towards patellofemoral pain. Pain focused on the tendon just below the kneecap, stiff at the start of a session and warming up as you go, is the pattern of patellar tendinopathy.

The muscles usually involved are the vastus lateralis, rectus femoris and vastus medialis, which all refer into the front of the knee and thigh and can make a knee feel like a joint problem when the tissue is muscular. A knee that swells, locks or gives way is a different matter and should be assessed before loading it again.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Vastus medialis: Inner knee and inside of the kneecap; can make the knee feel like it buckles.
  • Vastus lateralis: Down the outer thigh to the outside of the knee; can make lying on that side painful.
  • Rectus femoris: A deep ache around and behind the kneecap, and the front of the hip bone.
  • Gluteus maximus: Buttock and the tailbone; aches with long sitting and walking uphill.
  • Soleus: Heel and Achilles tendon, lower calf; can contribute to plantar fasciitis.
  • Vastus intermedius: Front of the mid-thigh, worse climbing stairs or standing up from a chair.

When to get medical care first

  • A knee that locks, catches or gives way under load.
  • Knee swelling that appears within hours of training.
  • A pop or tearing sensation at the knee followed by weakness or an inability to straighten the leg.
  • Front-of-knee pain that is getting worse week on week despite reducing load.
  • Pain, numbness or tingling travelling down the leg rather than staying at the knee.

Frequently asked questions

Are short femurs an advantage for squatting?

For reaching depth with an upright torso, usually yes. The trade-off is that more of the load goes through the knees, so knee tolerance tends to be the limit rather than position.

Should my knees go past my toes?

For most short-femur lifters they will, and that is normal. Knees travelling forward is how the ankle and knee share a squat. Pain, not knee position, is the signal worth paying attention to.

Why do my knees ache after squatting when my form is good?

Good position and high tissue load are not the same thing. An efficient, deep, upright squat still asks a great deal of the quadriceps and patellar tendon, and volume often matters more than technique here.

Do I still need mobility work?

Ankle and hip range still matter for the quality of the bottom position, but if your sticking point is drive out of the hole, strength work on the knee extensors is usually the more useful investment.
