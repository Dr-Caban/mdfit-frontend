Why rowing is worth considering

Rowing is non-impact, uses a large amount of muscle at once, and scales from very easy to extremely hard without changing the movement. For someone with knee osteoarthritis, a sore achilles or a foot that dislikes the treadmill, it keeps serious aerobic work available.

It also loads the back of the body — the lats, the rhomboids, the glutes — which is a useful counterweight for people who spend their days at a desk and whose training is front-heavy.

The sequence, and why order matters

A rowing stroke is legs, then back, then arms on the drive, and the exact reverse coming back: arms, then back, then legs. Almost every problem comes from blurring that order.

  1. Catch. Shins vertical, arms straight, shoulders in front of hips, back firm rather than rounded.
  2. Drive. Push the floor away with the legs first. The handle moves because the seat moves, not because the arms pull.
  3. Finish. Legs flat, a slight lean back, handle drawn to the lower ribs.
  4. Recovery. Hands away first, then hinge forward from the hips, then let the knees bend.

The most common fault is opening the back early — leaning back while the legs are still bending — which loads the lumbar spine at the worst moment and is the main reason people finish a row with a sore back.

Damper setting is not resistance

The lever on the flywheel changes how the air feels, not how hard the machine is. A high damper feels like heavy, slow rowing; a low one feels light and fast. Neither is harder in itself — how hard you pull determines that.

Most people are better served somewhere in the middle. Setting it at maximum because it feels more serious tends to produce a slow, grinding stroke that loads the low back and the lats heavily and teaches poor sequencing.

Programming it

Rowing suits both ends of the spectrum. Twenty to forty minutes at a conversational effort is excellent steady work; short hard intervals on a rower are brutal and effective.

A caution for beginners: because rowing is non-impact, people often do far more of it than their back and grip are conditioned for. Start with ten to fifteen minutes and build. Technique degrades with fatigue, and a long first session usually ends with the last ten minutes rowed badly.

Who should be careful

Rowing involves repeated spinal flexion under load. If you have a diagnosed disc problem, significant low back pain, or reduced bone density, that is worth a conversation before making the rower your main tool. It is not automatically off-limits, but technique and dose matter more than usual.

See herniated disc, low back pain and osteoporosis and exercise. If the hip hinge itself is the problem rather than the machine, that is usually a fixable thing rather than a reason to stop.

Where rowers get sore

Low back is the common complaint, and it usually means sequencing rather than the machine. Forearms and grip fatigue early for most beginners and improve quickly. Some people feel the rhomboids and between the shoulder blades, particularly if they pull high to the chest instead of to the lower ribs.

Knee discomfort at the catch is often a depth issue — being pulled into more knee bend than is comfortable — and adjusting foot plate height usually fixes it.

Muscles behind this pain

  • Latissimus dorsi: Bottom tip of the shoulder blade and down the back of the arm to the ring and little fingers.
  • Rhomboid major: Between the shoulder blade and the spine.
  • Gluteus maximus: Buttock and the tailbone; aches with long sitting and walking uphill.
  • Biceps femoris: Back of the knee on the outside and up the back of the thigh.
  • Quadratus lumborum: Low back and flank, over the SI joint and buttock, and the point of the hip.

When to get medical care first

  • Low back pain that appears during rowing and persists after you stop, especially with leg pain, numbness or weakness.
  • Chest pressure or pain, or pain spreading to the jaw, neck or arm: stop and seek emergency care.
  • Sudden sharp pain in the back or hamstring at the catch, which suggests a strain rather than fatigue.
  • Numbness or tingling in the hands that does not settle within minutes of finishing.

Frequently asked questions

What damper setting should I use?

Somewhere in the middle for most people. It changes the feel of the stroke, not the difficulty; how hard you pull sets that.

Is rowing bad for your back?

Rowing with poor sequencing can be. Rowing with the legs driving first and the back staying firm is well tolerated by most people, including many with a history of back pain.

How long should a rowing session be?

Ten to fifteen minutes to start, building toward twenty to forty for steady work. Stop when technique degrades rather than when the clock says so.

Is rowing a good substitute for running?

For cardiovascular purposes it is an excellent substitute and far kinder to joints. It does not provide the impact loading that benefits bone, so pair it with resistance training.
