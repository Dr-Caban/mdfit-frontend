Golf back pain: it's usually a hip problem

By Dr. Jared Caban, MD, MSc, CEP · Published 2026-09-08

The golf swing in two sentences

The golf swing is a rotational power move. The hips are supposed to turn, the thoracic spine (upper back) is supposed to turn, and the low back is supposed to stay relatively neutral. When hips or thoracic spine don't turn, the low back turns for them, and the low back wasn't built for that.

The two hip problems

Problem one: not enough internal rotation in the trail hip (the right hip for a right-handed golfer). You can't finish your backswing without the back having to twist. Problem two: not enough glute strength on the lead side. You can't rotate powerfully through impact without the back snapping into extension.

The thoracic spine piece

If your mid-back is stiff from sitting at a desk all day, the shoulder turn has to come from the low back. We check thoracic rotation on every golfer. A quick test: sit tall in a chair, cross your arms over your chest, turn as far as you can each way. If one side is noticeably worse than the other, that's the side where the back is doing the work for the mid-back.

What we actually fix

Hands-on soft-tissue work to open up hip rotation. Targeted thoracic mobility drills (open books, seated rotations, cat-cow with reach). Specific glute strengthening (single-leg deadlifts, cable rotations, step-up variations). A short daily routine in the MD Fit app you can do before golf and on off days.

What to expect

Most golfers notice their swing feels easier within 3–4 weeks of consistent work. Driving distance often improves slightly because the lead hip can rotate through impact. More importantly, the low back pain that was happening by hole 12 starts happening less often, then stops.

Should I keep playing while we work?

Usually yes, with changes. Fewer holes, a shorter range session, and a specific warm-up. We give you clear limits each week so you can stay on the course while the hips and back rebuild.
