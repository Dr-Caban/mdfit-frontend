Low back that catches when you roll over in bed? Meet the quadratus lumborum

By Dr. Jared Caban, MD, MSc, CEP · Published 2026-11-10

Two complaints reach our Sarasota clinic every week in almost the same words: "my back catches when I roll over in bed" and "I can't stand up straight after I sit." Both point to a deep muscle beside the spine most people have never heard of, the quadratus lumborum, or QL.

What the QL is and why it gets blamed so often

The quadratus lumborum runs from the back of your hip bone up to your lowest rib and the sides of the lumbar vertebrae. It hikes the hip, side-bends the trunk, and quietly steadies the pelvis every time you take a step, cough or roll over. It also anchors the twelfth rib while you breathe.

That job description explains the symptom pattern. Rolling over in bed asks the QL to lift one side of the pelvis against the mattress. Standing up after sitting asks it to let go after an hour of being shortened. Coughing or sneezing yanks on the rib it holds. When the muscle is irritated, each of those moments produces a sharp catch instead of a smooth movement.

We rarely treat the QL as the root cause. It is usually the muscle doing extra work because the gluteus medius and deep core are not pulling their weight. Release the QL alone and it tightens back up within a week or two.

A self-check you can do today

These are the QL self-check questions from our free app. They are educational, not a diagnosis, but several yes answers mean the QL deserves a close look.

  • Do you get a sharp, catching pain in your low back when you roll over in bed or get out of a car?
  • Does coughing, sneezing or a deep breath send a sharp pain into your low back or flank?
  • Is it hard to stand up completely straight after sitting for a while?
  • Does the pain feel very deep, right next to the spine, but also spread over the top of the buttock or hip?
  • Do you hurt standing or walking, but feel better leaning on a shopping cart or countertop?

Then try the hip hike test. Stand in front of a mirror and lift one side of your pelvis straight up toward your ribs without bending the knee or leaning your torso. Sharp pain on the lifting side, or a side that will not move smoothly, is a common QL finding.

How QL pain is different from a disc or the SI joint

The QL refers pain over the top of the buttock and into the SI joint area, so it is regularly mistaken for an SI joint problem, and it can feel scary enough that people assume a disc. A few patterns help us sort them out:

  • QL pain is usually one-sided, worse with side-bending and rolling, and eases with leaning support.
  • Disc-related pain more often travels below the knee and may come with numbness or weakness. See our sciatica page if that sounds familiar.
  • True SI joint pain sits lower, on the dimple at the back of the pelvis, and is provoked more by single-leg loading than by coughing.

Kidney pain can also sit in the flank. Back pain with fever, nausea or changes in urination is a medical visit, not a stretching program.

Why it keeps coming back

The causes we see most often are long sitting in a slumped position, lifting with a twist, standing for hours on hard or uneven surfaces, a leg length difference, and weak glutes or core. Snowbirds who drive down from the Northeast folded into a car seat for two days are a reliable November arrival. Only one of those is an injury; the rest are habits, and habits can be changed.

What to do this week

This is the order we use with patients and the order the free MD Fit app follows.

  1. Calm it. Lie on your back with knees bent and place a lacrosse ball in the soft tissue between your lowest rib and hip bone, just to the side of the spine. Rock gently for two to three minutes per side, once a day. Follow with the Dynamic Child's Pose video, rocking side to side for ten slow breaths.
  2. Restore movement. Use the Sit Turn Lean stretch: sit tall, reach one arm overhead and lean away, keeping both sit bones down. Three holds of thirty seconds per side. Add the Quadruped T-Spine Rotation so the mid-back shares the rotation the low back has been doing alone.
  3. Teach the core to help. The Core Brace Intro Seated video teaches you to brace without holding your breath. Do it daily.
  4. Strengthen the neighbours. Once the sharp catches have settled, add side planks from the knees, single-leg glute bridges and a one-sided farmer carry every other day. The carry trains the QL to stabilise against a load instead of guarding.
  5. Change the triggers. Break up sitting every thirty minutes, hinge at the hips to stand, and sleep on your side with a pillow between the knees.

What we do in the clinic when self-care stalls

If a week or two of this does not move the needle, the QL may need hands-on release, or the real driver may be somewhere else. Muscles behind this pain

  • Quadratus lumborum: Low back and flank, over the SI joint and buttock, and the point of the hip.
  • Gluteus medius: Along the belt line, over the SI joint, the buttock and the top of the hip.
  • Iliocostalis: Low back and buttock from the lumbar part; the lower shoulder blade and even the belly from the thoracic part.
  • Multifidus: Deep, pinpoint pain right next to the spine, the sacrum and sometimes the tailbone.

Red flags: see a physician, not a stretch

  • Loss of bowel or bladder control, or numbness in the groin or inner thighs. This is a medical emergency; go to the emergency room.
  • Back pain with fever, chills, nausea or changes in urination, which can point to a kidney problem rather than a muscle.
  • Numbness or weakness in one or both legs that is severe or getting worse.
  • Constant, severe back pain that no position relieves, or night pain with unexplained weight loss.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my back catch when I roll over in bed?

Rolling over asks the quadratus lumborum to lift one side of the pelvis against the mattress. When the muscle is irritated, that movement produces a sharp catch instead of a smooth roll.

Is QL pain the same as SI joint pain?

Not the same, but easily confused, because the QL refers pain over the top of the buttock near the SI joint. QL pain is provoked by side-bending, coughing and rolling; SI joint pain more by single-leg loading.

Should I stretch or strengthen my QL?

Usually both, in that order. Calm and lengthen the muscle first, then strengthen the glutes and core so the QL stops overworking.

How long does QL pain take to settle?

Acute flares often calm within days of gentle release and movement. Lasting change takes consistent strengthening over several weeks. If nothing improves after one to two weeks, book an assessment.
