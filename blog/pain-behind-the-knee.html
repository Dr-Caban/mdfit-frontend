Pain behind the knee: is it the popliteus, the gastrocnemius or the hamstring?

By Dr. Jared Caban, MD, MSc, CEP · Published 2026-11-10

The back of the knee is a crowded place. Three muscles cross it, a nerve and artery run through it, and a few structures that need a doctor rather than a lacrosse ball live there too. When someone points to the hollow behind their knee in our Sarasota clinic, the first job is to work out which one is talking.

Three muscles, three patterns

Muscle pain behind the knee almost always traces to one of three sources, each with a recognisable signature.

  • The popliteus is a small muscle that unlocks the knee from a fully straight position. Its pain sits precisely in the centre of the hollow, is worst walking or running downhill, and shows up when you squat low or first bend a straightened knee. Some also feel the knee is unstable when twisting.
  • The gastrocnemius is the big upper calf muscle, and both of its heads start just above the knee. Its pain is a deep, cramp-like ache in the upper calf that can reach up into the back of the knee and down into the arch. Night cramps and tightness pushing off on stairs are typical.
  • The biceps femoris, the outer hamstring, attaches on the outside of the back of the knee. Its pain is on the outer corner of the hollow and up the back-outside of the thigh, brought on by sprinting, long strides and long sitting.

Our quick rule: dead centre and worse downhill points to popliteus; lower and cramping points to the calf; outer corner and worse with stride length points to the outer hamstring.

A self-check for each one

These screens come from the muscle reports in our free app. They are educational, not a diagnosis; if any red flag below applies, skip them and get checked.

  1. Resisted knee bend. Sit in a chair, cross your good ankle over the front of the painful leg's ankle, and try to bend the painful knee against that resistance. Pain in the centre or outer back of the knee points to the popliteus or hamstring tendons.
  2. Single-leg calf raise. Hold a wall and rise onto the ball of one foot. Sharp calf pain, weakness, or an ache spreading into the back of the knee points to the gastrocnemius.
  3. Straight-leg raise with the foot turned in. Lie on your back and lift the straight leg with the foot turned slightly inward. A pull along the outer back of the thigh that arrives earlier than on the other side points to the biceps femoris.
  4. The downhill question. If going down a hill or stairs is clearly your worst moment, weight the popliteus more heavily.

Why these muscles get irritated

The popliteus is overloaded by downhill running, a sudden twist with the foot planted, feet that roll inward, and returning to activity too soon after another knee injury. The gastrocnemius objects to sudden jumps in mileage, hill and jump training, dehydration, high heels, and rapid switches to minimalist shoes. The outer hamstring is strained by sprinting without warming up, long sitting with bent knees, and weak glutes that leave the hamstrings extending the hip alone.

The shared thread is weak hips and glutes. That is why our plan always ends at the hip, even when the pain is at the knee. Our knee pain page explains the whole chain.

Things behind the knee that are not muscles

A Baker's cyst, a fluid-filled sac behind the knee, often sits alongside arthritis or a meniscus problem and produces fullness more than sharp pain. The back horn of the meniscus and the posterior cruciate ligament can hurt in the same spot, usually after a twist or fall. A blood clot in the calf vein is the one we never want to miss, because it can look like a calf strain and is a medical emergency. If the self-checks do not reproduce your pain, if there is visible swelling, or if the pain does not change with movement, see a clinician first. Hamstring problems higher up are covered on our hamstring strain page.

What to do this week

Once you have a likely muscle, follow its calm, restore and strengthen sequence in the app. In broad strokes:

  • Popliteus: gentle knee circles and pressure release behind the knee; knee neuroglides and heel slides; then banded terminal knee extensions and wall sits. Shorten your stride on hills and land with a slightly bent knee.
  • Gastrocnemius: foam roll the calf and stretch it with a straight knee; ankle neuroglides; then eccentric calf drops and toe walks. Hydrate and change shoes gradually. The calf guide has the full progression.
  • Biceps femoris: outer hamstring release and hip neuroglides; pike forward fold and dowel hip hinge; then Romanian deadlifts and hip thrusts to build glutes and hamstrings together. Warm up before anything fast.

Whichever muscle it is, break up sitting every thirty minutes, stop locking your knees when you stand, and add glute bridges or clamshells to your week.

When to come in

Popliteus pain can be stubborn because it is a small stabiliser doing a big job. If two weeks of consistent work has not changed your pain, or the self-checks point in more than one direction, an assessment saves time. Muscles behind this pain

  • Popliteus: Back of the knee; worse crouching, walking downhill or running.
  • Gastrocnemius: Upper calf, back of the knee and the arch of the foot; deep cramp-like tightness.
  • Biceps femoris: Back of the knee on the outside and up the back of the thigh.
  • Semitendinosus & semimembranosus: The buttock crease, back of the thigh and the inner back of the knee, sometimes the inner calf.
  • Gluteus maximus: Buttock and the tailbone; aches with long sitting and walking uphill.

Red flags: see a physician, not a stretch

  • Calf or behind-the-knee pain with swelling, warmth or redness, especially after travel, surgery or bed rest. This can be a blood clot; seek urgent medical care the same day.
  • A pop at the time of injury with immediate, significant swelling, or a feeling of being shot in the back of the leg with inability to walk normally.
  • A knee that is locked and will not bend or straighten, or that gives way under you.
  • Inability to put weight on the leg.
  • Numbness, pins and needles or a foot that drops or slaps when you walk.

Frequently asked questions

What does popliteus pain feel like?

A sharp, pinpoint pain in the centre of the hollow behind the knee, worse walking or running downhill, squatting low, or first bending a fully straight knee.

Can tight calves cause pain behind the knee?

Yes. The gastrocnemius starts just above the knee, and when it is tight or holding trigger points it commonly refers a deep ache up into the back of the knee and down to the arch of the foot.

Is pain behind the knee serious?

Most of the time it is muscular and responds well to release and strengthening. Swelling, warmth, redness, a locked knee or inability to bear weight are the exceptions and need a clinician promptly.
