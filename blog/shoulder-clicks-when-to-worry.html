Shoulder clicks: when to worry and when not to

By Dr. Jared Caban, MD, MSc, CEP · Published 2026-10-20

Why shoulders click

The shoulder is the loosest joint in the body. The ball of the humerus is bigger than the socket, which is why we can reach overhead but also why the shoulder can slip, pinch and click. Most clicks are harmless movements of a tendon over a bony edge.

The three questions

Question one: Does the click hurt? If you can click your shoulder on purpose and feel nothing, that is almost always fine. Painful clicks are the ones that need attention. Question two: Is it getting louder, more frequent, or weaker? A click that is stable for years is less concerning than a new click that is getting worse week to week. Question three: Can you still do what you need to? A click that doesn't limit lifting, reaching or sleep is usually safe to leave alone.

Clicks that need a professional

Pain with the click, especially in a specific position overhead. A feeling of catching or locking. Weakness, like dropping a glass or trouble reaching behind your back. Pain at night that wakes you up. New clicks after a fall or accident.

What a clinic visit looks like

At MD Fit, a shoulder evaluation checks shoulder-blade control, rotator cuff strength, neck involvement and how your shoulder tracks when you lift. Most clicky shoulders turn out to be scapular control problems, where the shoulder blade isn't moving smoothly with the arm. The fix is simple strengthening and some targeted mobility, usually 6–12 weeks.

When it is an actual tear

Some clicks come from labral tears or rotator cuff tears. Not all of these need surgery. Many can be managed well with rehab, especially in middle-aged and older adults where tendon changes are common. The evaluation tells you which bucket your shoulder is in.
